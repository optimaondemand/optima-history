📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When the king is gone, who do you obey?
In 1808, a conqueror from France took the Spanish throne from its king. News of it crossed the ocean to New Spain. Suddenly the people of Mexico had to answer a question nobody had expected: whom should they obey? This lesson follows that question from the cry of a priest in 1810 to the end of Spanish rule in 1821, including a terrible war in Texas.

📚 PART 1 · 1808: A KING WITHOUT A THRONE

Napoleon takes Spain

In 1808, the French emperor Napoleon sent his army into Spain. He forced the Spanish king to give up the throne and put his own brother in the king's place. Many Spaniards refused to accept this. They rose up against the French, and local councils began to rule in the name of the king they considered legitimate.

Why did this matter so much? In the Spanish empire, the king was the link that held everything together. Every official, every court, and every church officer served in his name. When the king was gone, the whole chain of command was in doubt. Nobody had planned for this. Nobody had a rule for what to do when a foreign army took the king prisoner.

In New Spain, the question was how to respond. Should the colony obey the French king? Should it obey the Spanish councils? Or should it govern itself until the real king returned? The viceroy supported a plan for a council of local leaders. But Spaniards in Mexico City feared losing power, and in September 1808, they overthrew him in a coup. The criollos saw that their voices would not count, even in an emergency.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
criollo (kree-OH-yoh). A person of Spanish descent who was born in the Americas, not in Spain.

Think about how these choices looked to different people. A Spanish official in Mexico City wanted to keep things as they were, because he held his job from the old government. A  landowner wanted a voice, because he felt he had earned one. A poor farmer wanted relief from debts and taxes. Each person asked who would help him, and each got a different answer.

📚 PART 2 · THE CRY OF DOLORES, 1810

A priest rings a bell

On September 16, 1810, a priest named Hidalgo rang the church bell in the town of Dolores and called his people to rise up. His speech is known as the Cry of Dolores (Grito de Dolores). Thousands of poor farmers and workers joined him. They captured towns and marched toward Mexico City.

Hidalgo was a parish priest, and he knew his people well. He had started projects in his town to help the poor earn a living. The crowd that followed him was made of many people who were poor and angry. They did not have training or good weapons. But they had numbers, and for a short time that was enough to frighten the Spanish government.

The movement was not tidy. Its members were angry at Spanish rule, at poverty, and at the unfair treatment of Native and mixed-race people. Hidalgo turned back before reaching Mexico City. In 1811, he was captured and executed. Others carried on the fight, but for years the royalist armies held the advantage.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Cry of Dolores (Grito de Dolores) (GREE-toh deh doh-LOH-res). The speech Hidalgo gave on September 16, 1810, in the town of Dolores. It started the revolt.
Hidalgo (ee-DAHL-goh). Miguel Hidalgo, a priest who began the Mexican independence movement in 1810.

The fight was not only a fight against Spain. It was also a fight among Mexicans. Some  joined Hidalgo at first, but turned away when his followers attacked their property. Others fought for the king, because they feared the poor more than they feared Spain. The war split families and towns, and it made peace harder to reach.

📚 PART 3 · TEXAS AT WAR, 1812 TO 1813

A bloody battle on the Medina

The fighting reached Texas in 1812. A Mexican rebel named Bernardo Gutiérrez joined with an American officer named Augustus Magee. Together they led a force of volunteers, many of them Americans from Louisiana, across the Sabine River. They took Nacogdoches, then La Bahía, then San Antonio. In 1813, they declared Texas independent as part of a republic.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
La Bahía (lah bah-EE-ah). A Spanish fort and mission in South Texas. Today it is in the town of Goliad.
Nacogdoches (nak-uh-DOH-chiz). A town in East Texas, named for a Native people who lived nearby.

Notice that the army was called the Republican Army of the North. It was a mix of Mexican rebels and American volunteers. Gutiérrez wanted a free Mexico, and many of the Americans wanted adventure, land, or both. When Magee died, the army lost the leader who had held it together, and its members began to quarrel about what they were fighting for.

Spain sent a strong army under General Arredondo. On August 18, 1813, the two sides met near the Medina River south of San Antonio. The rebel army was crushed. Historians often call it the bloodiest battle ever fought on Texas soil. Arredondo's soldiers then marched into San Antonio and punished the people who had supported the rebellion.

Arredondo had trained his men well, and he had more soldiers and better supplies than the rebels. The royalists were disciplined, and the rebel army was divided. Even so, a Spanish victory was not certain until the fighting began. The rebels had won earlier battles, and they had taken the capital of the province.

The province was wrecked. Families fled to Louisiana. Ranches were abandoned, and the few settlers who stayed were poor and frightened. Texas entered the 1820s even weaker than before.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Arredondo (ah-reh-DOHN-doh). Joaquín de Arredondo, the Spanish general who crushed the rebels in Texas.
Medina (meh-DEE-nah). A river south of San Antonio. The battle fought there in 1813 was one of the bloodiest in Texas history.

The aftermath was cruel. Arredondo's men hunted down rebels, and many people were executed or imprisoned. Property was taken. Families who had supported the rebellion were punished, and some women were held and forced to work. A province that had so few people lost many of them in a single summer. Texas needed new settlers more than ever, and few wanted to come.

📚 PART 4 · 1821: INDEPENDENCE

Spain loses Mexico

The independence war lasted more than a decade. In 1820, a revolt in Spain forced the king to accept a constitution, and conservative leaders in New Spain worried about the new rules. Iturbide, an army officer who had fought the rebels, changed sides. He joined with a rebel leader and issued a plan that promised independence, the Catholic faith, and equal rights for Spaniards and Mexicans.

It is worth asking why Iturbide's plan worked when Hidalgo's did not. Iturbide offered something to nearly everyone. The Church kept its place,  gained power, and Spaniards in Mexico were promised safety. Hidalgo had asked for much more change, much faster. Iturbide's plan was less a revolution than a deal, and many leaders were willing to sign it.

It worked. In September 1821, the armies entered Mexico City, and Mexico became independent. The new country inherited an enormous territory, a damaged economy, and a far northern province with three small towns and no real control. Texas had a new government and an old problem.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Iturbide (ee-toor-BEE-deh). Agustín de Iturbide, a Spanish army officer who led the final push for Mexican independence.

Notice what the new country did not have. It had little experience running a country on its own, no strong national government, and no shared sense of who its leaders should be. A war had destroyed trust between many groups, and the army had become the most powerful body in the land. Mexico was free, but it was also unsettled, and that would shape every decision about Texas that followed.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Napoleon's move in Spain set off a chain of events that ended three centuries of Spanish rule. It left Mexico free but poor, and it left Texas empty and scarred. The new nation had to decide what to do with its distant north.
