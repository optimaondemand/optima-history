📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Every time the nation added new land, the same question came back: would slavery spread there?
By 1860, the North and the South had been pulling apart for a generation. You will trace how a quarrel over taxes became a quarrel over slavery, and how that quarrel finally reached Texas.

📚 PART 1 · NULLIFICATION

Calhoun and the Union as a Compact

In 1828, Congress passed a tariff that made imported goods cost more. It helped Northern factories. Many Southerners sold cotton and bought manufactured goods, so they felt it hurt them. John C. Calhoun of South Carolina, then the vice president, wrote a paper called the South Carolina Exposition and Protest.

Calhoun argued that the states had made the Union, so a state could nullify a national law it believed broke the Constitution. This is called nullification. It is one form of states' rights.

Calhoun’s idea rested on a picture. In his picture, the Union was like an agreement among separate states. If the national government broke the agreement, a state could say so and refuse to obey. Others saw it differently. They said the United States was one nation, and that no single state could overrule the whole country.

In 1832, South Carolina tried it. President Andrew Jackson said no state could do that, and he got ready to use troops. A lower tariff ended the crisis. But Calhoun’s idea did not die. Later, defenders of slavery used it too. By the 1830s, Calhoun himself was arguing that slavery was a good thing for the country, and not just a hard fact to be put up with. Many Northerners, and some Southerners, rejected that claim. The argument over it never stopped.

📚 PART 2 · SLAVERY AND NEW LAND

New Land, Same Question

The Constitution did not say whether slavery could spread into land the nation gained later. So each time the country grew, the argument came back. In 1820, the Missouri Compromise tried to settle it by drawing a line across the western lands. North of the line, slavery would be banned. South of it, slavery would be allowed. When Texas joined the Union in 1845, it added a large new slave state. After the war with Mexico, the nation gained huge western lands, and the quarrel grew hotter. In 1846, Congressman David Wilmot asked Congress to ban slavery in any land won from Mexico. The House passed the idea, but the Senate did not. The fight went on for years.

Slavery mattered to Texas for a plain reason. In East Texas and along the rivers, farmers grew cotton on large farms. Enslaved people did the work. During the 1850s, the number of enslaved people in Texas grew very fast, as cotton farming spread west. A large part of the state’s wealth was tied to slavery. That is why Texans watched the national fight so closely.

Congress tried again and again to find a middle road. The Compromise of 1850 included a harsher Fugitive Slave Act. It made even people in Northern states help catch those who had escaped slavery. In 1854, the Kansas-Nebraska Act let settlers vote on slavery, and fighting broke out in Kansas. Settlers from both sides rushed in, and the violence became so bad that people called it “Bleeding Kansas.” The Republican Party was formed that same year to stop slavery from spreading into new land. Sam Houston, one of Texas’s senators, was one of only two Southern senators to vote against that law.

In 1857, the Supreme Court ruled in the Dred Scott case that Congress could not ban slavery in the territories. It also said that Black Americans could not be citizens. Many Northerners were furious, and many Southerners felt safer.

📚 PART 3 · THE CRISIS ARRIVES

John Brown at Harpers Ferry

John Brown was a white abolitionist who believed slavery could be ended only by force. In October 1859, he led a small band to seize a weapons arsenal at Harpers Ferry, Virginia. He hoped to start a revolt of enslaved people. Brown had already taken part in deadly violence in Kansas, where in 1856 he and his sons killed five men who supported slavery. At Harpers Ferry, though, his plan failed. Soldiers and Marines under Colonel Robert E. Lee captured him in two days. Brown was hanged in December.

In the North, many people honored him. Many Southerners were horrified. They feared that Northerners wanted slave revolts and would excuse them.

The Fear in Texas

Texas felt that fear sharply. Look at how many people were enslaved there.

Not every Texan wanted to leave the Union over it. In 1859, Sam Houston won the race for governor by speaking for the Union. But the fear of revolts ran deep, and it gave the other side a strong argument.

In 1860, about 3 of every 10 people in Texas were enslaved (the dark squares).

In the hot, dry summer of 1860, fires burned in several North Texas towns. Many white Texans blamed a plot by abolitionists and enslaved people. No plot was ever proved. Still, mobs punished and killed people on rumor.

Lincoln Is Elected

In November 1860, Abraham Lincoln was elected president. His name was not even on the ballot in Texas. Texans voted by a wide margin for John C. Breckinridge, the candidate Southern Democrats backed. Lincoln said he would not touch slavery where it already existed. But he and his party opposed letting it spread into new land. Many Southern leaders believed that would one day doom slavery everywhere. They decided they could no longer trust the Union.

The vote itself was split four ways. The Democratic Party had broken in two over slavery, and a fourth candidate ran as well. Lincoln won with less than 40 of every 100 votes. But the Northern states had more people, and so more electoral votes. That was enough to make him president.

South Carolina voted to secede in December 1860, and other Southern states soon followed. In Texas, leaders began calling for a convention to decide.
