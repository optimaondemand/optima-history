📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can a people become the most important power in a region without ever building an empire?

When the Bronze Age Collapse this course studied in Week 9 tore through the Near East, most great powers on the map either fell or came out badly hurt. One place did not. A narrow strip of coastline in the Levant, home to a handful of small port cities, made it through mostly unharmed. Over the next few centuries, those cities did something remarkable. Without conquering anyone, they quietly became the busiest traders in the whole Mediterranean world. In other words, while bigger powers were still picking up the pieces, these small cities were already back in business.

📚 CITIES, NOT AN EMPIRE

Phoeniciafuh-NEE-shuha narrow strip of coastline in the modern Levant, home to a string of independent trading cities. was never one single kingdom, the way Egypt or Assyria was. It was a group of independent city-states along the Levant coast. Each city had its own king, its own harbor, and its own fleet. They worked together when it helped them. They competed when it did not. This pattern should sound familiar. It is close to what this course first saw with Sumerian cities like Uruk back in Week 2: separate centers held together more by shared culture and trade than by any single crown. In other words, no one king ever ruled all of Phoenicia at once. Phoenicia's cities had little land and no great army. What they lacked in territory, they made up for in ships.

Phoenician sailors became the finest navigators and shipbuilders anywhere on the Mediterranean Sea. Using tall, prized stands of Lebanese cedar, they built strong ships that could cross open water other peoples avoided. Phoenician traders carried glassware and fine metalwork. Above all, they carried Tyrian purpleTEER-ee-una costly purple dye Phoenicians made from crushed sea snails., a costly dye pulled drop by drop from the crushed glands of a small sea snail. This dye cost so much that its color became a mark of royalty for centuries afterward, all the way into the Roman Empire. Phoenician traders needed tin to make bronze, but had none of their own. So they pushed steadily west, planting trading posts and coloniesKOL-uh-neea settlement founded by people from another land, still linked back to its home city by trade and culture. along the North African coast, in Sicily and Sardinia, and as far as the Strait of Gibraltar and the Iberian coast beyond it. This whole far-reaching system of ships, ports, and trade goods is what this course means by maritime commerceMAIR-ih-time KOM-ursstrade carried across the sea by ships..

📚 A DIFFERENT KIND OF GIFT

Phoenicia's most lasting gift to the wider world was not a good at all. It was a tool. Egyptian hieroglyphics and Mesopotamian cuneiform, both already met earlier in this course, each needed scribes to master hundreds of different signs. The alphabetAL-fuh-beta writing system where each symbol stands for one sound, not a whole word or idea. the Phoenicians built and spread used only about twenty-two simple symbols. Each one stood for a single consonant sound. In other words, it was simply easier to learn. A trading network stretched across the whole Mediterranean was exactly the kind of place an easier writing system could spread fast. It moved port to port, carried by merchants, not just kept inside temple and palace walls. Within a few centuries, Greek traders picked up this same alphabet. They adapted it further, adding their own vowel signs, a step this course studies directly in Week 16. That adapted Greek alphabet is, eventually, the distant ancestor of the one used to write this very sentence. In other words, a system built for busy merchants counting cargo ended up shaping how nearly the whole Western world reads and writes today.

📚 VALUABLE ENOUGH TO SPARE

Phoenicia's story took a harder turn in the sixth century BC. This course already met Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon in Week 13, builder of the Ishtar Gate and destroyer of Jerusalem. Starting around 585 BC, Nebuchadnezzar laid siege to Tyrethe wealthiest of the Phoenician port cities, built partly on an island just off the coast. itself, the richest of the Phoenician cities. Tyre was built partly on an island and guarded by its own strong navy. The siege dragged on for roughly thirteen years, an amazing length of time by any ancient measure. Yet Tyre was never actually destroyed the way Jerusalem was. Surviving accounts say the siege ended in a deal. Tyre accepted Babylonian overlordship and likely paid tribute, but kept its own king, its own walls, and its own fleet mostly whole. In other words, Nebuchadnezzar's army camped outside Tyre's walls for thirteen full years and still could not force the city to surrender outright.

This is worth pausing on, using the same question this course has asked about Assyria's terror and Babylon's grandeur: what actually holds a state together? Phoenicia's cities never built anything close to the war machine this course studied in Assyria back in Week 11. They never tried to hold land the way Egypt or Babylon did either. What kept Tyre standing through a thirteen-year siege was not strength of arms. It was being too valuable to destroy. Phoenicia controlled Mediterranean shipping, shipbuilding know-how, and a trade network no conquering power wanted to burn down along with the city. In other words, a city can sometimes protect itself just by being too useful to lose. A civilization can secure its own survival through being needed just as surely as through walls and armies, and sometimes more surely still. Compare this directly with Assyria's approach in Weeks 11 and 12. Assyria ruled by fear and lost everything within a single generation once its army faltered. Phoenicia never had a strong army to begin with, and yet it outlasted Assyria anyway.
