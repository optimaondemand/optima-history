📚 A KINGDOM STANDING ALONE

By the seventh century BC, the northern kingdom of Israel no longer existed as its own state at all. Assyria had destroyed it in 722 BC. Assyria's kings also deported much of its people, a policy this course has already seen used elsewhere to stop conquered peoples from planning future revolts. That left only the smaller southern kingdom, Judah, ruled from Jerusalem. For over a century, Judah worked hard just to survive between Egyptian and Mesopotamian power.

Judah's independence finally ended under Nebuchadnezzar II. This week's first Primary Source, drawn from a Babylonian chronicleKRON-ih-kula written record of events, usually listed year by year in plain, factual order., records his forces capturing Jerusalem in 597 BC. They carried off its king, a young ruler named Jehoiachin. Much of its elite population went with him to Babylon. Cuneiform ration tablets found in Babylon actually name Jehoiachin directly. These tablets list food handed out to royal prisoners there. This is a rare case where an outside, non-biblical record backs up a specific detail from this story. In other words, we do not have to take this story only on faith. A real object backs it up.

Nebuchadnezzar placed a new king, Zedekiah, on Judah's throne in Jehoiachin's place, expecting loyalty in return. A later Judean revolt tested Nebuchadnezzar's patience past its limit. His response was final and harsh. Zedekiah was captured. He was forced to watch his own sons killed. Then he was blinded, and carried off to Babylon himself. In 586 BC, Jerusalem itself was destroyed, along with its Temple, the central site of Israelite worship. A further wave of its people was deported to Babylon, beginning the period Jewish tradition remembers as the Babylonian exilebeing forced to leave your home country, often for a long time or forever, usually by a conquering power..

📚 A BELIEF WORTH UNDERSTANDING ON ITS OWN TERMS

This course has studied several religious systems so far. Sumerian, Egyptian, and Mesopotamian religion more broadly each recognized many gods with different jobs. These gods were often tied closely to nature, kingship, or a specific city. Israelite religion, as shown in its own surviving texts, worked differently. It centered on monotheismMON-oh-thee-iz-umbelief in and worship of only one god., devotion to a single god, and on a covenantKUV-uh-nunta binding, two-way agreement, often between a god and a people, with promised duties on both sides.. A covenant was a binding, mutual agreement, just between that god and the Israelite people. Under its terms, loyalty and obedience to a shared law were understood to carry real weight. Good or bad, that weight would show up in the community's own fortunes across history. In other words, Israel's own story was tied to how well its people kept their promise to their god.

Prophets living through this very crisis wrote and preached in exactly these terms. Jeremiah was still in Jerusalem as the city fell. He warned that the coming disaster was a direct result of the people breaking their own covenant. Ezekiel had already been sent to Babylon, years earlier. He wrote visions trying to make sense of that same disaster from exile itself. Reading their words is one clear way to see this covenant idea used in real time. Real people living through these very events used it. It was not some distant historian summing things up long after the fact. In other words, we are not just reading a summary written after the fact. We are reading words from people living through it.

🎯 TRY IT: TWO KINDS OF BELIEF
Sort each feature by the religious system it describes.
Many gods, each often tied to a particular city, natural force, or aspect of kingship.
One god, bound to a single people through a mutual agreement carrying real historical consequences.

This is a truly different kind of belief. It is not simply the same kind of belief as its neighbors, with the names changed. It deserves to be taken seriously as its own thing. It should not be flattened into some version of Mesopotamian or Egyptian religion. At the same time, this course does not claim to settle any question of religious truth, one way or another. Here is what can be said with confidence, as a matter of history rather than belief itself: Israelite writers often explained their kingdom's own defeats, including this one, as tied directly to this covenant. That framework is worth understanding honestly on its own terms. It should not get turned into some other civilization's own terms instead.

📚 AN ENDING THAT WAS NOT, IN FACT, AN ENDING

Exile is a form of forced migration. It might reasonably have meant the end of Judah's people and beliefs for good. They could have simply melted into the wider Babylonian population, the way conquered peoples sometimes did vanish from the record entirely. That is not what happened here. Religious and cultural identity survived through decades of exile in Babylon itself. It stayed alive without a king, a temple, or even a homeland to anchor it. This is a truly rare outcome, worth noting rather than assumed. In other words, losing a homeland did not mean losing who they were.

Its return, when it came, arrived from an unexpected direction. In 538 BC, a new conqueror had just taken Babylon itself. He issued a decree. That decree let Judean exiles return home and rebuild their Temple. This was a policy very different from anything this course has studied from Assyria or Babylon. That conqueror's name was Cyrus. His own remarkable rise, and his very different way of holding an empire together, is where this course turns in full in Week 17. In other words, the empire that conquered Babylon did not treat Judah's exiles the way Babylon had.
