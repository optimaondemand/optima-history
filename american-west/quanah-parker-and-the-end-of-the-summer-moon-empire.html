📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1875, the last free band of the strongest people on the southern Plains rode in and gave up. It had taken years of war, and the loss of the bison, to make it happen.
The Comanche held the southern Plains for more than a century. Their end did not come in one battle. You will see how the Civil War gave the Comanche a new chance, how the Army ground them down in the Red River War, and how one man, Quanah Parker, led his people through the surrender and beyond it.

📚 PART 1 · A TIME OF DANGER

A Chance for the Comanche

When Texas left the Union in 1861, the U.S. Army pulled out of its forts on the frontier. Texas tried to fill the gap with its own soldiers, but most of its fighting men went east to the war. The settlers who remained had little protection. In many places, they pulled back toward the east. In 1864, Comanche and Kiowa raiders struck Elm Creek in North Texas, killed settlers, and took captives. The Comanche had a few years of strength they had not seen in a decade.

After the war, the army came back. In 1867, at Medicine Lodge Creek in Kansas, leaders of the Comanche and several other peoples signed a treaty. It promised them a reservation in what is now Oklahoma, with food and goods from the government. Many bands agreed. But the Quahadi, who lived on the high, dry plains of the Llano Estacado, did not. They had never signed, and they did not intend to live on a reservation.

Meanwhile, some Comanche and Kiowa raiders used the reservation as a safe place to rest. They raided in Texas, then rode back north to the safety of Fort Sill. In May 1871, near Jacksboro, a Kiowa war party attacked a wagon train and killed seven teamsters. General William T. Sherman, the army’s top general, happened to be passing through and was shocked. It was one more sign that the old policy was failing.

📚 PART 2 · THE LAST WAR

The Bison and the Red River War

A bigger change was coming from the Plains itself. The Comanche depended on the bison. In the 1870s, hunters from the East came by the thousands. Railroads made it easy to ship hides. A single hunter with a heavy rifle could kill many animals in a day. By the middle of the decade, the great southern herds were nearly gone. The army was glad. Generals knew that without bison, the Plains peoples could not live as they had.

In June 1874, the Quahadi leader Isa-tai promised that his medicine would protect warriors from bullets. A young Quahadi war leader, Quanah Parker, joined the attack on a camp of hunters at a trading post called Adobe Walls, in the Texas Panhandle. The hunters had powerful rifles and thick walls. The attack failed. After it, the army and the Plains peoples were headed for open war.

The army launched the Red River War that summer. Soldiers marched from several directions, so that no band could be safe. Colonel Ranald Mackenzie led the 4th cavalry against a large village hidden in Palo Duro Canyon. At dawn on September 28, 1874, his troopers came down the canyon wall and caught the camp by surprise. Few Comanche and Kiowa were killed. But the soldiers burned the lodges and winter food, and captured about 1,400 horses. Mackenzie had his men shoot most of them. Without horses, a Plains people could not hunt or move.

Black soldiers took part in these campaigns too. The Buffalo Soldiers of the 10th Cavalry were stationed at forts in West Texas such as Fort Concho and Fort Davis, and they marched and fought on the Plains. The Texas Rangers also played a part. A new group of Rangers, the Frontier Battalion, was created in 1874 and fought several skirmishes. But in this war, the main force against the Comanche was the U.S. Army.

📚 PART 3 · QUANAH PARKER

Quanah Parker

By the spring of 1875, the bands that were still free were hungry and tired. The Quahadi held out longest. Their leader was Quanah Parker. His mother was Cynthia Ann Parker, a Texan taken captive as a child. His father was Peta Nocona, a Comanche chief. Quanah grew up as a Comanche and became a respected war leader.

In June 1875, he led his band, about four hundred people, to Fort Sill and gave up. The age of the free Comanche on the southern Plains was over. Historians point out that the Comanche had not been beaten by one battle. They had been worn down by lost horses, lost bison, hunger, disease, and a government that could keep sending soldiers.

What happened next shows another side of Quanah. He did not retreat from the world. He learned English and studied the white economy. He leased grazing land on the reservation to Texas cattlemen, and he became a rancher himself. He built a large house, which people called the Star House. The government named him a judge on the reservation court, and he argued for his people in Washington. In 1905, he rode in President Theodore Roosevelt’s inaugural parade.

Quanah led a people who had lost everything they once had. He answered for them when it was hard, and he kept finding ways for them to live. He died in 1911, and he was buried near Fort Sill, beside his mother.

Historians still ask whether the Comanche might have found another path, with different treaties or a government that kept its word. It is hard to know. What we do know is what the end of the Comanche cost, and who paid it. The Texans who settled the plains won what they had long sought. The Comanche paid for it with their homeland and their way of life.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

An empire that lasted a hundred years ended because horses, bison, and railroads all changed at once. When you read about the end of a way of life, ask what it cost, who paid, and who is left to answer for the people who lost.
