📚 PART 1 · AFTER THE BRACEROS

After the Braceros

During World War II, the United States brought in Mexican farm workers under a program that lasted until 1964. After the war, many farmers in Texas and other states wanted the workers to keep coming. Some workers also stayed or crossed the border without papers. In 1954, the federal government launched a drive to find and send home people who were in the country without permission. It was called Operation Wetback, a name now considered insulting. Hundreds of thousands of people were deported, and some of them were United States citizens.

When the Bracero Program ended in 1964, farm workers still wanted jobs, and farmers still wanted workers. But there was no longer a legal way for many of them to come. In 1965, Congress changed the immigration laws and ended the old system that favored people from northern Europe. It also, for the first time, put a limit on immigration from the Western Hemisphere. Many people continued to cross the border to find work, and the number of immigrants without legal papers rose.

In 1986, Congress passed a law that gave legal status to millions of people who had lived in the country for years, and it also punished employers who hired workers without papers. Texas had many of these workers. Debates over immigration, the border, and the rights of newcomers have stayed a major part of Texas life ever since. People still disagree about how to balance order, fairness, and the need for workers.

📚 PART 2 · TRADE ACROSS THE LINE

Factories and Free Trade

As the Bracero Program ended, Mexico tried a new plan. In 1965, it began to let foreign companies set up factories along its northern border, with special tax breaks. These factories, called maquiladoras, assembled goods such as clothes, electronics, and car parts. They used parts brought in from the United States and sent the finished goods back across the border. Cities such as Ciudad Juárez, across from El Paso, and Matamoros, across from Brownsville, grew fast as workers moved there for jobs.

💡 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
colonia (koh-LOH-nee-uh): A small, often poor neighborhood or settlement near the border, sometimes lacking basic services such as paved roads or running water.
maquiladora (mah-kee-lah-DOR-uh): A factory in Mexico, often near the U.S. border, that assembles goods for export, usually using parts brought in from other countries.

In 1994, the United States, Canada, and Mexico put into effect the North American Free Trade Agreement, called NAFTA. It removed most tariffs on goods moving among the three countries. Supporters said that it would lower prices, create jobs, and bring the countries closer. Critics said that some factory jobs in the United States would move to Mexico, and that workers would be paid too little. Both sides found evidence for their views. Trade among the three countries grew sharply.

Texas stood in the middle of this trade. Laredo became the busiest inland port in the United States, with thousands of trucks crossing each day. El Paso, Brownsville, and Eagle Pass grew as well. The border region became joined across the line. Families lived on one side and worked on the other. Companies on both sides depended on each other. This follows a basic idea in economics: each region does what it does best, and both can gain by trading. The border is a place where that idea works in real life, with all its benefits and problems.

Growth did not reach everyone. Along the border, thousands of families lived in colonias, settlements built by poor residents on cheap land that often lacked paved roads, clean water, and sewers. The state and the national government have worked to improve them, but many have lasted for decades. The border is a region of great energy and great need at once.

📚 PART 3 · A CHANGING STATE

A Changing Texas

Texas grew fast in these years. In 1980, it had about 14 million people. By 2000, it had nearly 21 million, and by 2020 it had about 29 million. Many newcomers came from other U.S. states, drawn by jobs. Many others came from abroad. Mexico sent the largest number of people, but also many came from Central America, Asia, and Africa. After the end of the Vietnam War in 1975, thousands of Vietnamese refugees settled on the Gulf Coast, where many worked in the fishing and shrimping business, and in Houston. By 2020, about four in ten Texans were Hispanic.

Rapid growth put pressure on schools, roads, hospitals, and water. It also changed how Texans lived and voted. In the early 2000s, Texas became a state in which no single racial or ethnic group made up a majority of the population. Newcomers formed new businesses, new churches, and new neighborhoods, and they made the state more varied than before.

Many people kept their heritage alive as they settled. Mexican Americans celebrated their holidays and passed on Tejano music, foods, and traditions. Vietnamese Texans opened markets and held festivals. Indian and Chinese families built temples and cultural centers. These traditions, like earlier German and Czech ones, became part of what it means to be a Texan. At the same time, young Texans of every background grew up speaking English and thinking of themselves as Texans first.

Texas shows a mix of change and continuity. The border still divides two countries, yet trade and families tie them together. Debates about immigration and borders are not new, but they have taken new forms. Where people once crossed to pick crops, many now cross to work in offices and factories. And the border, drawn after a war in 1848, remains a place where two countries meet and shape each other.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A border can both divide people and join them. When you study trade and migration, ask who gains, who is left out, and what the people on both sides want for their families.
