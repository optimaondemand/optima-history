💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
citizen (SIT-ih-zun): A member of a country or state who has rights and also duties.
interest group (IN-ter-est GROOP): A group of people who work together to influence leaders on an issue they care about.
jury (JOOR-ee): A group of citizens chosen to listen to a court case and decide the facts.
lobby (LOB-ee): To try to persuade lawmakers to pass or reject a law.
political party (puh-LIT-ih-kul PAR-tee): A group of people with similar ideas who work together to win elections.
poll tax (POHL TAX): A fee people had to pay before they were allowed to vote.
primary election (PRY-mair-ee ee-LEK-shun): An election held before the main election, where a party chooses its candidates.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A right is something you may do. A duty is something you owe. A free people needs both.
A government is only as good as the people who use it. In this lesson you will see the rights Texans hold, how many Texans were once kept from using them, and why a good citizen does more than wait for a turn to vote.

📚 PART 1 · YOUR RIGHTS

Rights Texans Hold

The Texas Bill of Rights is the first article of the state constitution, which tells you how much the delegates of 1876 cared about it. It guarantees many rights. Three are worth a close look.

The right to vote. Today, a Texan who is a citizen, is at least 18, and has registered ahead of time may vote. Voting is how the people choose who holds the offices of government. In Texas, voters choose more officials than in most places, from the governor to the local judge and the school board.

Jury service. The Constitution guarantees a trial by jury in many cases. A jury is made up of ordinary citizens, picked from among the people of the county. They hear the evidence and decide the facts. Serving on a jury is called a right, but it is also a duty. If the people will not serve, the courts cannot work. A person who receives a jury summons is required to answer it.

Free speech and a free press. The Texas Bill of Rights protects the right to speak, write, and publish on any subject, while leaving people responsible for any misuse of that right. A free people needs to be able to argue about the way it is governed, and to read the facts in print.

📚 PART 2 · THE FIGHT FOR THE BALLOT

Who Was Kept Out

These rights were not open to every Texan. After Reconstruction ended, Texas leaders found ways to narrow who could vote.

In 1902, Texas adopted a poll tax. A person had to pay a fee in order to vote. The fee was small to some people and large to others. Many Black Texans, many Mexican Americans, and many poor white farmers could not easily afford it. Voter turnout dropped sharply.

There was another barrier. In Texas, the Democratic Party was so strong that the real contest was often settled in its primary election, where the party picked its candidate. Texas Democrats ran their primary as a private club, and they kept Black voters out. People called this the white primary. In 1944, Dr. Lonnie Smith, a Black dentist in Houston, challenged it. In Smith v. Allwright, the U.S. Supreme Court ruled that a party could not keep voters out of a primary because of race.

Steps in the fight over who could vote in Texas.

Women had to fight for their right to vote as well. Texas women won the right to vote in primaries in 1918. In 1919, Texas became the first state in the South to approve the 19th Amendment. Later steps came more slowly. In 1966, a federal court struck down the Texas poll tax. In 1971, the voting age was lowered to 18.

None of these rights was handed down. Ordinary people had to organize and take cases to court, often over many years, to make the law match its promise. Those people showed courage and perseverance, and Texas is a better place for it.

📚 PART 3 · TAKING PART

Parties and Interest Groups

Most elections in Texas are contests between political party candidates. A party is a group of people with similar ideas about government, who work together to win office. Through most of the 1900s, the Democratic Party was far the stronger in Texas. Today the two major parties are the Democrats and the Republicans. In a primary election, the voters of each party pick the person who will carry its name in the main election.

An interest group is different. It is not trying to win elections. It is a group that wants to influence what leaders decide about one issue or set of issues. Texas has groups for farmers and ranchers, teachers, doctors, oil and gas companies, builders, and many others. They lobby, meaning they meet with lawmakers to argue for or against bills. Lobbying is protected by the right to petition the government, and it can give lawmakers useful facts. But a lawmaker has to weigh what a group says against what is good for everyone, since a group usually speaks for its own members.

A Duty, Not Just a Right

Think about the difference between a right and a duty. A right protects you from others. A duty is what you owe to others. A citizen has both. A Texan owes obedience to the laws, taxes to pay for government, and service on a jury when called. A Texan also owes something less written down: to stay informed, to vote, and to take part in the life of the community.

The old thinkers who inspired the Constitution believed that a free government depends on the habits of its people. If citizens give up on government and leave it to a few, the few will run it. If citizens take part, government stays closer to the people. Many Texans in history gave years of their lives to win a right that you will be able to use. What you do with it is up to you.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A right unused is a right in danger. As you read about Texans who held office, ask what they did with the rights they had, and who had to win those rights first.
