💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
artificial intelligence (ar-tuh-FISH-ul in-TEL-uh-junss): Computer programs that try to do things that usually take human thinking, such as understanding language or solving problems.
consortium (kun-SOR-shum): A group of companies that join together to work toward a shared goal.
integrated circuit (IN-tuh-gray-tid SUR-kit): A tiny electronic circuit with many parts built onto one small piece of material. Computer chips are integrated circuits.
semantic web (suh-MAN-tik web): The idea that web pages should carry labels a computer can understand, so that it can sort and connect information instead of only showing words.
semiconductor (sem-ee-kun-DUK-ter): A material used to make computer chips, which control electric current in electronic devices.
talent pipeline (TAL-unt PYP-lyn): A steady flow of trained people, such as graduates, who are ready to fill skilled jobs.
technology (tek-NOL-uh-jee): The tools, machines, and methods people use to solve problems.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1984, a freshman at the University of Texas started selling computers out of his dorm room. Within a few years he was running a company.
For most of its history, Austin was a government and college town. In the late 1900s it became one of the nation’s leading homes of technology. You will see how Texas Instruments began and why its chips and calculators mattered, how a few early plants grew into a tech industry, what a company called MCC did, how Michael Dell made computers cheaper, and why the University of Texas mattered.

📚 PART 1 · A QUIET CAPITAL

A Capital and a College

Austin became the capital of the Republic of Texas in 1839. For most of its history, it lived on two things, state government and the University of Texas, which opened in 1883. It had little heavy industry. It was smaller than Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio. People thought of it as a pleasant, quiet place with hills, lakes, and a lot of lawmakers.

That began to change in the 1960s. Texas Instruments, the Dallas firm that began as a company for finding oil underground, opened a plant in Austin that made semiconductors. These are the tiny pieces at the heart of computer chips. IBM built a large facility in the city in 1967, and Motorola opened one in the 1970s. Each firm hired engineers and technicians, and some of them left to start their own small companies. A pool of skilled workers began to form.

Austin offered some advantages. It had a major university that trained engineers and scientists. It had a mild feel and a lively culture that attracted young people. The cost of land and living was lower than in California. And Texas did not tax personal income. Business leaders in Austin, including the chamber of commerce, worked to recruit companies.

Texas Instruments: From Oil Maps to Calculators

Texas Instruments did not begin as an electronics company. In 1930, two men, J. Clarence Karcher and Eugene McDermott, started a Dallas firm called Geophysical Service. Its job was to help oil companies find oil. Crews set off small blasts and listened to how the sound waves bounced back from layers of rock deep underground. The echoes made a kind of map that showed where oil might be trapped. In 1951 the company took the name Texas Instruments and moved more and more into electronics.

A tiny part called the transistor gave the company its big chance. A transistor controls the flow of electricity, and it replaced the large, hot glass tubes used in older radios and computers. In 1954 Texas Instruments began selling transistors made of silicon, a material that held up better in heat than earlier ones. Four years later, in 1958, a TI engineer named Jack Kilby built the first working integrated circuit in the company’s Dallas lab. Instead of wiring separate parts together, he put several parts on one small piece of material. Robert Noyce, who worked for another company, soon came up with a different version, and the two men are both remembered as inventors of the chip. Later chips held thousands of parts, and then millions. Kilby won the Nobel Prize in Physics for his work in 2000.

The company also wanted to put its chips into everyday things. In 1965, TI’s leader, Patrick Haggerty, asked Kilby to design a calculator small enough to carry. Calculators of that time were heavy machines that sat on desks. Kilby’s team built a prototype by 1967, and in 1972 TI sold the Datamath, a handheld calculator that ordinary people could buy. Because TI made its own chips, it could keep lowering the price, and models such as the TI-30 became favorites of students through the 1970s.

The next step came in 1990, when TI released the TI-81, a graphing calculator that could draw the picture of an equation on a small screen. Later models, such as the TI-83 and the TI-84, followed. Math teachers built lessons around these machines, and many asked students to buy one. By the 2000s, TI was still the leading calculator maker for American schools. A Dallas oil-mapping firm had become a name that millions of students knew from their backpacks.

Texas Instruments also helped make Austin. Its Austin plant was one of the first chip plants in the city, and the engineers and technicians it hired became part of Austin’s early pool of skilled workers.

📚 PART 2 · MCC AND SILICON HILLS

The MCC and Silicon Hills

The big turning point came in 1983. In 1982, Japan had announced a plan to build a new generation of computers, a “fifth generation” with artificial intelligence. Many American companies worried that Japan would take the lead in computing. In response, several leading firms joined together to form the Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation, or MCC. A consortium like this was unusual, because the companies were competitors who agreed to share research.

Dozens of cities wanted MCC. Austin won, beating other cities such as Atlanta and San Diego. The University of Texas, the governor, and local leaders all joined to make the offer attractive. MCC’s first leader was Admiral Bobby Ray Inman, a retired Navy officer and intelligence chief. MCC brought hundreds of top scientists and engineers to Austin. Its research had mixed success, and it never matched what Japan had planned. But the people it brought stayed, and some started new companies.

Some Texans called the area “Silicon Hills,” a copy of California’s Silicon Valley.

Cyc and a Computer with Common Sense

One MCC project aimed at a very hard problem: teaching a computer common sense. It was led by a scientist named Doug Lenat, and it was called Cyc, which is said like the first part of “encyclopedia.” Computers in the 1980s could do arithmetic in a flash, but they did not know the simple things every child knows, such as that water is wet or that a person cannot be in two places at once. Lenat’s team set out to write down millions of facts like these, along with the rules for using them, so that a computer could reason with them. Cyc never became a product that families bought, but the work went on for decades. In the mid-1990s Lenat started a company, Cycorp, to carry it forward.

A younger researcher, Ramanathan V. Guha, worked on Cyc at MCC in the late 1980s and early 1990s. He then moved on, and his ideas traveled with him. At Apple, he built a system for describing what is inside files and websites, so that a computer could sort them by meaning. He called it the Meta Content Framework. At Netscape, an early web company, he helped turn that idea into a public standard called RDF, the Resource Description Framework, which is a way of attaching labeled facts to things on the web. Also at Netscape, in 1999, Guha and a coworker made the first version of RSS, a feed that let a website announce its new stories to anyone who signed up. At first the letters stood for RDF Site Summary.

Together, these tools fed an idea called the semantic web, in which web pages carry labels that computers can read, not only words that people can read. Guha joined Google in 2005. There he started Google Custom Search, which lets a website or a group of sites build a search box that looks only through chosen pages. He later helped launch Schema.org, a shared set of labels that Google and other search companies use. A line of work that began in an Austin research lab for competing companies had reached the search boxes people used every day.

Michael Dell Starts a Company

Michael Dell arrived at the University of Texas in 1984 as a 19-year-old freshman. His family expected him to become a doctor, but he was more interested in computers. In his dorm room he bought parts and upgraded personal computers, called PCs, for students and local businesses. With about $1,000, he started a company called PC’s Limited. Business grew so fast that he left school to run it full time.

His big idea was to change how computers were sold. In the 1980s, most PCs reached buyers through stores, and each step added to the price. Dell sold straight to customers, first by phone and later over the web. He built each computer after it was ordered, using the parts the buyer chose. That meant fewer unsold machines sitting in warehouses and no store markup. In 1988 the company became Dell Computer Corporation and sold shares to the public. Its headquarters grew up in Round Rock, just north of Austin.

📚 PART 3 · GROWTH AND ITS COSTS

Why It Worked, and What It Cost

The University of Texas at Austin is a big reason. It graduates thousands of engineers and computer scientists each year, and many stay in the area. This steady flow of trained workers is called a talent pipeline. Companies know that if they set up in Austin, they can hire from it. The university also does research that leads to new companies. In turn, the tech companies hire students and give money back to the school.

Dell Computer became the best-known company of the new tech Austin. In 1996 it began taking orders on the web, and online sales grew quickly through the late 1990s. In 2001 Dell became the world’s top seller of computer systems, passing Compaq. Because it sold direct and built to order, Dell could charge less than many rivals, and other PC makers had to cut their own prices to compete. Over those years, a personal computer went from a costly purchase to something a family or a school could afford. In the middle 2000s other makers, such as Hewlett-Packard, passed Dell at times, but it stayed one of the largest PC companies in the world.

Other firms followed. Samsung built a major chip plant in Austin in the 1990s. Apple, Google, which is part of Alphabet, and many others opened large offices.

In recent years, even bigger names have come. In 2020, Oracle, one of the world’s largest software companies, moved its headquarters to Austin. In 2021, Tesla announced it would move its headquarters to the Austin area and build a huge factory there. The city that had once been a quiet capital had become a center of the digital age. From about 345,000 people in 1980, Austin grew to nearly 960,000 by 2020, and it passed one million in 2025.

Elon Musk’s companies have added to the story. Tesla’s Texas factory, called Giga Texas, rose in southeast Austin beside the Colorado River and began building cars in the 2020s, first the Model Y and later the Cybertruck. In 2026, Musk announced plans for Terafab, a large chip factory that his companies hope to build in Texas. It is still only a plan. If it is built, it would put chip making back at the center of Austin’s story, as it was when Texas Instruments first came to town.

SpaceX, Musk’s rocket company, also works in Texas, far from Austin’s offices. On the Gulf Coast near Brownsville, at a site called Starbase, it builds and tests Starship, its giant rocket, and launches it from the coast. Near Waco, at McGregor, SpaceX tests its rocket engines at a large site it has used since the 2000s. These places made Texas a home for space work as well as computers.

Tech also has its own booms and busts, like the oil and cattle businesses before it. In the late 1990s, many Austin companies grew quickly as the internet took off. Investors poured money into new firms, and young workers flooded into the city. In 2000 and 2001, many of those internet companies failed, and thousands of Austin workers lost their jobs. But the University, the skilled workers, and the larger companies remained, and the industry recovered. The lesson was that a city built on a new industry has to be ready for sudden swings.

The story also shows how places change by choices as well as luck. Austin did not have to become a tech city. Leaders made offers, workers took risks, and companies chose to build there. Another city might have won MCC, and another history might have followed. Austin’s path was its own, and it began to set the city apart from the rest of the state.
