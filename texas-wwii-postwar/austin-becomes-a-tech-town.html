📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1984, a freshman at the University of Texas started selling computers out of his dorm room. Within a few years he was running a company.
For most of its history, Austin was a government and college town. In the late 1900s it became one of the nation’s leading homes of technology. You will see how a few early plants grew into a tech industry, what a company called MCC did, and why the University of Texas mattered.

📚 PART 1 · A QUIET CAPITAL

A Capital and a College

Austin became the capital of the Republic of Texas in 1839. For most of its history, it lived on two things, state government and the University of Texas, which opened in 1883. It had little heavy industry. It was smaller than Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio. People thought of it as a pleasant, quiet place with hills, lakes, and a lot of lawmakers.

That began to change in the 1960s. Texas Instruments, the Dallas firm that began as a company for finding oil underground, opened a plant in Austin that made semiconductors. These are the tiny pieces at the heart of computer chips. IBM built a large facility in the city in 1967, and Motorola opened one in the 1970s. Each firm hired engineers and technicians, and some of them left to start their own small companies. A pool of skilled workers began to form.

Austin offered some advantages. It had a major university that trained engineers and scientists. It had a mild feel and a lively culture that attracted young people. The cost of land and living was lower than in California. And Texas did not tax personal income. Business leaders in Austin, including the chamber of commerce, worked to recruit companies.

📚 PART 2 · MCC AND SILICON HILLS

The MCC and Silicon Hills

The big turning point came in 1983. In 1982, Japan had announced a plan to build a new generation of computers, a “fifth generation” with artificial intelligence. Many American companies worried that Japan would take the lead in computing. In response, several leading firms joined together to form the Microelectronics and Computer Technology Corporation, or MCC. A consortium like this was unusual, because the companies were competitors who agreed to share research.

Dozens of cities wanted MCC. Austin won, beating other cities such as Atlanta and San Diego. The University of Texas, the governor, and local leaders all joined to make the offer attractive. MCC’s first leader was Admiral Bobby Ray Inman, a retired Navy officer and intelligence chief. MCC brought hundreds of top scientists and engineers to Austin. Its research had mixed success, and it never matched what Japan had planned. But the people it brought stayed, and some started new companies.

Some Texans called the area “Silicon Hills,” a copy of California’s Silicon Valley. And one young man became the most famous. Michael Dell, a student at the University of Texas, began building and selling computers in 1984. He sold them directly to customers, which kept prices low. His company grew fast, and by the 1990s Dell Computer was one of the biggest in the world. Other firms followed. Samsung built a major chip plant in Austin in the 1990s. Apple, Google, which is part of Alphabet, and many others opened large offices.

📚 PART 3 · GROWTH AND ITS COSTS

Why It Worked, and What It Cost

The University of Texas at Austin is a big reason. It graduates thousands of engineers and computer scientists each year, and many stay in the area. This steady flow of trained workers is called a talent pipeline. Companies know that if they set up in Austin, they can hire from it. The university also does research that leads to new companies. In turn, the tech companies hire students and give money back to the school.

In recent years, even bigger names have come. In 2020, Oracle, one of the world’s largest software companies, moved its headquarters to Austin. In 2021, Tesla announced it would move its headquarters to the Austin area and build a huge factory there. The city that had once been a quiet capital had become a center of the digital age. From about 345,000 people in 1980, Austin grew to nearly 960,000 by 2020.

Tech also has its own booms and busts, like the oil and cattle businesses before it. In the late 1990s, many Austin companies grew quickly as the internet took off. Investors poured money into new firms, and young workers flooded into the city. In 2000 and 2001, many of those internet companies failed, and thousands of Austin workers lost their jobs. But the University, the skilled workers, and the larger companies remained, and the industry recovered. The lesson was that a city built on a new industry has to be ready for sudden swings.

This growth brought costs. Housing prices climbed fast, and many longtime residents were priced out. Traffic grew worse. Newcomers brought different politics and customs, and older Austinites worried about losing what made the city special. The city, and the state, had to decide how much growth they wanted and who should pay for it. Austin shows that success can create its own problems.

The story also shows how places change by choices as well as luck. Austin did not have to become a tech city. Leaders made offers, workers took risks, and companies chose to build there. Another city might have won MCC, and another history might have followed. Austin’s path was its own, and it began to set the city apart from the rest of the state.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A university, a few early plants, and a bold offer to one consortium helped turn a quiet capital into a tech center. When a place changes, ask which choices set it in motion, and who pays for the change.
