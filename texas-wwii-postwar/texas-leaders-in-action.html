💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
filibuster (FIL-ih-bus-ter): A way of blocking a vote by speaking for a very long time.
impeachment (im-PEECH-ment): A formal charge against a public official for serious wrongdoing, brought by the House of Representatives.
keynote address (KEE-noht uh-DRESS): The main speech at the start of a large political meeting.
segregation (seg-rih-GAY-shun): The forced separation of people by race.
Speaker of the House (SPEE-ker uv thuh HOWS): The leader of the U.S. House of Representatives, chosen by its members.

📚 PART 1 · LEADERS FROM TEXAS

Sam Rayburn: Mr. Speaker

Sam Rayburn was born in Tennessee in 1882. His family moved to a cotton farm near Bonham, Texas, when he was a boy. He studied hard, taught school, and was elected to the Texas House of Representatives at age 24. At 29 he became its Speaker. In 1913, Texans sent him to the U.S. House of Representatives. He served there for 48 years, until his death in 1961.

Rayburn rose to be Speaker of the House for a total of 17 years, longer than anyone before him. People called him “Mr. Sam.” He was not a showy speaker. He was known for keeping his word, for working in private, and for knowing how to count votes. In 1936, he helped pass a law to bring electric power to farms, which most power companies had refused to serve. In August 1941, as war loomed, the House voted on extending the draft. It passed by a single vote, 203 to 202, after Rayburn worked to hold his members together. Without it, the army might have fallen apart months before Pearl Harbor.

Two Voices for Civil Rights

Henry B. González was born in San Antonio in 1916 and grew up in a Mexican American family. He served on the San Antonio city council and then in the Texas Senate. In 1957, lawmakers brought bills meant to keep Texas schools segregated. González and another senator spoke against them for about 36 hours, taking turns, in a filibuster to stop a vote. The effort did not stop every bill, but it drew attention to the issue. In 1961, he became the first Mexican American from Texas elected to Congress. He served for 37 years.

Barbara Jordan was born in Houston in 1936. She studied at Texas Southern University and Boston University Law School. In 1966, she won a seat in the Texas Senate, the first Black person elected there since Reconstruction, and the first Black woman ever. In 1972, she became the first Black woman from the South elected to the U.S. House of Representatives. Her voice was deep and her speeches were remarkable. In 1974, a House committee debated the impeachment of President Richard Nixon. Jordan’s speech in that debate, on what the Constitution requires, was broadcast across the country and made her famous.

In 1976, she gave the keynote address at the Democratic National Convention, the first Black woman to do so for a major party. She left Congress in 1979 and taught at the University of Texas. She died in 1996.

📚 PART 2 · MORE LEADERS

Kay Bailey Hutchison: A New Era

Kay Bailey Hutchison was born in Galveston in 1943. She won a seat in the Texas House in 1972 and later served as the state treasurer. In 1993, she won a special election for the U.S. Senate, becoming the first woman to represent Texas there. She served until 2013, working on issues such as transportation and the military. Later she served as the American ambassador to NATO.

Two Texans in the White House

Two presidents are tied to Texas. Dwight D. Eisenhower was born in Denison, Texas, in 1890, though his family moved to Kansas when he was a toddler. He led Allied armies in World War II and was president from 1953 to 1961. Lyndon B. Johnson was born near Stonewall in the Hill Country in 1908. He taught Mexican American children in the town of Cotulla before going into politics, and Sam Rayburn was an early mentor. Johnson became a leader of the Senate, vice president, and then president after John F. Kennedy was killed in 1963. He signed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965.

📚 PART 3 · LEADERSHIP

What They Had in Common

Look back at the four leaders. What do their stories share?

Preparation. Rayburn knew how Congress worked better than almost anyone. Jordan studied the Constitution and law until she could speak about it with ease. They did not trust the moment to do their work for them.

Courage. A filibuster lasting a day and a half against powerful men took nerve. So did Jordan’s decision to run in a state that had seen few Black officeholders in nearly a century. Courage in public life often means taking a risk for something other than yourself.

Persistence and patience. Rayburn worked for decades. González served in Congress for 37 years. Hutchison worked her way from the Texas House to the Senate. None of them won everything at once.

Service to something bigger than themselves. The best of these leaders used their office to help people who were not in the room. Rayburn pushed power lines out to farm families. González and Jordan spoke for Texans who had been shut out.

Notice what is missing from this list: money, and fame. None of the four became a leader just by wanting to be one. They became leaders by doing hard things for other people, again and again, until others trusted them with more. In the language of an older tradition, we would say they had developed habits that served the common good, and that is what we mean when we speak of virtue.

⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Preparation, courage, persistence and service ran through all four stories. Which of these four habits do you most want to build? (No wrong answer. Just check in with yourself.)
Courage in public life often means taking a risk for something other than yourself, like a filibuster or a run for office where few had won before. It usually starts with small, honest choices.

Every one of these leaders began as an ordinary Texan in a small place. Think about who in your own community leads by preparation, courage, and service, and what they do that makes others follow.
