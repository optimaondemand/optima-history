📚 PART 1 · THE COOL MACHINE

A Machine Changes a State

Texas summers are long and brutal. For generations, the heat held back growth. People built houses with high ceilings and wide porches, and they worked in the cool of the morning. Offices and factories were hard to run in July. In 1902, an engineer named Willis Carrier built the first modern machine to cool and dry air. For decades it stayed too costly for homes. But after World War II, window units and home systems became cheap enough for ordinary families.

Air conditioning changed where people were willing to live and work. Offices, factories, schools, and shopping centers could now run in comfort all summer. Builders put up sealed glass towers that would have been unbearable before. In 1965, Houston opened the Astrodome, the first big domed stadium with air conditioning. People called it the Eighth Wonder of the World. Cool air, along with cheap land and low taxes, drew workers and companies from the colder North.

The numbers tell the story. In 1940, Texas had about 6.4 million people, and most lived on farms and in small towns. By 1950, more Texans lived in cities than in the country, and the state became urban. By 1970, the population had grown to over 11 million. Historians call the warm southern and western states the Sunbelt. Texas became one of its biggest stars.

📚 PART 2 · THREE CITIES

Three Cities, Three Paths

Houston grew on oil, shipping, and then science. After the war, the city built the Texas Medical Center, which brought together hospitals and medical schools on one campus. Doctors there, including Michael DeBakey and Denton Cooley, became world leaders in heart surgery. In 1961, the United States chose Houston for its new Manned Spacecraft Center, now named for Lyndon Johnson. Rice University gave land for it. The Houston congressman Albert Thomas and Vice President Johnson helped to win the decision. Texans soon heard words like “Houston, we have a problem,” and the city’s name became part of the space race.

Houston also became the voice of the space program. Mission Control in Houston guided the Apollo flights, including the 1969 landing on the Moon. Thousands of engineers, scientists, and contractors moved to the area to work on spacecraft. Their jobs paid well, and they needed houses, schools, and shops. Spending by the federal government on science and space helped turn Houston into a city of research as well as oil.

Dallas and Fort Worth grew together into one big region. Dallas stayed the center of banking and business. Fort Worth added aircraft factories, and both cities built electronics and defense industries. In 1974, they opened one huge airport between them, Dallas-Fort Worth International. Dallas grew into a place where companies chose to put their headquarters.

San Antonio took a different road. It had few oil wells and few big factories. Its main strength was the military. Kelly Field, Fort Sam Houston, Lackland, and other bases employed thousands of people. The city also built on its history and its Mexican American culture. It made the San Antonio River Walk into a tourist attraction, and in 1968 it hosted a world’s fair called HemisFair. San Antonio grew large, but more slowly and with less wealth than Houston or Dallas. Each city kept its own character, which is one reason Texas has no single center.

📚 PART 3 · GROWTH AND ITS COSTS

Universities and the Cost of Growth

The war and the G.I. Bill sent many more Texans to college. The state’s two leading universities, the University of Texas at Austin and Texas A&M, grew into major research schools. They had a special advantage. Years earlier, the state had set aside public land in West Texas for their support. When oil was found there in 1923, the land earned large sums. The money went into the Permanent University Fund, and it helped build libraries, labs, and campuses. Research money from the government, much of it tied to the Space Race and the Cold War, did the rest.

Other schools grew too. Rice University in Houston, a private school, built a strong reputation in science and engineering. The University of Houston became a state university in 1963, and Texas A&M, which had once been an all-male military school, began to admit women the same year and made its military corps voluntary. Texas Southern University, a school for Black students founded in Houston in 1927, educated many leaders of the civil rights era.

Growth brought troubles as well. New people needed roads, houses, schools, and water, and cities spread outward in every direction. Houses and highways covered farmland and prairie. Water became a serious worry. West Texas farmers pumped the great Ogallala aquifer for their crops, and it began to drop faster than rain could fill it. Cities built reservoirs and pipelines. In 1957, after years of drought, the state set up a Water Development Board to plan for the future. In places like San Antonio, people argued over how to protect the Edwards Aquifer, which gave the city its drinking water.

Texans changed the environment to fit their growth. They dammed rivers, drained wetlands, and paved prairies. They also made choices about their resources, from oil to groundwater, that would matter for generations. Growth gave Texas new wealth and a new place in the nation. It also tied the state closely to the rest of the country. Yet new jobs and new people came faster than some of its old ways of life could adjust to. The boom brought opportunity to many, though not to all of them equally.
