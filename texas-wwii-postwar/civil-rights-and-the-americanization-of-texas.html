📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1946, a Black mail carrier in Houston walked into a law school and asked to be admitted. The state said no. The case that followed would change the nation.
Texas had become more like the rest of the country in its economy. But it was still part of the South, with laws that kept people apart by race. You will see how Black and Mexican American Texans took their cases to court, how Congress acted, and how Texas leaders helped lead the change.

📚 PART 1 · SEPARATE AND UNEQUAL

A Segregated State

After the war, Texas was a prosperous state, but it still kept many of its people separate and unequal. Black Texans went to separate schools, rode in separate sections of buses, and drank at separate water fountains. Most state colleges did not admit them. Mexican American Texans faced separate schools, signs that kept them out of restaurants and swimming pools, and a lack of fair treatment in courts. Both groups also faced barriers at the voting booth. The poll tax, a fee a person had to pay to vote, kept many poor people from the ballot.

Courts had already begun to change things. In 1944, in a case from Houston, a Black dentist named Lonnie Smith argued that Texas Democrats could not keep Black voters out of their primary election. In Smith v. Allwright, the Supreme Court agreed. This ended the white primary, a major tool for keeping Black Texans from voting. But much more remained.

📚 PART 2 · IN THE COURTS

Cases That Changed the Law

In 1946, Heman Sweatt, a Black mail carrier from Houston, applied to the University of Texas Law School. It was the best law school in the state, and it was all white. Texas refused to admit him. Instead, the state set up a hurried law school for Black students in a few rooms in Houston. Sweatt went to court. The NAACP lawyer Thurgood Marshall argued his case. In 1950, in Sweatt v. Painter, the Supreme Court ruled that the separate school was not equal. It could not give him the same education, with the same teachers, library, and standing. The Court ordered Texas to admit him. The decision did not end segregation, but it helped lead to the end of it. Four years later, in Brown v. Board of Education, the Court ruled that segregated public schools were unconstitutional.

Mexican Americans organized to defend their rights too. The League of United Latin American Citizens, or LULAC, began in Corpus Christi in 1929. It encouraged Mexican Americans to learn English, to vote, and to demand equal treatment as citizens. In 1948, Dr. Hector P. García, a Texas doctor and Army veteran, founded the American G.I. Forum to defend the rights of Mexican American veterans. A year later, the Forum took up the case of Felix Longoria, a soldier killed in the Philippines. A funeral home in his hometown refused to hold his funeral service because he was Mexican American. The protest drew national attention. Senator Lyndon Johnson arranged for Longoria to be buried at Arlington National Cemetery.

In 1954, Hernández v. Texas reached the Supreme Court. Pete Hernández, a farm worker, had been convicted of murder in Jackson County by an all-white jury. No Mexican American had served on a jury there in over twenty-five years. His lawyers, Gus García and Carlos Cadena, were the first Mexican American attorneys to argue before the Supreme Court. The Court ruled that the Constitution protects Mexican Americans from this kind of discrimination. It was an important victory for their rights.

📚 PART 3 · NEW LAWS, NEW LEADERS

Texans Lead the Way

By the 1960s, the national civil rights movement had grown strong. Texas was a part of that story, and one of its sons held the highest office. When President Kennedy was killed in 1963, Lyndon Johnson of Texas became president. He pressed Congress to pass the Civil Rights Act of 1964, which banned segregation in public places and discrimination in jobs. In 1965, he signed the Voting Rights Act. The states ratified the Twenty-fourth Amendment in 1964, which ended poll taxes in federal elections. In 1966, a federal court struck down the Texas poll tax for all elections.

Texans of both races stepped forward. Barbara Jordan, born in Houston in 1936, became a lawyer and a speaker of great power. In 1966, she was elected to the Texas Senate, the first Black person to serve there since the 1800s. In 1972, she went on to Congress. Oveta Culp Hobby, who had led the Women’s Army Corps in the war, helped run the Houston Post. In 1953, President Eisenhower named her the first Secretary of Health, Education, and Welfare. Each in her own way opened doors that had been shut to women.

Change came slowly. Many Texas school districts resisted ending segregation, and some did so only after lawsuits and federal orders. Discrimination did not disappear when the laws changed. But the direction was clear. Texas had joined the national economy, and now it was being held to the same constitutional standards as every other state. Some Texans resented being told what to do from Washington. Others, who had suffered under the old rules, welcomed the change. By the end of the 1960s, the old legal order of the Jim Crow South was gone, and Texas was no longer a place apart.
