💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
defense industry (dih-FENSS IN-dus-tree): Businesses that make weapons, ships, planes, and other goods for a country's armed forces.
mobilize (MOH-buh-lyz): To get a country, its people, and its factories ready for war.
pipeline (PYP-lyn): A long line of connected pipe used to carry oil or gas over great distances.
training field (TRAY-ning FEELD): A military base where new soldiers or pilots learn their skills.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When the war began, a cook on a battleship in Hawaii, a young man from Waco, rushed to a machine gun he had never been trained to fire.
World War II reached every part of Texas. Bases, factories, and shipyards filled with new workers. You will see how Texas helped mobilize for the war, what the new industry meant for its cities, and who got new opportunities along the way.

📚 PART 1 · THE CALL TO ARMS

From Pearl Harbor to the Training Fields

On December 7, 1941, Japanese planes attacked the U.S. naval base at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii. A Texan named Doris Miller, from Waco, was a cook on the battleship West Virginia. The Navy at that time did not let Black sailors serve in fighting jobs. During the attack, Miller helped carry wounded men, including his captain, and then took a machine gun and fired at the attacking planes. He received the Navy Cross, and he became a symbol of courage for many Americans. He was later killed in action in 1943.

The war brought a huge flow of soldiers into Texas. Good weather, open land, and the support of Texas congressmen made it an excellent place to train. Randolph Field and Kelly Field in San Antonio trained pilots. Fort Bliss in El Paso grew into a major post. In 1942, the Army opened Camp Hood near Killeen, which became Fort Hood and trained tank crews. Dozens of other camps, air fields, and hospitals were built. At Avenger Field in Sweetwater, a group of women known as the Women Airforce Service Pilots learned to fly military aircraft, so that men could be freed for combat.

Texans also served in great numbers, and many of them were Mexican Americans. Oveta Culp Hobby of Houston became the first director of the Women’s Army Auxiliary Corps, which later became the Women’s Army Corps. Texas was also the birthplace of two famous leaders. Dwight Eisenhower, born in Denison, led the Allied armies in Europe. Admiral Chester Nimitz, born in Fredericksburg, commanded the Pacific Fleet.

📚 PART 2 · THE HOME FRONT AT WORK

Factories, Ships, and Oil

The war created a giant defense industry in Texas. Before 1941, the state had few big factories. By 1945, it had many. Aircraft factories rose in Fort Worth and Dallas. At the Fort Worth plant, workers built B-24 bombers by the thousands, and in Dallas, North American Aviation built fighter planes and trainers. Many of these workers were women, who took jobs that men had once held.

The Gulf Coast became a center of shipbuilding. Yards at Houston, Orange, and Beaumont built destroyers, tankers, and Liberty ships, the cargo ships that carried supplies across the Atlantic. Workers poured in from farms and small towns. In Orange, a small town on the Sabine River, shipyards employed thousands, and housing was so short that many people lived in trailers or shared beds in shifts.

Texas also fueled the war. Oil was the lifeblood of modern armies, fueling ships, tanks, and bombers. Texas produced a very large share of the oil used by the Allies. German submarines sank tankers along the coast, which made shipping oil by sea dangerous. In response, the government built two long pipelines, the Big Inch and the Little Big Inch, to carry oil and fuel from Texas to the East Coast. Texas plants along the Gulf also made synthetic rubber and chemicals for the war. That planted the seeds of the great Texas petrochemical industry.

📚 PART 3 · AFTER THE WAR

A Changed State

The cities grew fast. In the 1940s, Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio each added many thousands of people. Military bases and factories gave jobs to workers who had been on farms or on relief a few years earlier. Housing shortages, crowded buses, and long lines for food became part of life. The war also brought rationing. Families used ration books to buy sugar, gasoline, tires, and meat, and they planted victory gardens. Texas schools collected scrap metal and sold war bonds.

For many Texans, wartime work meant new opportunities. Women entered factories and offices in great numbers. Black and Mexican American Texans found better-paying jobs in defense plants than had been open to them before, though they often still faced discrimination and the limits of segregation. After the war, many did not want to go back to the old ways. That helped set the stage for the civil rights movement.

In Crystal City, in South Texas, the government ran an internment camp. Families of Japanese, German, and Italian descent, including some American citizens, were held there. Texas also held many German prisoners of war in camps, where they worked on farms and in other jobs. These camps were far from the battlefield, yet they were part of the same war.

When the war ended in 1945, Texas was a different place. It had more people, more factories, more military bases, and more ties to Washington. The state had gone into the war with a mostly rural economy, and it came out of it with a growing set of cities and modern industries. Texans had given a great deal. Thousands did not come home. And the state that had been shaped by cattle and cotton was now built, in part, on planes, ships, and chemicals.
