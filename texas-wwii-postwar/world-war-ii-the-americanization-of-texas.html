📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Texas had once been its own country. By 1945, a Texan soldier might wear the same uniform, carry the same ration book, and read the same news as a soldier from Ohio.
Radio and the New Deal began to link Texas more closely to the nation. The war carried that much further. You will see how the draft, federal spending, and wartime rules bound Texas into national life, how the Bracero Program started, and what Texans kept that was distinctly their own.

📚 PART 1 · TEXANS IN THE SERVICE

One Nation in Uniform

In 1940, Congress passed the first peacetime draft in American history. Every young man had to register, and the government decided who would serve. After Pearl Harbor, the draft grew. Hundreds of thousands of Texans served in the armed forces, and thousands lost their lives. A young man from Lubbock and one from Boston might end up in the same barracks. Soldiers from Texas ate the same food, trained under the same rules, and fought in the same units as soldiers from every other state.

This mixing mattered. Before the war, many Texans had rarely left their home county. Now they saw the Pacific, North Africa, and Europe. They met people from other regions and other countries. Some returned with new ideas about how the world worked. Many of the soldiers later said that the war made them feel American in a way they had not before. At the same time, they still thought of themselves as Texans. The two feelings lived side by side.

Mexican American soldiers show how complex this was. Many served with great honor. Macario García, who was born in Mexico and raised near Sugar Land, won the Medal of Honor for his bravery in Europe. After he returned home to Texas in 1945, a cafe in Richmond refused to serve him because of his background. The story is a reminder that fighting for the country did not always bring equal treatment at home.

📚 PART 2 · THE NATION REACHES IN

Rules From Washington

The war also drew Texans into a national system of rules. Washington set prices and wages, controlled the flow of raw materials, and decided which factories would build what. rationing meant that every family in the country followed the same rules for sugar, gas, and tires. People who did not obey could be punished. The federal government became the biggest customer for Texas goods, and the biggest employer.

Money followed. Federal spending on bases, factories, and shipyards in Texas totaled many billions of dollars. Most of the state’s new factories and training fields were planned in Washington, not Austin. When the war ended, many of those bases and plants stayed open, and the federal government kept spending. Texas was now tied to national institutions much more closely than before. That ranged from the Army and Navy to Social Security and income taxes.

The war also brought a major new labor program. By 1942, American farms had lost workers to the draft and to defense jobs. In August of that year, the United States and Mexico signed an agreement that brought Mexican workers north to harvest crops. They were called braceros, from a Spanish word that refers to a person who works with his arms. The contracts promised wages, housing, and fair treatment, though workers often found that those promises were not kept. Many braceros did the hardest farm labor in the country.

💡 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
bracero (brah-SAIR-oh): A Mexican worker who came to the United States under the wartime farm labor agreement. The word comes from the Spanish word for arm.

Texas, though, was left out at first. Mexico refused to send braceros to Texas for several years, because of how Mexican Americans and Mexican workers had been treated there, with segregation and low pay. Texas did not join the program until 1947. The program lasted until 1964, and millions of contracts were signed in all. Its effects reached far beyond the war years.

📚 PART 3 · AFTER THE WAR

What Changed, and What Stayed

The war ended in 1945. For returning soldiers, the G.I. Bill paid for college and helped many buy homes. Thousands of Texans who would never have gone to college now did. That helped build the state’s universities and a growing middle class. These benefits came from the federal government, which was now a regular part of Texas life.

Many historians say that World War II largely finished a process that radio and the New Deal had begun, the Americanization of Texas. By 1945, Texans served in the same army, paid the same taxes, followed the same national rules, and listened to the same news as other Americans. The old idea of Texas as a place apart was weaker than ever. Even so, the change was not one thing that had to happen. It came from decisions by Texans and by Washington, along with a war that left no other choice.

Some things stayed. Texans kept their pride in the state’s history. They kept many of their own laws, and they kept a stronger belief in limited government than many other places. And Texas did not become just like everywhere else. Its oil, its cattle, its border with Mexico, and its mix of peoples stayed part of its story. In the next decades, the state would find new ways to be both American and Texan.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A war can bind a people together more tightly than years of peace. Joining a larger nation brought Texans benefits and also took away some of their separateness. When two identities meet, ask what each one gives up, and what each one keeps.
