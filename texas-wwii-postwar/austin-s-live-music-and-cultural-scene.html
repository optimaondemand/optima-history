📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In the early 1970s, a long-haired country singer from Abbott, Texas, packed up in Nashville and moved to Austin. Soon, cowboys and hippies were dancing in the same hall.
Austin has been a city of artists and thinkers for a long time. In the 1970s, its music scene made it famous. You will meet some earlier Texas artists and writers, see how the Armadillo and a television show put Austin on the map, and see why a college town became a music town.

📚 PART 1 · ARTISTS AND THINKERS

A City of Artists

Long before the music scene, Austin had a culture of its own. Elisabet Ney, a German sculptor, came to Texas in 1873 and settled in Austin. She made statues of Sam Houston and Stephen F. Austin, which now stand in the state Capitol and in the U.S. Capitol in Washington. Her studio, Formosa, was a meeting place for artists and thinkers, and it is now a museum.

In the early and middle 1900s, three Texas writers gathered in Austin and became close friends. J. Frank Dobie wrote about the cowboys, stories, and wild places of the Southwest. Walter Prescott Webb, a historian, wrote about how the Great Plains shaped Texas. Roy Bedichek, a naturalist, wrote about birds and the outdoors, including a book called Adventures with a Texas Naturalist. They often met to talk and swim at Barton Springs, a spring-fed pool in the middle of the city. They helped make Austin a place where people thought and wrote about Texas.

📚 PART 2 · ARMADILLOS AND OUTLAWS

The Armadillo

In 1970, a group of young Austinites turned an old National Guard armory on the south side of town into a music hall. They called it the Armadillo World Headquarters. The armadillo was a funny Texas animal, and an artist named Jim Franklin painted it on posters. The Armadillo was cheap, loose, and welcoming. It had a beer garden, a big stage, and an audience that included college students, hippies, and ranchers.

In those days, Texans in music mostly sorted themselves into groups. Rock fans did not mix with country fans. Austin was different. In 1972, Willie Nelson, a Texas songwriter who had been unhappy in Nashville, moved back to Austin. He grew his hair long, wore jeans, and played for crowds that cheered him on. He and other singers, such as Waylon Jennings, were called “outlaws,” because they recorded music their own way instead of the way the Nashville studios wanted. The style was called outlaw country. Austin people sometimes called its fans “cosmic cowboys.”

The music spread beyond the city. In 1974, a public television station at the University of Texas made a pilot for a show called Austin City Limits. Willie Nelson played on the first episode. The program began its regular run in 1975 and is still on the air. It showed viewers across the country what was going on in Austin. Musicians from many styles, from blues to rock to country, played on its stage. The show helped turn Austin’s music into something the whole nation knew.

📚 PART 3 · A MUSIC CAPITAL

The Live Music Capital

Blues and rock grew in Austin, too. A club called Antone’s opened in 1975 and brought in great blues players from Texas and Chicago to perform. A young guitarist named Stevie Ray Vaughan, who grew up in Dallas and made his name in Austin, became a national star in the 1980s. After his death in 1990, the city placed a statue of him along the lake in downtown Austin. Austin became a city where players of many kinds could find a stage and a crowd.

The Armadillo closed in 1980, but its spirit lived on. In 1987, a group of Austin friends, who worked at a local newspaper and in the music business, started a festival called South by Southwest, or SXSW. About 700 people came the first year. It was meant to show off local and regional bands. It grew into a giant event with film and technology sections as well. Hundreds of thousands of visitors now come to Austin each March. In 1991, the state officially named Austin the Live Music Capital of the World.

Why Austin? Students play a big part. The University of Texas has tens of thousands of undergraduates who want to hear music, and they make a ready audience on any night. Cheap rent in early years meant musicians could afford to live in the city. The state government and the university also meant a steady crowd of educated listeners. Clubs on Sixth Street and elsewhere gave bands places to play nearly every night of the week.

Austin has also tried to hold on to its identity. Around 2000, local residents began a slogan, “Keep Austin Weird.” It meant they wanted to protect small, local businesses and unusual people against big chains. Some people felt that the city was getting too expensive and too much like everywhere else. Others said the slogan had itself become a marketing gimmick. That argument continues.

There is a link between the music scene and the tech scene. People moved to Austin for the culture, and companies followed them. Austin’s way of life, which was more relaxed and creative than many Texas cities, made it unlike most of the state. In a time when Texas was becoming more like the rest of America, Austin showed that one Texas city could stay different.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A dusty armory, a television show, and a college crowd helped make a city known for its music. When a place gets a reputation, ask who built it, who keeps it alive, and who is priced out when it succeeds.
