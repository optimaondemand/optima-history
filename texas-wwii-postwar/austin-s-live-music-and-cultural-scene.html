armory (AR-mur-ee): A building where weapons and military equipment are stored and where soldiers train.
blues (BLOOZ): A style of music that grew out of African American communities in the South, known for tough or sad stories and bending guitar notes.
festival (FES-tuh-vul): A special event with music, food, and activities, often held every year.
outlaw country (OWT-law KUN-tree): A style of country music of the 1970s that rejected the polished Nashville sound.
pilot (PY-lut): A trial episode made to test a new television show.
sculptor (SKULP-ter): An artist who shapes stone, clay, metal, or other materials into figures.
songwriter (SONG-ry-ter): A person who writes the words or music of songs, often for other singers to perform.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In the early 1970s, a long-haired country singer from Abbott, Texas, packed up in Nashville and moved to Austin. Soon, cowboys and hippies were dancing in the same hall.
Austin has been a city of artists and thinkers for a long time. In the 1970s, its music scene made it famous. You will meet some earlier Texas artists and writers, learn about Willie Nelson, Waylon Jennings, and Stevie Ray Vaughan, see how the Armadillo and a television show put Austin on the map, and see why a college town became a music town.

📚 PART 1 · ARTISTS AND THINKERS

A City of Artists

Long before the music scene, Austin had a culture of its own. Elisabet Ney, a German sculptor, came to Texas from Germany and built a studio in Austin in the early 1890s. She made statues of Sam Houston and Stephen F. Austin, which now stand in the state Capitol and in the U.S. Capitol in Washington. Her studio, Formosa, was a meeting place for artists and thinkers, and it is now a museum.

In the early and middle 1900s, three Texas writers gathered in Austin and became close friends. J. Frank Dobie wrote about the cowboys, stories, and wild places of the Southwest. Walter Prescott Webb, a historian, wrote about how the Great Plains shaped Texas, and his book The Great Plains, published in 1931, became one of the best-known books about the region. Roy Bedichek, a naturalist, wrote about birds and the outdoors, including a book called Adventures with a Texas Naturalist. They often met to talk and swim at Barton Springs, a spring-fed pool in the middle of the city. They helped make Austin a place where people thought and wrote about Texas.

Folk Songs at a Gas Station

Music was already growing in Austin by the early 1960s. A former gas station on North Lamar Boulevard, called Threadgill’s and run by Kenneth Threadgill, became a place where students and local musicians sat together and played. A young University of Texas student named Janis Joplin sang there on Wednesday nights before she became a famous rock singer. Austin’s mix of students, lawmakers, and working musicians was beginning to form an audience, and in a few years that audience would need a bigger room.

📚 PART 2 · ARMADILLOS AND OUTLAWS

The Armadillo

In 1970, a group of young Austinites, including a band manager named Eddie Wilson, turned an old National Guard armory on Barton Springs Road, just south of downtown, into a music hall. They called it the Armadillo World Headquarters. The armadillo was a funny Texas animal, and an artist named Jim Franklin painted it on posters. The Armadillo was cheap, loose, and welcoming. It had a beer garden, a big stage, and an audience that included college students, hippies, and ranchers.

In those days, Texans in music mostly sorted themselves into groups. Rock fans did not mix with country fans. Austin was different, and Willie Nelson was a big reason. He was born in 1933 in Abbott, a small Texas town, and as a young man he wrote songwriter songs for a living. In 1960 he went to Nashville, Tennessee, the center of country music. His song “Crazy” became a hit for the singer Patsy Cline in 1961, and other stars recorded his songs too. But the Nashville studios wanted a smooth, polished sound, and Nelson’s own records did not sell well.

In 1972, Nelson moved back to Texas and settled in Austin. He grew his hair long, wore jeans, and played at the Armadillo for crowds of college students and old-time country fans. In 1973 he started a yearly Fourth of July Picnic, first held near Dripping Springs. In 1975 he made a quiet, simple album called Red Headed Stranger, which he produced himself. A song on it, “Blue Eyes Crying in the Rain,” made him a national star.

Waylon Jennings was another Texan. He was born in Littlefield, worked as a radio disc jockey, and in 1958 joined the band of the rock and roll star Buddy Holly as a bass player. In 1959, Jennings gave up his seat on the small plane that crashed and killed Holly. Later he moved to Nashville and signed with a big record company, but he felt that producers told him too much about which songs to sing and which musicians to use. In the early 1970s, he and Nelson both fought for the right to make records their own way. Jennings’s 1973 album Honky Tonk Heroes showed what he could do with that freedom.

Nelson and Jennings, and other singers like them, were called “outlaws,” because they recorded music their own way instead of the way the Nashville studios wanted. The style was called outlaw country. Austin people sometimes called its fans “cosmic cowboys.” In 1976, a collection called Wanted! The Outlaws, with songs by Jennings, Nelson, Jessi Colter, and Tompall Glaser, became the first country album to be certified platinum for selling a million copies. In 1977, Jennings had a hit with “Luckenbach, Texas,” a song about a tiny Hill Country town, and in 1978 he and Nelson sang a duet, “Mammas Don’t Let Your Babies Grow Up to Be Cowboys.”

Austin City Limits

The music spread beyond the city. A manager at KLRU, the public television station at the University of Texas, named Bill Arhos wanted a show that would put Austin’s music on the air. In 1974, he got money from PBS to make a pilot for a show called Austin City Limits. The pilot was taped on October 17, 1974, with Willie Nelson as the star. PBS picked the show up the next year, and its regular run began in 1975.

The show was taped in a small studio on the university campus, called Studio 6A, where the audience sat close to the stage. It showed viewers across the country what was going on in Austin. Musicians from many styles, from blues to rock to country, played on its stage. The show is still on the air, and it is now called the longest-running music show in the history of American television. Today it is taped at a larger theater in downtown Austin, where a statue of Willie Nelson, dedicated in 2012, stands outside. The show also gave Austin another event. In 2002, the Austin City Limits Music Festival began in Zilker Park, and it has been held there ever since. The show helped turn Austin’s music into something the whole nation knew.

📚 PART 3 · A MUSIC CAPITAL

The Live Music Capital

blues and rock grew in Austin, too. A club called Antone’s opened on Sixth Street in 1975. Its founder, Clifford Antone, had grown up in Port Arthur and came to Austin in 1965 to attend the University of Texas. The club brought in great blues players from Texas and Chicago. Jimmie Vaughan and his younger brother, a guitarist named Stevie Ray Vaughan, became part of its circle.

Stevie Ray Vaughan was born in Dallas in 1954 and made his name in Austin. In 1978 he formed a band called Double Trouble, named for a blues song by Otis Rush. He played with great speed and a thick, loud Texas sound. The record producer John Hammond helped the band win a record contract. Their first album, Texas Flood, came out in 1983, and that year Vaughan also played guitar on David Bowie’s hit album Let’s Dance. He became a national star in the 1980s.

On August 27, 1990, Vaughan died in a helicopter crash in Wisconsin after a concert on tour. He was 35. After his death, the city placed a statue of him along the lake in downtown Austin,. In 2015, he and Double Trouble were inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame. Austin became a city where players of many kinds could find a stage and a crowd.

Songwriters on the Stage

Another strength of Austin’s music was songwriting. Many Texas songwriters wrote songs like short stories, and they played in Austin clubs and on Austin City Limits. Guy Clark, who was born in Monahans, Texas, in 1941, wrote songs such as “Desperados Waiting for a Train.” Other singers recorded his songs, and younger writers looked up to him. Steve Earle, who was born in Virginia but grew up in San Antonio, played bass in Clark’s band as a young man. Earle’s first album, Guitar Town, came out in 1986, and he made his first appearance on Austin City Limits in 1987.

Robert Earl Keen, who was born in Houston in 1956, wrote songs about Texas life that were both funny and sad. He first played on Austin City Limits in 1989 and returned several times. Songs such as “The Road Goes On Forever” and “Merry Christmas from the Family” became favorites at his concerts. Kris Kristofferson, a songwriter born in Brownsville, Texas, who wrote “Me and Bobby McGee,” also appeared on the show, including a songwriters special with Willie Nelson and Waylon Jennings.

The Armadillo closed in 1980, but its spirit lived on. In 1987, a group of Austin friends, who worked at a local newspaper and in the music business, started a festival called South by Southwest, or SXSW. About 700 people came the first year. It was meant to show off local and regional bands. It grew into a giant event with film and technology sections as well. Hundreds of thousands of visitors now come to Austin each March. In 1991, the state officially named Austin the Live Music Capital of the World.

Why Austin? Students play a big part. The University of Texas has tens of thousands of undergraduates who want to hear music, and they make a ready audience on any night. Cheap rent in early years meant musicians could afford to live in the city. The state government and the university also meant a steady crowd of educated listeners. Clubs on Sixth Street, a downtown street lined with old brick buildings, and elsewhere gave bands places to play nearly every night of the week. A band could start in a small room, build a following among students, and then move to bigger stages.

Austin has also tried to hold on to its identity. Around 2000, local residents began a slogan, “Keep Austin Weird.” It meant they wanted to protect small, local businesses and unusual people against big chains.

There is a link between the music scene and the tech scene. People moved to Austin for the culture, and companies followed them. Austin’s way of life, which was more relaxed and creative than many Texas cities, made it unlike most of the state. In a time when Texas was becoming more like the rest of America, Austin showed that one Texas city could stay different.
