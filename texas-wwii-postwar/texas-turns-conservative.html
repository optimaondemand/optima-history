💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
conservative (kun-SER-vuh-tiv): Favoring limited government, lower taxes, and the keeping of traditional values and customs.
liberal (LIB-er-ul): Favoring a larger role for government in helping people and fixing social problems, and more change in customs.
political party (puh-LIT-ih-kul PAR-tee): An organized group of people with similar ideas who work to win elections and run the government.
realignment (ree-uh-LYN-munt): A lasting shift in which groups of voters change which political party they support.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
For nearly a hundred years after Reconstruction, almost every elected official in Texas was a Democrat. Then, in a few decades, the map turned.
Texas politics changed more in the late 1900s than at almost any time since Reconstruction. You will see how the state went from a Democratic stronghold to a Republican one, what reasons historians give, and how Texas moved away from the national mood of the 1950s and 1960s.

📚 PART 1 · ONE PARTY FOR A CENTURY

One Party for a Century

After Reconstruction ended, white Texans overwhelmingly voted for the Democratic Party. The party stood against the Republicans who had led the Union and Reconstruction, and for decades it won nearly every election in the state. In Texas, the real contest was often the Democratic primary, where candidates fought each other for the nomination. The Republican Party was small. Black Texans, who could often not vote freely, had little say.

But the Texas Democrats were not united. Some, called conservative Democrats, wanted low taxes, limited government, and good relations with business and the oil industry. Others, called liberal Democrats, supported the New Deal and a larger role for government. For years, the two groups competed inside the same party. Lyndon Johnson, for example, belonged to the Democrats and often worked with both wings.

Two Senate races show the split. Ralph Yarborough was a liberal Democrat who backed civil rights and federal programs. In 1970, he lost his party’s primary to Lloyd Bentsen, a wealthy and more conservative Democrat. Bentsen went on to serve in the Senate for over twenty years. In 1990, Texans elected Ann Richards, a liberal Democrat, as governor. She was funny, tough, and popular. Even so, her time in office was short. Texas Democrats could still win, but they had to fight harder each time.

The first big crack appeared in 1961. When Lyndon Johnson became Vice President, his Senate seat opened up. In a special election, a Republican named John Tower, a college professor from Wichita Falls, won it. He was the first Texas Republican in the U.S. Senate since Reconstruction. Many voters liked his message of strong defense and smaller government. The win showed that Texans might vote for a Republican.

📚 PART 2 · THE TURN

The Turn

The change grew in the decades after. Several things pushed it. First came the national fight over civil rights. When Democrats in Washington passed the Civil Rights Act of 1964 and the Voting Rights Act of 1965, some white conservative voters in the South began to leave the party. Second, the national Democratic Party moved in a more liberal direction, supporting bigger government programs and new social changes. Many Texans did not agree. John Connally, who was a Democratic governor, switched to the Republican Party in 1973.

Voters also looked at the presidential race. Texas voted for Democrat Jimmy Carter, a Southerner, in 1976. But in 1980, it supported Ronald Reagan, a Republican who promised lower taxes, a stronger military, and less government. Texas has voted Republican for president in every election since 1980. In 1978, William Clements became the first Republican governor of Texas since Reconstruction. His win was narrow, but it showed that a Republican could win statewide.

The pace quickened in the 1990s. In 1994, George W. Bush, the son of a former president who had moved to Texas, defeated Democratic Governor Ann Richards. In 1998, Republicans won every statewide office. By the early 2000s, they also held both houses of the legislature. A state that had been a Democratic stronghold for a century had become a Republican one in about a generation.

📚 PART 3 · WHY DID IT HAPPEN?

Why Did It Happen?

Historians point to a mix of reasons. One is economic. Texas had grown rich on oil, business, and growth. Many Texans valued low taxes and free markets, and they felt that Republicans spoke for those ideas. Newcomers from other states brought their own views. Many moved to the suburbs, which leaned toward the Republican Party. Business leaders in Houston and Dallas, who had often backed conservative Democrats, now supported Republicans.

Culture mattered too. Many Texans were religious, and many were uneasy about rapid changes in family life, schools, and the courts. The rise of Christian conservative groups pushed many churchgoing voters toward the Republican Party. Texas also had a long tradition of self-reliance and a distrust of distant government. Some historians argue that the realignment is an old Texas outlook finding a new party, rather than a sudden change of heart.

Elections and money also played a part. After 1980, Republicans built strong local groups in suburbs and small towns, and raised large sums to back candidates. Democrats had held power for so long that many had grown used to winning without a hard fight. Redrawing the lines of voting districts, which the party in power does after each census, also helped the Republicans keep their gains once they won the legislature. Voters who liked one party’s candidates often kept voting for that party, which made the change last.

Not everyone agrees on the causes, and not every Texan changed. Large cities such as Houston, Dallas, San Antonio, and Austin often voted Democratic. Many Mexican American voters in South Texas leaned Democratic as well. The state has grown more varied, and its politics are not one thing. Elections can be close, and no result lasts forever.

This shift also marks a divergence. In the 1950s and 1960s, Texas politicians such as Johnson and Sam Rayburn helped shape a national consensus in favor of a strong federal government. In the following decades, Texas moved away from that consensus. It supports lower taxes, fewer regulations, and a smaller role for Washington more strongly than the country as a whole. This is one way Texas became distinct again, after years of growing more alike.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Voters change their loyalties slowly, for many reasons. When you study a political change, ask what the voters wanted, what moved them, and why thoughtful people explain it differently.
