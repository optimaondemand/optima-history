📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Texas is the second largest state in the nation. Most days, it looks like the rest of America. But a few things about it are not like anywhere else.
For about a hundred years, Texas grew closer to the nation. Then, in some ways, it began to move apart again. You will see how Texas came to converge with the rest of the United States, where it began to diverge, and why the border matters to both stories.

📚 PART 1 · COMING TOGETHER

Coming Together

In 1900, Texas was still a frontier state in many ways. Most people lived on farms, cotton was king, and the state had only recently closed its open range. Texans also remembered that their state had been its own nation. Over the next seventy years, a series of changes drew Texas closer to the rest of the country.

Railroads and telephones tied Texas to national markets. Oil gave it a place in a world economy. Radio brought the same programs and news to every town. The New Deal brought federal jobs, electricity, and farm programs, and it made Washington a regular presence. World War II built bases and factories across the state and sent hundreds of thousands of Texans to fight beside Americans from every region. After the war, air conditioning and growing cities made Texas part of the booming Sunbelt. Finally, the civil rights movement brought the state’s laws into line with national constitutional standards.

By the middle of the century, a Texan could work for a national company, serve in a national army, watch national television, and vote in national elections, much as people did in Ohio or Georgia. Texas leaders held top positions in Washington. Lyndon Johnson and Sam Rayburn guided national policy. Texas, in many ways, had become a typical American state.

📚 PART 2 · DRIFTING APART

Drifting Apart

Yet beginning around 1970, Texas started to move in its own direction in several ways. The first was politics. Beginning in the 1960s, Texas voters turned from the Democratic Party to the Republican Party, and the state moved away from the national mood of the mid-century. Texans have often favored lower taxes, less regulation, and a smaller role for Washington more than the nation as a whole. Not all Texans agree, but the state has taken its own course.

The second is energy and the economy. Texas is the nation’s largest producer of oil and natural gas. It is also the country’s leading producer of wind power. Texas has its own electric grid, run by an organization called ERCOT, which mostly stays inside the state. Because it is separate from the large national grids, it is mostly free from federal oversight, but it also cannot easily borrow power from other states. In February 2021, a deadly winter storm knocked out much of the grid, and millions of Texans lost power in freezing weather. Hundreds died. The disaster began a national debate about the costs and benefits of Texas’s independent system.

The third is people. Texas had about 29 million residents in 2020, and it adds hundreds of thousands each year. It has become one of the most diverse states in the country. About four in ten Texans are Hispanic, and large groups of Black, Asian, and white Texans live in its cities and suburbs. Its largest cities, such as Houston, Dallas, San Antonio, and Austin, are among the biggest in the nation. And its culture, from barbecue and Tejano music to football on Friday nights, still has a strong Texas flavor. The state has also built its own tech and music scenes, and both have an unusual character.

📚 PART 3 · THE BORDER

The Border

One feature of Texas links the whole story. It is the border with Mexico, about 1,254 miles of it along the Rio Grande. It was fixed in 1848, after a war between the United States and Mexico. For a long time, it divided two countries and also two ways of life. People on both sides have crossed it for many reasons, including work, trade, family, and safety. The history of the border is part of the history of Texas from the days of Spain to the present.

In the twentieth century, the border became more connected than ever. Mexican workers came north in wartime and after, and many stayed and raised families. Factories on the Mexican side and trucking on the Texas side tied the economies together. Since 1994, the North American Free Trade Agreement and its successors have helped trade grow into a very large business. Texas is the nation’s biggest exporter, and Mexico is its biggest customer.

The border also brings disagreements. Texans argue about immigration, security, and how the state and the nation should share responsibility. They also share a precious resource. The Rio Grande is a main source of water for farms and cities on both sides, and a 1944 treaty between the two countries set rules for how it is shared. Droughts have made this water a source of tension. The river that marks the boundary also ties its neighbors together.

📚 PART 4 · BOTH AT ONCE

Both at Once

Is Texas just another American state, or a place apart? The answer is both. Its economy is tied to the nation’s, its people follow national news, and its leaders sit in Washington. At the same time, Texas has its own history as a republic, its own political habits, its own power grid, and its own sense of identity. A place can be deeply a part of a nation and still be itself.

Historians do not see this as a story with a set ending. Texans, and the people who came to live among them, shaped each step by the choices they made. The question of what it means to be both American and Texan will be answered again by each new generation.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A people can grow closer to a nation and still keep something of its own. When you ask what makes a place distinct, ask what it has in common with its neighbors, where it goes its own way, and who gets to decide.
