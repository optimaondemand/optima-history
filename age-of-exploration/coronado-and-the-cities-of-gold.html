📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A rumor can send an army across a continent.
After Estevanico and his companions returned, Spaniards could not stop talking about the north. Was there a second Mexico out there, with cities full of gold? One huge expedition set out to find out. It crossed some of the strangest country any European had ever seen, including the Texas Panhandle, and it came back with nothing. This lesson follows it.

📚 PART 1 · FROM RUMOR TO FACT

The Seven Cities

The viceroy of New Spain wanted to know if the stories were true. In 1539, he sent a friar named Fray Marcos de Niza north, with Estevanico as a guide. Estevanico went ahead of the group and reached a town of the Zuni people, called Cíbola. The Zuni killed him there. Fray Marcos de Niza did not go in. He said that he saw the town from a hill far away, and that it looked bigger than Mexico City.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Cíbola (SEE-boh-lah). The Spanish name for the Zuni towns in what is now New Mexico, where the "Seven Cities" were said to be.
Fray Marcos de Niza (fry MAR-kohs deh NEE-sah). A friar sent north in 1539 to check the stories about rich cities. "Fray" means "brother" and is used for friars.

Many Spaniards already believed an old European story about seven rich cities somewhere across the sea. Now a friar seemed to have found them. A rumor turned into a "fact" in a few months, even though no Spaniard had walked into the town and looked inside.

📚 PART 2 · THE MARCH

Coronado sets out

In 1540, a young governor named Coronado led the largest expedition yet. It included several hundred Spanish soldiers, many Native allies and servants from Mexico, and herds of horses, cattle, and sheep. They marched north for months to Cíbola. They found a Zuni town built of stone and mud brick. There was no gold. Coronado's men fought their way in and found food, not treasure.

The Zuni did not give up their town easily. They fought back, and Coronado was hurt when stones struck him. The Zuni people still live in that part of New Mexico today.

Later, when Coronado's men saw Cíbola up close, they were furious with the friar. He had made a small Zuni town sound like a golden city. Whether he lied or only saw what he hoped to see is something historians still debate.

The expedition spent the winter along the Rio Grande, where fighting broke out with the Pueblo people. By spring, the men were tired and angry. Then a man the Spaniards called "the Turk" told them about a rich land called Quivira, far to the east. He said its ruler ate from plates of gold.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Coronado (kor-oh-NAH-doh). Francisco Vázquez de Coronado, the Spanish governor who led the expedition north in 1540.
Quivira (kee-VEE-rah). A land of farming towns in what is now Kansas. The expedition was told it was full of gold.
📚 PART 3 · THE LLANO ESTACADO

An ocean of grass

To reach Quivira, Coronado marched east onto the Great Plains. He crossed a high, flat grassland that the Spaniards called the Llano Estacado. Today we call it the Staked Plains. It lies in the Texas Panhandle. One Spanish soldier wrote that traveling across it was like being at sea. There were no trees, no hills, and no landmarks. A man could ride all day and see the same horizon in every direction.

The Spaniards feared getting lost. Scouts piled up bison dung and bones to mark a trail they could follow back. In many places there was little wood and little water.

Even measuring distance was a problem. Some of the Spaniards counted their steps to keep track of how far they had gone. A terrible hailstorm struck the army on the plains, smashing equipment and frightening the horses.

Here they met people who lived on the plains. The Spaniards called them Querechos. These nomads lived in tents made of tanned bison hides, and they followed the herds from place to place. They used dogs to carry their loads, because this was before the horse came to the plains. People today often link the Querechos to the Apache, who will become very important in the story of Texas.

The dogs pulled a simple sled called a travois, made from poles. It was a clever way to move a camp without horses. When the horse reached the plains many years later, everything about this way of life would change.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Querechos (keh-REH-chohs). The name the Spaniards gave to bison hunters they met on the plains.
📚 PART 4 · QUIVIRA

The end of the road

At last, Coronado reached Quivira, in what is now Kansas. The people there lived in round houses made of grass, and they farmed corn. There was no gold. The Spaniards believed the Turk had lied to them, and he was put to death. In 1542, Coronado turned back. The long march had found no riches and no second Mexico.

People today link Quivira to the ancestors of the Wichita people. They farmed, hunted, and lived in settled towns, but they had nothing like the gold the Spaniards had been promised.

But the expedition did change what Spain knew. It had crossed a huge grassland, met people who lived by hunting bison, and seen farming towns far from Mexico. None of it matched the dream. And the dream itself had begun as a rumor that grew a little bigger each time somebody told it.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Coronado came looking for a city of gold and found grass, wind, and people who lived on the bison. Spain still wanted a second Mexico, and another army was searching in the east at the same time.
