📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does a few hundred soldiers bring down an empire of millions?
In 1519, the conquistador Hernán Cortés landed on the coast of Mexico. Two years later, the greatest city in the Americas belonged to Spain. This lesson is the control case for the whole quarter: understanding exactly why the Spanish model worked here is what makes three hundred years of it failing in Texas make sense.

📚 PART 1 · AN EMPIRE OF MILLIONS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
Before you read on: how many soldiers do you think it would take to conquer a city of 200,000 people? (No wrong answer — just check in with yourself.)

Tenochtitlán

By 1519, the Mexica (Aztec) capital of Tenochtitlán held a population of roughly 200,000 people, built on an island in a lake and connected to the shore by causeways. Tribute from millions of people across a wide empire flowed into the city. This was not a village Cortés was approaching. It was one of the largest cities on Earth.

When Cortés landed in 1519, he had a few hundred Spanish soldiers. On paper, that should not have been enough to threaten an empire this size. It very nearly was not.

📚 PART 2 · CONQUEST BY COALITION

Allies, not just soldiers

The Mexica ruled their empire by demanding tribute from conquered peoples, and not every one of those peoples was loyal. When Cortés reached Tlaxcala, a rival state the Mexica had never fully subdued, he found people willing to fight alongside him against a shared enemy. Thousands of Tlaxcalan warriors joined the Spanish force. This is the detail easiest to leave out of the story, and the one that actually explains it: Cortés did not conquer an empire with a few hundred men. He assembled a coalition, and that coalition numbered in the tens of thousands.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
A student who argues "Spanish weapons alone explain the conquest" is missing most of the actual explanation. Steel swords and horses mattered, but thousands of Tlaxcalan allies and, soon, a devastating epidemic mattered more.

Disease finishes what the coalition began

In 1520, smallpox arrived with the Spanish, a disease the Mexica had no immunity to. It swept through the city, killing enormous numbers of people, including much of Tenochtitlán's leadership, in the middle of the siege. The city fell in 1521. No one planned the epidemic. It was not a weapon Cortés chose. It was a consequence of contact between two populations that had never met before, and it happened to arrive at the exact moment it would matter most.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Coalition and disease, not weapons alone, explain Tenochtitlán's fall. Hold onto that as you read on: both of those depended on Mexico already having a large, divided, settled population for Spain to work with. What happens where none of that exists?
