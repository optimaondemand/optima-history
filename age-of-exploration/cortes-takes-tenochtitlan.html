📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How could a few hundred men topple an empire of millions?
In 1519, a Spanish officer named Cortés landed on the coast of Mexico with about five hundred soldiers. Two years later, one of the most powerful empires in North America was gone. The answer to how it happened is not what most people expect. The Spaniards did not win by themselves.

📚 PART 1 · THE MEXICA EMPIRE

A city of canals and causeways

In 1519, central Mexico was ruled by the Mexica. Their capital, Tenochtitlán, sat on an island in a lake. Wide roads called causeways connected it to the shore, and canals ran through the streets. Perhaps 200,000 people lived there, which made it as big as the largest cities in Europe.

Farmers grew food on small man-made islands in the lake. Huge markets sold everything from cloth to gold to turkeys. When Spanish soldiers first saw the city, one of them later said it looked like something out of a storybook.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Mexica (meh-SHEE-kah). The people who ruled the empire in central Mexico. They are often called the Aztecs.
Moctezuma (mohk-teh-SOO-mah). The ruler of the Mexica when Cortés arrived.
Tenochtitlán (teh-nohch-teet-LAHN). The capital city of the Mexica empire, built on an island in a lake. Today Mexico City stands on the same spot.

The Mexica ruled by force. They conquered neighboring peoples and made them pay tribute. Cloth, corn, feathers, and gold flowed into Tenochtitlán from millions of people. The ruler, Moctezuma, sat at the top of this system. But many conquered peoples hated paying. They were waiting for someone who could help them break free.

The Mexica religion also included human sacrifice. The Spaniards were horrified by it. So were many of the peoples the Mexica had conquered, because captives from those peoples were often the victims. That is one reason so many were ready to join a rebellion.

📚 PART 2 · CORTÉS LANDS, 1519

Finding allies

Cortés sailed from Cuba in early 1519 with about five hundred soldiers and a few horses. They landed on the Gulf coast of Mexico. A young woman named Malintzin joined him there. She spoke the language of the Mexica and the language of the Maya, and she helped him understand and talk with the people he met.

Cortés was not even supposed to be there. The governor of Cuba had canceled his permission to lead the expedition, but Cortés sailed anyway. On the coast, he founded a town called Veracruz and had his men name him their leader. He also had his ships sunk so that nobody could turn back. Cortés had decided that he would win or he would not survive.

As Cortés marched inland, he learned how many peoples resented the Mexica. In Tlaxcala, warriors fought the Spaniards at first. Then their leaders made a choice. They decided that the Mexica were a greater enemy than the strangers, and they became allies. Thousands of warriors from Tlaxcala and other peoples joined the march to the capital. This group of partners was a coalition.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Malintzin (mah-LEEN-tseen). A young woman who joined Cortés on the coast and translated for him.
Tlaxcala (tlahs-KAH-lah). A region and people in central Mexico who were enemies of the Mexica.
📚 PART 3 · THE CITY FALLS

Defeat, disease, and a siege

In November 1519, Cortés entered Tenochtitlán and was welcomed by Moctezuma. Within months, trouble broke out. Moctezuma died, and in June 1520 the Mexica drove the Spaniards out of the city. Many Spaniards died that night. For a while, it looked as though the conquest was over.

Then smallpox arrived. It came with the Spanish in 1520. The people of the Americas had never met this disease, and their bodies had no defense against it. It killed huge numbers of people, including leaders and warriors. Nobody planned this. The Spaniards did not understand disease, and they had not brought it on purpose. But it changed the war.

Cortés came back with his allies. They built boats for the lake and surrounded the city. For months, the coalition cut off the food and the fresh water. In August 1521, Tenochtitlán fell. The last Mexica ruler, Cuauhtémoc, was captured. The Spaniards began to build a new city on the same spot. It became Mexico City, the capital of Spanish rule in the Americas.

The siege lasted about seventy-five days. Disease swept through the city, and food ran out. By the end, many of the people inside were too weak to fight.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Cuauhtémoc (kwow-TEH-mohk). The last Mexica ruler, who led the defense of the city.
📚 PART 4 · WHAT REALLY DECIDED IT

Not a few hundred, but a coalition

It is tempting to say the Spaniards won because they had steel swords and horses. Those helped, but they were not enough. A few hundred Spaniards could not have taken a city that large. They won because tens of thousands of allies fought beside them, because disease weakened the city, and because many peoples were glad to see the Mexica fall.

Some people tell this story as if the Spaniards were unstoppable. That is not what happened. They were nearly destroyed in 1520. They won in 1521 because the choices of many people, Spanish and Native, had lined up in their favor.

Things could have gone differently. If the leaders of Tlaxcala had made another choice, Cortés might have died on the road. Real people made hard choices, and those choices shaped the outcome.

We know much of this story from people who were there. Díaz, a Spanish soldier, wrote a long account of the conquest years later. As you read his words, ask what he saw with his own eyes and what he may have remembered differently after many years.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Díaz (DEE-ahs). Bernal Díaz del Castillo, a Spanish soldier who later wrote an eyewitness account of the conquest.
💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

The conquest of Mexico worked because a large, settled population already paid tribute to rulers, and Spain could take their place. Keep that in mind. The next question is what Spain actually won, and what happened when the same system reached a place that looked very different.
