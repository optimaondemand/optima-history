📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What can cracked bones tell us about a Bronze Age king's own worries?

Far to the east of the Indus Valley, along the Yellow River in northern China, Shang China left behind exactly the kind of written proof of kingship the Indus Valley lacked. Its kings asked questions of their ancestors by cracking heated bones, then had scribes carve the questions, and sometimes the answers, onto the very same bones. This lesson reads those oracle bones directly, meets a warrior queen named Fu Hao, and asks what a distant Bronze Age court's own day-to-day worries can teach us.

Far to the east of the Indus Valley, along the Yellow River in northern China, a very different kind of river valley civilization was taking shape by around 1600 BC. Historians call it the Shang dynasty. It is the first Chinese dynasty confirmed by both digging and its own writing. Chinese tradition remembers an earlier dynasty, the Xia, founded by the legendary flood-taming hero Yu the Great. No confirmed writing or dig evidence for the Xia has yet turned up. Historians treat it, like Gilgamesh's Uruk or the Trojan War, as legendary rather than confirmed history. The Shang dynasty is a different matter entirely. Its kings, its capital cities, and even the names of certain people are recorded in the civilization's own surviving writing. At its height, Shang territory covered much of what is now northern China, organized around a network of walled towns loyal to the king at Anyang.

📚 TALKING TO THE ANCESTORS

Those records take a strange form: oracle bonesOR-uh-kula bone or shell used in Shang China to ask the royal ancestors or gods a question, then carved with the question and answer.. These were the flat shoulder bones of cattle, or the underside of turtle shells, prepared by Shang diviners working for the royal court. A diviner would press a heated point against the bone or shell. This caused it to crack. The diviner then read the pattern of the crack as an answer from the royal ancestors or gods. Kings asked about the harvest, whether to go to war, a coming illness, or the meaning of a dream. Afterward, scribes carved the original question, and sometimes the answer, right onto the same bone. This practice, called divinationdiv-uh-NAY-shuna practice meant to answer questions or predict the future by reading signs, such as cracks in a heated bone., gives modern historians something rare: a huge stash of evidence. Tens of thousands of carved bones survive today. They show a Bronze Age royal court's own day-to-day worries, written in its own hand. Oracle bone writing, dated to around 1250 BC under a king named Wu Dingwoo-DINGa Shang king, ruling around 1250 BC, under whom the earliest confirmed Chinese writing appears., is the first confirmed Chinese writing system. It is the direct ancestor of the Chinese characters still used today, thousands of years later.

For centuries after the Shang fell, no one remembered what oracle bones were. Farmers who dug them up sold them to pharmacies as "dragon bones," to be ground up as medicine. Only in 1899 did a Chinese scholar named Wang Yirong notice strange carved marks on some of these bones. According to the most common telling, he first spotted the marks on "dragon bones" he had bought from a pharmacy to treat his own illness. He realized the marks were real ancient writing, not random scratches. That discovery was made almost by accident. It opened up a whole lost period of Chinese history for the first time. Since then, well over one hundred thousand inscribed oracle bones have been recovered, most of them from Anyang itself.

📚 ANYANG AND A WARRIOR QUEEN

Around this same time, the Shang capital settled at Anyang, known to archaeologists as Yinxu. Large royal tombs have been dug up there. One of the most striking belonged to Fu Haofoo-HOWa wife of King Wu Ding who personally led military campaigns, a rare example of a woman with real military power in the ancient world., a wife of King Wu Ding. Her tomb was found still sealed and unlooted in 1976. It held a huge store of ritual bronze vessels, jade objects, and weapons, more than 1,600 items in all. Oracle bone writings naming Fu Hao describe her leading her own military campaigns for the king. In at least one account, she commanded thousands of troops. This is a rare, well-recorded case of a woman holding real military power in the ancient world. It is worth comparing to Hatshepsut's very different path to power in Egypt two weeks ago.

📚 BRONZE AT A REMARKABLE SCALE

Shang China also made ritual bronze vessels of great skill. Craftsmen cast them using a method called piece-mold casting. A clay model was carved, then cut into separate pieces that formed a mold. This method differed from the lost-wax casting used in the Near East. It could produce some of the largest and most finely carved bronze objects made anywhere in the ancient world. One surviving vessel, the Houmuwu ding, weighs over 1,600 pounds, among the heaviest bronze objects ever cast anywhere in the ancient world. Producing bronze on this scale needed tin and copper. Both metals were hauled in from mines sometimes hundreds of miles away, proof of trade networks reaching well beyond the Shang king's direct land. Shang China's elites also wore fine silk cloth, made from threads unwound from the cocoons of silkworms raised on mulberry leaves, a Chinese skill that stayed mostly unknown outside East Asia for many centuries. These vessels were not everyday tools. They were ritual objects, used to offer food and wine to royal ancestors, whose favor and guidance, as the oracle bones show, the Shang kings sought all the time.

📚 A CIVILIZATION UNTOUCHED BY DISTANT COLLAPSE

One more point is worth holding onto as this course's Quarter 1 draws toward its close. Next week's lessons turn to the palace civilizations of the eastern Mediterranean. Week 9 will describe a wave of crisis and collapse that hits much of the Near East around 1200 to 1150 BC. Shang China, thousands of miles away with no direct contact with that world, is not touched by it at all. Its dynasty continues on for another century beyond that collapse, only ending around 1046 BC, when a rival people called the Zhou overthrew it. Keep this in mind as a useful check against assuming any single historical event, however dramatic, reached the whole world at once.
