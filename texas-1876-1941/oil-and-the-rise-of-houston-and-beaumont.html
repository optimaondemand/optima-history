A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
gusher (GUSH-er): An oil well that shoots oil high into the air before it is brought under control.
refinery (ree-FY-nuh-ree): A factory that turns crude oil into fuels and other products.
salt dome (SAWLT DOHM): An underground hill of salt that pushes up the rock above it, often trapping oil.
wildcatter (WYLD-kat-er): A person who drills for oil in a place where no one has found it before.

📚 PART 1 · A HILL CALLED SPINDLETOP

Hunting for Oil

Texans had known about oil for a long time. It seeped out of the ground in a few places, and people used it as medicine and grease. The first true oil field in Texas came in near Corsicana in 1894, when workers drilling for water struck oil. A man named Joseph Cullinan, who had once worked for Standard Oil, helped develop that field. But Corsicana was small. Most people thought of oil as a minor business.

A man in Beaumont thought differently. Pattillo Higgins was a self-taught geologist who had lost an arm in a boyhood accident. He believed that a low hill south of town, called Spindletop, sat on a salt dome, and that oil lay beneath it. Experts laughed at him. His first drilling attempts failed. Higgins then found a partner, Captain Anthony Lucas, a mining engineer who had worked on salt domes in Louisiana. Lucas needed more money. He found help from bankers in Pittsburgh, including the Mellon family.

Drilling was hard. The ground was soft sand, and it kept caving in. Lucas’s crew had to fix their equipment again and again. Finally, on the morning of January 10, 1901, at about a thousand feet down, drilling mud blew out of the hole. Then oil roared up through the derrick and shot high into the air. It took nine days to bring the gusher under control. It produced roughly 100,000 barrels of oil a day, a stream no one had seen before.

📚 PART 2 · THE BOOM

Beaumont Booms

News spread fast. Within weeks, thousands of people came to Beaumont. They were drillers, investors, gamblers, and shopkeepers. A small city of about ten thousand soon held many times that number. Land near Spindletop sold for fantastic prices. Hundreds of derricks soon covered the hill, so close together that people could walk from one to the next without touching the ground.

Companies sprang up. Joseph Cullinan founded the Texas Company, later called Texaco, and a group backed by the Mellons formed the Gulf Oil Corporation. The oil from Spindletop was thick and not good for lamps. But it burned well as fuel for trains, ships, and factories. That mattered, because cars and engines were spreading across the country. Oil was about to become the fuel of the modern age.

The boom did not last. By 1904, Spindletop was producing much less, because too many wells had drawn down the underground pressure. Some of the early drillers had wasted oil, letting it flow into pits and creeks, and fires broke out. Texans began to ask how this resource should be used. The question of how to control it would shape the state’s politics for decades.

📚 PART 3 · THE WINNER IS HOUSTON

Houston Takes the Lead

Beaumont won the early headlines, but Houston won the long race. Houston had one big problem. It sat inland, about fifty miles from the Gulf, on a shallow stream called Buffalo Bayou. Large ships could not reach it. Business leaders there, including Jesse H. Jones, convinced the federal government to help pay for a deeper channel. After years of digging, the Houston Ship Channel opened in 1914. Ocean ships could now sail right up to the city’s docks.

Oil companies saw their chance. Pipelines carried crude oil from many fields to Houston. Along the channel, companies built refinery after refinery, along with storage tanks. Texaco moved its headquarters to Houston in 1908, and Humble Oil, founded in 1911, grew into one of the nation’s largest producers. Houston became a place where oil was refined and shipped out to the world. Galveston, still rebuilding, could not keep up.

The change shows in the numbers. Houston had about 45,000 people in 1900. By 1930, it had about 292,000, and it had become the largest city in Texas. Other places grew too. Port Arthur, near Beaumont, became a refining center for Gulf Oil and Texaco. Oil money built skyscrapers and hospitals, and drew workers from farms across the South.

Oil shows how supply and demand work. When Spindletop came in, so much oil flowed that the price dropped to a few cents a barrel, lower than the price of a drink of water, as people joked. When the supply dried up and engines needed more fuel, the price rose again. Producers learned to ration what came out of the ground, since a field pumped too fast would lose its pressure and leave oil behind. The same lesson would matter even more in a later oil boom.

Oil also tied Texas to the world. Texas fuel moved to markets across the country and to Europe. Wars and new engines made the demand grow. Oil brought wealth, but it also made Texas depend on prices set far away. Spindletop was luck as much as skill. A gusher could come in on a single day, and a field could run dry in a few years. Pattillo Higgins, who started it all, did not end up the richest man of the boom. People who take the first risk do not always collect the biggest reward.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

One gusher did not make Houston the largest city in Texas. A channel, a railroad, pipelines, and the ambition of its leaders did. When you see a boom, ask what luck started it and what work made it last.
