💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
amendment (uh-MEND-ment): A change or addition to a constitution.
checks and balances (CHEKS and BAL-un-sez): A way of sharing power so that each part of government can limit the others.
federalism (FED-er-uh-liz-um): A system that divides power between a national government and state governments.
limited government (LIM-it-ed GUV-urn-ment): The idea that a government should have only the powers the people give it, and no more.
Reconstruction (ree-kun-STRUK-shun): The years after the Civil War when the nation worked out how to bring the South back into the Union.
Redemption (ree-DEMP-shun): The name Democrats gave to their return to power in the South at the end of Reconstruction.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Texans had just lived under a government they feared. So they wrote a new one that could do very little.
In 1875, Texans chose delegates to write a new state constitution. They did not want a government that could do great things. They wanted one that could not do too much. You will see what they were reacting against, and how they built their answer. It is still the constitution Texas lives under today.

📚 PART 1 · THE REACTION

The Government They Feared

Texas wrote its Constitution of 1869 while the army still helped govern the state. That constitution gave the governor a four-year term and the power to appoint judges and many other officials. It also set up public schools run from the capital in Austin. Governor Edmund J. Davis used these powers. He built a state police force and a larger school system, and taxes rose to pay for them.

Black Texans and many Republicans saw a government that built schools and tried to protect rights. Many white Texans, especially Democrats, saw something else. They saw high taxes, a police force that answered to one man, and power moving away from their own counties and toward Austin. Both views were real, and the second one won at the ballot box.

After Redemption in 1874, the new Democratic leaders wanted the 1869 constitution gone. The timing helped them. A money panic in 1873 had begun a long depression. Many farmers had little cash, and they wanted government to cost less. In 1875, voters chose ninety delegates to a convention in Austin. About seventy-five were Democrats, and only fifteen were Republicans. About forty of the delegates belonged to the Grange, a farmers’ club, so some people called it the “Grange convention.”

Notice that the delegates did not invent everything new. They looked back to Texas’s first state constitution, from 1845, and borrowed from it. They were not trying to design a bold new government. They were trying to build a fence around the one they had just lived under.

📚 PART 2 · THE DESIGN

Three Big Ideas

The delegates worked for about two and a half months. Their finished plan rested on three ideas.

Limited government. The convention believed that power given to officials will be misused sooner or later. So it kept power small. The governor’s term was cut to two years, and the salary was set low. The legislature would meet only every other year, for a short time. The constitution also limited how much the state could tax and how much it could borrow. If officials could not do much, they could not do much harm.

How the two constitutions handled four important questions.

Checks and balances. Like the U.S. Constitution, the Texas Constitution splits power among three branches: one makes the laws, one carries them out, and one decides what they mean. Texas went further. Voters elect the lieutenant governor, the attorney general, the comptroller, the land commissioner, and other officers on their own. The governor cannot fire any of them. So even the executive branch is divided, and no single person runs it. Judges are elected too, so they answer to the voters and not to the governor.

Federalism. Texas had just spent four years fighting the Union, and then it had been governed by the army. The delegates wrote a careful sentence about where Texas stood. The first section says Texas is a “free and independent State, subject only to the Constitution of the United States.” It goes on to say that the Union depends on keeping “local self-government” for the states. Texas accepted its place in the nation. But the delegates wanted it written down that Texans would govern themselves at home.

The constitution also had a Bill of Rights, protecting free speech, trial by jury, and other rights. It protected a family’s homestead from being taken to pay most debts. Voters approved the new constitution in February 1876, about seven votes in ten.

📚 PART 3 · THE COST

Long and Tight, or Short and Open?

Compare the Texas Constitution to the U.S. Constitution. The national one is short. It sets up the main offices, divides their powers, and leaves most of the details to Congress and the courts. The Texas Constitution is much longer. It spells out how much officials may be paid, how long they may serve, how taxes may be raised, and many other details. The U.S. Constitution trusts later leaders to fill in the gaps. The Texas Constitution mostly does not, because the men who wrote it did not trust the leaders they had just lived under.

This had a cost. Because so many details are written into the constitution, a change often needs an amendment, and an amendment needs a vote of the people. Texas voters have approved hundreds of amendments over the years. A document meant to keep government on a short leash has had to be fixed again and again to keep up with a growing state.

There was a darker cost too. The 1876 constitution required separate public schools for white and Black children. Remember that Black Texans had voted and held office under the government the convention was reacting against. As the new plan took hold, they lost ground. The limits the delegates wrote were aimed at the governor, but they did not fall on the governor alone.

Historians ask what Texas would have looked like had the delegates made a different choice. They might have kept the strong governor but added limits. They might have trusted the legislature more. We cannot know. We do know what they chose, and that Texas has lived with the choice for almost 150 years. The question the delegates answered, how much power to trust to government, is one every free people has to answer.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A constitution tells you what its writers feared. As you read the Texas Constitution, ask what each limit was meant to prevent, and who paid for it.
