💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
boomtown (BOOM-town): A town that grows very fast because of a sudden rush of money and workers.
proration (proh-RAY-shun): Dividing a limited amount among many producers, such as setting how much oil each well may pump.
roughneck (RUF-nek): A worker on an oil drilling crew who does the hard, heavy jobs on the rig.
royalty (ROY-ul-tee): A payment to a landowner for oil taken from the land.

📚 PART 1 · A WILDCATTER’S GAMBLE

Dad Joiner’s Well

Columbus Marion Joiner was born in 1860. By 1926, he was an oil wildcatter in his sixties, a man who had made and lost money and was known as “Dad.” Most geologists believed that East Texas had no oil worth drilling for. Joiner disagreed. He leased land from a widow named Daisy Bradford, near Henderson, in Rusk County. He had little money, and he sold shares in his wells to farmers, shopkeepers, and anyone who would invest, often more shares than the wells could support.

His first two wells failed. Drilling the third, called the Daisy Bradford No. 3, took months. Crowds of curious neighbors came out to watch. On September 8, 1930, a test showed oil, and the crowd cheered. On October 3, 1930, oil flowed freely, and the well was a true discovery. Joiner had struck oil after years of failure. A man whose life had been a long string of dry holes had found the first well in what turned out to be an enormous field.

Luck helped him, and so did stubbornness. Two more wells came in nearby that winter, one at Kilgore and one at Longview, farther than anyone had thought oil could be. It became clear that all the wells tapped one huge pool. The East Texas field stretched about forty-five miles long and up to ten miles wide. It was the largest oil field in the lower 48 states, and it would pump oil for decades.

Joiner had little more than stubbornness at first. But his third well, the Daisy Bradford No. 3, proved him right after months of drilling. Evidence is what separates stubbornness from good judgment.
📚 PART 2 · LIFE IN THE OIL PATCH

Boomtown Life

Word spread, and people poured in. Tens of thousands of workers from across the country came for jobs. In the first years of the Depression, many of them had lost their farms or their jobs. Anyone willing to work with their hands could find a place on a drilling crew. These roughnecks lived in tents, shacks, and crowded boardinghouses. Kilgore, a small town of about 700 people, soon held about ten thousand. Tyler, Longview, and Gladewater boomed too.

Kilgore became famous as the home of the “World’s Richest Acre.” Oil lay under the town itself, and drillers put wells in front yards, on sidewalks, and in the middle of the downtown streets. One acre of downtown had about two dozen derricks on it. Oil seeped into the streets, and the air smelled of gas. Fires broke out often. Many people made fortunes. Landowners who had been poor farmers received a royalty, a payment for each barrel pumped from their land. A family could go from poverty to wealth in a year.

Boomtowns also had troubles. Housing, schools, and police could not keep up with the crowds. Gamblers and thieves moved in with the workers. Rules about safety were few, and drilling was dangerous. But the oil patch also built things that lasted. East Texas towns gained new schools, hospitals, and a college. In the middle of the worst economic crisis the country had seen, this part of Texas was growing.

📚 PART 3 · A FLOOD OF OIL

Too Much Oil

The huge field brought a new problem. Thousands of wells were pumping at once, and oil sold for what the market would pay. In 1930, a barrel had sold for about a dollar. As East Texas oil flooded the market, prices crashed. In 1931, oil fell to as low as ten cents a barrel, and in some places even lower. At those prices, nobody could earn a living. Wells pumping too fast also ruined the underground pressure, leaving oil behind that could never be recovered.

The Railroad Commission, which Governor Hogg had helped create, now had the power to regulate oil. It tried to limit how much each well could pump. This was called proration. But many drillers ignored the rules, and the courts sided with them at first. Some pumped “hot oil,” oil taken in violation of the limits. They sold it quietly on the side. In August 1931, Governor Ross Sterling took a dramatic step. He declared martial law in the oil field and sent in the Texas National Guard to shut down wells.

Over the next few years, the state and the national government worked out better controls. In 1935, Congress passed a law to stop the shipping of hot oil between states. The Railroad Commission’s system of proration held. Because Texas was such a huge producer, the commission’s decisions about how much oil to pump affected prices around the world. A state agency that began by regulating trains had become a great power in the oil business.

Joiner did not enjoy the wealth he had found. He had sold overlapping shares in his wells, and investors sued him. An Oklahoma oilman named H. L. Hunt bought out his leases for a large sum. Hunt would become one of the richest men in the country. Joiner’s long years of effort had paid off for others more than for himself. Perseverance brought him to the discovery, but luck and the deals that followed decided who got the money.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

East Texas gave work and wealth to people when the nation had little of either. It also showed that a resource with no limits can ruin itself. When a boom arrives, ask who profits, who pays, and who sets the rules.
