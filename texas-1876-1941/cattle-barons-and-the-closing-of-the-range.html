A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
barbed wire (BARBD WYR): Fence wire with sharp points twisted into it, used to keep cattle in or out.
corporation (kor-puh-RAY-shun): A business owned by many investors, who share its profits and risks.
overgrazing (oh-ver-GRAY-zing): Letting too many animals eat the grass until it cannot grow back.
regulate (REG-yoo-layt): To control something by rules and laws.
wage (WAYJ): Money paid to a worker for work done, usually by the day, week, or month.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
For a few years, one cowboy could ride a hundred miles and never meet a fence. Then a twisted piece of wire changed the plains.
The open range did not last. Within a generation, great ranches were fenced, bought by companies, and sometimes plowed under. You will meet the cattle barons who built huge ranches in Texas, see what barbed wire did, and see how the ranching world changed for the people who worked in it.

📚 PART 1 · THE BARONS

The Cattle Barons

The cattle drives made a few ranchers very rich. People called them cattle barons. Several of them began before the Civil War, and they grew with the markets that came after it.

Richard King started the King Ranch in South Texas in 1853. He was a former steamboat captain. He built it into one of the largest ranches in the world, and it still covers more than 800,000 acres. Charles Goodnight and Oliver Loving drove cattle west and north in 1866 along what became the Goodnight-Loving Trail, to feed soldiers and Native people on reservations in New Mexico, and to find new markets in Colorado. Loving was wounded in a fight with Comanche raiders on the way, and he died. Goodnight carried his friend’s body hundreds of miles home to Weatherford for burial, as he had promised.

John Chisum, who grew up in North Texas, built a herd of tens of thousands along the Pecos River in New Mexico. He is a different man from Jesse Chisholm, for whom the Chisholm Trail was named. W. T. Waggoner built a ranch in North Texas that grew to over half a million acres. In 1911, workers on the Waggoner Ranch drilled for water and found oil instead. C. C. Slaughter ran cattle on the South Plains and became a Dallas banker. These ranchers took risks and worked hard. They also had the good fortune of cheap land and a growing nation that wanted beef.

📚 PART 2 · THE WIRE

The Wire and the Windmill

In 1874, an Illinois farmer named Joseph Glidden patented a new kind of fence: wire twisted with sharp barbs. It was cheap, strong, and easy to build. A story says that in 1876, a salesman fenced a pen of wild Texas longhorns in San Antonio’s Military Plaza to show that the wire could hold them. Whether or not it happened just that way, ranchers soon saw that barbed wire would let them keep cattle on their own land, and keep other herds off it.

Open-range ranchers hated it. Fences cut off trails, and cut off water holes that had been free to everyone. In 1883, in a drought year, angry cowboys and small ranchers began to cut fences across Texas. The trouble spread to dozens of counties. People were hurt and killed. In 1884, the legislature made fence cutting a felony, and it also required fence builders to put in gates. The open range was ending.

Texas historian Walter Prescott Webb, who was born in East Texas in 1888 and taught at the University of Texas, later wrote that three tools made it possible to settle the dry Great Plains: the Colt revolver, barbed wire, and the windmill. Each did a different job. The revolver helped defend against attack, the wire marked ownership, and the windmill gave water. Webb’s idea is a good reminder that machines can change a landscape, though no one tool does it alone.

Fences and wells changed the land itself. Windmills let cattle graze where there had been no water, and herds grew. Many ranchers put more cattle on the range than it could feed. This overgrazing tore up the grass, and mesquite brush spread across ground where grass had been thick. A mid-1880s drought and a fall in cattle prices ruined many ranchers who had borrowed to buy too many animals.

📚 PART 3 · A NEW BUSINESS

Ranching Becomes a Business

Ranching now needed more money than most families had. Land, fences, windmills, and a large herd all cost a lot. So corporations moved in. Investors in Chicago, Scotland, and England put their money into Texas ranches. In 1879, the Matador Ranch began in the Panhandle, and in 1882 it was sold to a Scottish company. The biggest of all was the XIT. The Texas government had no money to pay for a new state capitol, so it traded three million acres of Panhandle land to a Chicago group in exchange for building one. The group turned the land into a ranch that stretched across ten counties.

The cowboy changed too. In the days of the drives, many cowboys hoped to start herds of their own. By the 1880s, ranches were big companies, and a cowboy worked for a wage, usually about $25 to $40 a month. In 1883, dozens of cowboys in the Panhandle went on strike for higher pay. The ranch owners refused, and the strike failed. The cowboy’s life was still hard, but now he worked for someone else.

Government Steps In

Texans began to ask whether companies from far away should own so much of the state. In 1891, Governor James Stephen Hogg signed a law limiting how much land foreign owners could hold. The same year, Texas created the Railroad Commission to regulate what railroads could charge, since ranchers and farmers depended on them. It was the beginning of a regulatory era in Texas.

Meanwhile, settlers kept coming. Railroads sold land to farmers, and new towns grew up. Farmers plowed the grass where ranches had been and planted wheat and cotton. For a time, the rains came and the crops grew. But the grass had held the soil in place for thousands of years. When it was gone and the rains stopped, the wind would take the soil away. Texans of a later generation would pay for that.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

The cowboy of the open range is the one people remember, but the ranch of wire, windmills, and wages was the one that lasted. When a new tool arrives, ask who gains, who loses, and what it does to the land.
