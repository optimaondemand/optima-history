barrier island (BAIR-ee-er EYE-lund): A long, narrow island of sand that lies along a coast and shields the mainland from the sea.
port (PORT): A place on the water where ships load and unload goods and passengers.
seawall (SEE-wawl): A strong wall built along a shore to hold back the sea.
storm surge (STORM SERJ): A rise of sea water pushed onto land by the winds of a storm.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1900, Galveston was the richest city in Texas. By the end of one night, it was a ruin.
Galveston sat on a thin island at the edge of the Gulf, and for sixty years it was the doorway to Texas. Then a storm came ashore. You will see why Galveston rose, what happened on September 8, 1900, and how the city fought to survive.

📚 PART 1 · A CITY ON A SANDBAR

Queen of the Gulf

Galveston lies on a barrier island, a long strip of sand off the Texas coast. The island has a deep natural harbor, which was rare on the shallow Gulf shore. Ships could anchor close to the town. The Republic of Texas made Galveston a major port in the 1830s. Immigrants from Europe arrived there, and many went on to settle in Texas.

Cotton made Galveston rich. Railroads brought bales to the island from farms all over the state. Ships carried them to New England, Britain, and Europe. Merchants built stately offices and warehouses on a street called the Strand. People nicknamed it the “Wall Street of the Southwest.” Banks, insurance firms, and cotton brokers did business there. Galveston also had the first telephones, electric lights, and many other modern things in Texas. In 1880, Galveston was the biggest city in the state, with about 22,000 people, and by 1900 it had about 37,000.

But the island was low. Most of it stood only a few feet above the sea, and at its highest it reached about nine feet. Everyone knew the Gulf could flood it. A smaller storm in 1875 had done much damage. Some people suggested building a seawall. Others said the city could not be badly hurt, and the cost seemed too high. Galveston chose to do nothing, and few expected what would happen.

📚 PART 2 · THE STORM

September 8, 1900

In early September of 1900, a hurricane crossed Cuba and entered the Gulf of Mexico. Weather science then was young. The U.S. Weather Bureau, which did not trust the warnings from Cuban forecasters, expected the storm to turn toward Florida and the Atlantic coast. Isaac Cline, the Weather Bureau’s man in Galveston, had once written that no great storm was likely to harm the island. On September 7, the sea began to rise and a heavy swell rolled in, and Cline warned people along the beach to move to higher ground. Many did not.

By the morning of September 8, a storm surge was pouring over the island. By afternoon, much of the city was under water, and the wind rose to hurricane strength. Houses broke apart and became battering rams that smashed other homes. The surge reached about fifteen feet. Some people climbed to their roofs, and others crowded into big buildings that they thought would stand. At St. Mary’s Orphanage, ten Catholic sisters and ninety of the children in their care died. Cline lost his wife, Cora, in the flood.

When the storm passed, about a third of the city’s buildings were gone. At least 6,000 people were dead, and some estimates are higher, perhaps 8,000 or more. It is still the deadliest natural disaster in the history of the United States. There were so many dead that survivors could not bury them all. At first, crews took bodies out to sea, but they washed back to shore. In the end, the bodies were burned. Cline sent word to Washington on the first boat across the bay, because the telegraph lines were down.

📚 PART 3 · THE REBUILT CITY

Rebuilding

Help came from across the country. Clara Barton brought the Red Cross, and money and supplies arrived by train and ship. But Galveston also had to rebuild itself. The leading citizens decided the old city government could not handle the job. In 1901, they adopted a new plan. A small commission of five people took charge of the city. This commission form of government spread to hundreds of other cities. It was an example of Progressive Era reform, born in a disaster.

Then came the engineering. Between 1902 and 1904, workers built a concrete seawall about three miles long and seventeen feet high. Later builders extended it to about ten miles. The city also decided to raise the island itself. Crews dredged sand from the harbor floor and pumped it into the streets, lifting the ground as much as seventeen feet in places. Thousands of buildings were jacked up on screws while the fill went in beneath them, including a church and houses with families still inside. It was one of the largest modifications of a city’s land ever attempted.

The work paid off. In 1915, another hurricane struck, and the wall and the higher ground held. Far fewer people died than in 1900. But the island city never regained its old place. Insurance companies and banks grew nervous about the risk. Ships were also growing bigger, and Houston was digging a deep channel inland, which let large vessels reach it directly. Meanwhile, oil in southeast Texas was about to bring new wealth, and most of it passed Galveston by.

Galveston did not die. It remained a port, a college town, and a tourist city, and many of its old buildings still stand. But the story shows how a single event can change a city’s whole future. Historians do not say Galveston was doomed. If the storm had struck somewhere else, or the warning had been believed, the story might have gone differently.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Galveston was the best-run, richest city in Texas, and it still lost to the sea. Its people responded with courage and hard work and new ideas. When disaster strikes, ask who was warned, who listened, and what a community owes to the next generation.
