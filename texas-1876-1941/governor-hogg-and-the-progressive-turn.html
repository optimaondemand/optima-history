Progressive (pruh-GRES-iv): A reformer of the early 1900s who wanted government to fix problems caused by big business and fast change.
regulation (reg-yoo-LAY-shun): A rule made by government to control how something is done.

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A big man from East Texas told voters he would make the railroads obey the law. Then he did it, and other states copied him.
The Populists lost most elections, but their complaints were heard. A Democrat named James Hogg turned many of those complaints into law. You will see how he built the Railroad Commission, how the Progressive reformers followed, and how Texas women won the right to vote.

📚 PART 1 · A REFORMER IN AUSTIN

Big Jim Hogg

James Stephen Hogg was born in East Texas in 1851. His father was a Confederate general who died in the war when Hogg was a boy. Hogg learned the printing trade, ran a small newspaper, and studied law. He grew very tall and heavy, and people called him “Big Jim.” In 1887, Texans elected him attorney general, the state’s chief lawyer.

Texas railroads were then among the most powerful businesses in the state. Farmers and ranchers complained that railroads charged too much, favored some customers, and sold stock that was worth less than they claimed. As attorney general, Hogg took railroads to court for breaking Texas law. He won several cases, and he became a hero to many farmers.

In 1890, he ran for governor. He promised voters a commission to oversee railroads, and he won. Texas voters also approved a change to the state constitution so the legislature could create it. Hogg took office in January 1891, and by April the legislature had passed the law.

📚 PART 2 · REGULATION

The Railroad Commission

The Railroad Commission of Texas had three members. They set the rates that railroads could charge to haul freight in Texas. They could stop a railroad from charging one shipper less than another. The first leader of the commission was John H. Reagan. He had been a Confederate cabinet officer, and he left the United States Senate to take the job. His long public record gave the new agency credibility.

Railroads fought back. They went to court and argued that Texas could not control them. A federal court at first stopped some of the rules. But the commission and Hogg held on, and the idea spread. Other states set up similar groups, and in time Congress did too. This was one kind of regulation. Hogg also supported laws that limited how much debt a railroad could take on, and a law to keep foreign companies from buying too much Texas land.

Hogg also took on the way railroads sold stock. Some companies had issued shares and bonds worth far more than their tracks and trains, a trick called “watered stock.” Investors and customers paid the price when such companies failed. In 1893, Texas passed the Stock and Bond Law, which said that a railroad could not issue stocks and bonds worth more than the real value of its property. Reformers across the country watched what Texas did. To Hogg, the point was simple. Businesses that served the public, and depended on public land and public law, ought to answer to the public.

The Railroad Commission outlived Hogg by a long time. In the early 1900s, as oil became a Texas business, the legislature gave the commission power over oil pipelines. Later, in the oil boom of the 1930s, it would become one of the most important oil agencies in the world. Hogg left office in 1895. He went into business himself, and after Spindletop he made money in oil. His daughter Ima Hogg became one of the great supporters of Texas art and music.

Hogg stands between two movements. He was a Democrat, not a Populist. Yet he listened to Populist complaints, and he made them into laws. Later reformers called Progressives used the same ideas to attack other problems. Some were about government, such as reform of voting, and some were about the problems of growing cities. In 1905 the legislature passed a law that required parties to hold primary elections to pick their candidates. It was meant to give voters more say.

📚 PART 3 · SUFFRAGE

Women Win the Vote

Women’s suffrage, the right to vote, was one of the great reform battles. Texas women began organizing in the 1890s. In 1893, they formed the Texas Equal Rights Association in Galveston. But the movement was small for years, and the legislature ignored it.

In the 1910s, it took on new life. Minnie Fisher Cunningham became president of the Texas Equal Suffrage Association in 1915. Jane McCallum of Austin was one of the organization’s leading voices. She wrote newspaper articles, gave speeches, and helped plan the lobbying at the Capitol. Women wrote letters, held rallies, and went door to door. They also argued that women would use the vote to clean up politics, which appealed to many Progressives.

A political fight in Austin helped them. In 1917, the legislature impeached Governor James Ferguson, who was accused of misusing public money. The new governor, William P. Hobby, owed much to suffrage supporters, and he backed their cause. In March 1918, Texas women won the right to vote in primary elections. In Texas in those years, the Democratic primary was the real contest, so this mattered a great deal. About 386,000 women registered in just seventeen days.

The next year, Congress approved a national amendment, the Nineteenth, giving women the vote everywhere. On June 28, 1919, Texas became the first state in the South, and the ninth in the nation, to ratify it. Jane McCallum later served as Texas Secretary of State, from 1927 to 1933.

The story has a hard side, too. Many white suffrage leaders did not work with Black women, and some accepted the customs of the segregated South. The poll tax, which Texas began requiring in 1902, and the white primary kept many Black and Mexican American voters from the ballot, women and men alike. A law on paper did not always become a vote in practice. That fight would last for decades.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Hogg took a farmers’ anger and turned it into law. The women who won the vote took years of work and a lucky political moment. When a reform succeeds, ask who built it, who gained from it, and who still waited.
