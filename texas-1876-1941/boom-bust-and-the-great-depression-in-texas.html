📚 PART 1 · BOOMS THAT ENDED

A Pattern of Boom and Bust

Texas had seen booms before. Cattle prices soared in the 1870s and 1880s, then crashed in the drought of 1886. Cotton prices soared during World War I, when Europe needed Texas cotton. In 1920, as the war boom ended, they fell sharply. Oil fields gushed in 1901 and again in 1930, and each time prices collapsed because too much oil came out of the ground.

Why did Texas have this pattern? Many of its leading products were raw materials, such as cotton, cattle, and crude oil. They were sold in big markets where prices were set far away. When the world wanted more, Texans got rich. When it wanted less, they lost. Texas had few factories making finished goods, so it had little else to fall back on. This made the state more open to sudden swings than states with more kinds of business.

📚 PART 2 · THE DEPRESSION ARRIVES

The Crash of 1929

In October 1929, the New York stock market crashed. Prices of shares fell by large amounts in a few days. Banks that had lent money to stock buyers began to fail. In the months that followed, factories closed and workers lost their jobs. By 1932, about one American worker in four had no job. This long period of hard times is called the Great Depression.

Farmers felt it first. In 1929, a pound of cotton sold for about seventeen cents. By 1931, it sold for less than six cents, which was below what it cost to grow it. Many farmers could not pay their debts, and they lost their land. Tenant farmers had it worst. Landowners sent families away or cut what they were paid, and thousands of families left the land with everything they owned loaded on a truck or wagon. Some went to town to find work, and some went on the road.

Then came the drought. In the early and middle 1930s, rain stopped falling on the High Plains of the Texas Panhandle. Farmers there had plowed up the grass that held the soil, and the wind now picked up the dry dirt. Great dust storms rolled across the land. The worst came on April 14, 1935, called Black Sunday, when a black wall of dust turned day into night. The region became part of the Dust Bowl. Dust filled houses, killed crops, and made children and livestock sick. Many families gave up and left.

Many families who left the land headed west. Thousands of Texans joined the long stream of Dust Bowl migrants to California, where they hoped to find work in the fields and orchards. They often found low pay, crowded camps, and a cold welcome. Newcomers from the Southern Plains were all called “Okies” by Californians, no matter what state they came from. Others stayed near home and took what work they could find, picking cotton for pennies or doing odd jobs in town.

Oil workers and city people suffered too. When oil prices fell, drilling stopped and crews were laid off. Banks in every part of Texas closed their doors. In cities such as Houston, Dallas, and San Antonio, unemployment was high. Some people stood in lines for food, and some lived in shacks of scrap lumber. East Texas was an odd exception. Because of the oil boom there, jobs and wages held up better for a time, though the oil crash soon hit it too.

📚 PART 3 · HELP AND HARDSHIP

Relief

At first, most help came from families, churches, and charities. Neighbors shared what they had. Cities set up soup kitchens, and local governments gave out food and clothing. But the need was too great. In Texas, the state government had little power or money. The constitution of 1876 limited what the state could spend and borrow, and Texans were not used to looking to government for help.

Texas leaders took different paths. Miriam “Ma” Ferguson, the first woman to serve as governor of Texas, held office a second time from 1933 to 1935. She supported some federal aid. The next governor, James Allred, worked closely with President Franklin Roosevelt’s New Deal. Under the New Deal, the federal government created programs that gave out money and created jobs. The Civilian Conservation Corps put young men to work building roads, bridges, and state parks, such as those at Bastrop and Garner.

Not everyone shared equally in the help. Mexican American Texans were often treated unfairly. In the early 1930s, local officials in Texas and other states pressured many Mexican families to leave the country. Some of them were United States citizens. Black Texans were often the last hired and the first fired. Women and children worked for very low pay. The Depression was hard on almost everyone, but it was hardest on those with the least power.

Texans had a reputation for self-reliance, and many people were proud and slow to ask for help. But hunger and loss showed that some troubles are too large for any one family or town. People endured with patience and a good deal of mutual aid. By the late 1930s, jobs were returning, though full recovery came only with the war that followed.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A boom feels like it will last forever, and a bust feels the same way. Texans got through the Depression by patience and by helping one another. When prices fall and jobs vanish, ask who bears the loss, and what a community owes the people who have the least.
