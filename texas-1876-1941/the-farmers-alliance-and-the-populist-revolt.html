💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
cooperative (koh-OP-uh-ruh-tiv): A group of people who join together to buy, sell, or work as one, and share the gains.
crop lien (KROP LEEN): A claim a merchant held on a farmer's next crop in return for goods bought on credit.
platform (PLAT-form): A list of the goals and promises of a political group.
Populist (POP-yuh-list): A member of the People's Party, a political party of the 1890s that spoke for farmers and workers.
tenant farmer (TEN-unt FAR-mer): A farmer who rents land from an owner instead of owning it.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A farmer could grow a bumper crop of cotton and still end the year owing more than he made. In the 1880s, thousands of Texans decided that was not just bad luck.
As the cattle range closed, farming spread across Texas. Many farmers found themselves stuck in debt. You will see how they organized in the Farmers’ Alliance, why Lampasas County matters to the whole country, and how the Populist revolt rose and then faded.

📚 PART 1 · COTTON AND DEBT

From Cattle to Cotton

When the open range closed, the empty land did not stay empty. Railroads reached into new parts of Texas. They sold land to families who came to farm. Barbed wire fenced the fields, and better plows broke the prairie sod. Cotton was the main crop. It sold for cash, and merchants and railroads knew how to ship it. By the 1890s, Texas grew more cotton than any other state.

But a cotton farmer needed money months before the crop came in. Seed, tools, flour, and cloth all had to be bought in spring. Most farmers had no savings, so they turned to the local merchant. The merchant sold them goods on credit, at high prices, with high interest. In return, the merchant took a crop lien. That meant the merchant had a legal claim on the farmer’s coming crop. At harvest, the merchant took his share first.

Then came falling prices. Around 1880, cotton sold for about ten cents a pound. By the mid-1890s, it brought less than six cents. A farmer who grew more cotton to pay his debts only pushed prices lower. Many farmers lost their land and became tenant farmers, paying rent to owners. By 1900, about half of all Texas farmers were tenants. They had worked hard, and many felt the whole system was set against them.

📚 PART 2 · FARMERS ORGANIZE

The Alliance

In 1877, farmers in Lampasas County, in the Texas Hill Country, formed a club to help one another. They called it the Farmers’ Alliance. At first it was small. But it spread quickly, because every farming county had the same troubles. By the late 1880s, it had hundreds of thousands of members across Texas and other states. Because it began in Lampasas, Texas holds a real place in the story of the nation’s farm protest.

The Alliance tried to fix the problem by working together. Members started a cooperative. They bought supplies in large amounts to cut the cost, and they sold their crops together to get better prices. Charles Macune, a leader of the Alliance, started the Texas Exchange in 1887. It was a good idea, but it ran short of money, and bankers would not lend to it. Macune then proposed a plan in which the federal government would store farmers’ crops in warehouses and lend them money against the crops. That would let them wait for better prices. Congress never passed it.

In 1886, Alliance delegates met in Cleburne and wrote a list of demands. They asked for the government to regulate the railroads, to limit the land held by large companies and speculators, and to put more money into circulation. Money was scarce in those years, which kept prices low. The demands show how the farmers saw it. They believed that railroads, banks, and merchants had more power than the people who grew the food.

Black farmers organized too. The Colored Farmers’ Alliance began in Houston County, Texas, in 1886, and it grew to over a million members in many states. It was a separate group, because white and Black farmers did not usually belong to the same club. They shared the same debts and some of the same goals, yet white and Black farmers were often kept apart by the customs of the time.

Workers had their own protests. The Knights of Labor, a national union, was strong in Texas in the 1880s. In 1886, thousands of railroad workers struck against lines owned by Jay Gould. The strike spread across Texas and neighboring states, and it failed. Gould’s railroads held on, and the union lost much of its strength. Farmers and workers both learned how hard it was to win against big companies.

📚 PART 3 · A PARTY OF THEIR OWN

The Populist Party

Farmers decided to enter politics. In 1891 and 1892, Alliance members and others formed the People’s Party, whose members were called Populists. Their national platform, written in Omaha in 1892, called for government ownership of railroads, an income tax, and an end to the old money system. In Texas, the Populist candidate for governor in 1892, Thomas Nugent, won about one vote in four. That was a strong showing for a new party in a state where almost everyone voted Democratic.

The Democrats fought back hard. Many Democrats said that dividing white voters would let Black voters decide elections. Some Populists tried to win Black votes, and the two parties accused each other of fraud. Meanwhile, Texas Democrats such as Governor James Hogg took up some Populist ideas, such as regulating railroads. That took away some of the Populists’ strength.

The big test came in 1896. The Democrats chose William Jennings Bryan of Nebraska as their candidate for president. Bryan gave a famous speech that said the country was crucifying working people on a “cross of gold.” He wanted more silver money, which would raise prices. Most Texas Populists decided to back him. Bryan won Texas by a wide margin, but he lost the nation to William McKinley. Without a candidate of their own, the Populists faded. By 1900, the party was almost gone.

Populism lost at the polls, but its ideas did not vanish. Railroad regulation, the income tax, and the direct election of senators all became law in later years. Historians still debate how much the Populists changed the country, and how much they were a flash of anger over hard times. In Texas, the story leaves behind a lesson about how ordinary people can organize when they feel the rules favor others.
