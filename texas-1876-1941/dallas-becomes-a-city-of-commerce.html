commerce (KOM-ers): The buying and selling of goods and services, especially on a large scale.
diversify (dy-VER-suh-fy): To spread out into many different kinds of business instead of only one.
finance (FY-nans): The work of managing money, such as banking, lending, and investing.
insurance (in-SHUR-unts): An arrangement in which people pay a fee so that money is paid to them if something bad happens.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Dallas had no seaport, no great river, and no oil under its streets. By 1940, it was the money center of the Southwest.
Dallas is not near the coast or the oil fields. It grew because of where its railroads crossed and because of what its leaders did with that. You will see how Dallas became a city of commerce, how East Texas oil built it up, and why it kept growing after the oil boom.

📚 PART 1 · THE CROSSROADS

A City With No River

John Neely Bryan founded Dallas in 1841, at a spot on the Trinity River where travelers could cross. The Trinity is too shallow for large boats, so Dallas never became a port. For thirty years it stayed a small town. Then came the railroad.

In 1872, the Houston and Texas Central Railroad reached Dallas. The next year, the Texas and Pacific Railroad crossed it east to west. That made the town a crossroads, with tracks running in four directions. Farmers sent their cotton to Dallas by wagon and train. Merchants set up warehouses, and soon Dallas was one of the biggest inland cotton markets in the nation. Leather goods, saddles, and farm machinery were made and sold there too.

Where trade goes, money follows. Dallas built banks to lend to farmers and merchants, and companies grew to sell insurance against fire, storms, and other losses. By 1900, Dallas had about 42,000 people, more than twice what it had a decade before. Its leaders worked hard to advertise the city. In 1914, they won a major prize. The federal government chose Dallas as the home of one of the twelve Federal Reserve Banks, the banks that oversee the nation’s money. Dallas had beaten out other Texas cities, including Houston.

📚 PART 2 · OIL AND BANKS

Oil Money Moves In

Dallas had no oil wells, but it was close to the fields. Oilmen needed money to drill, and they needed lawyers, bankers, and offices. Dallas had all of these. After the discovery in East Texas, Dallas became the place where oil deals were signed. Oil companies opened offices there. In 1922, the Magnolia Petroleum Company finished a skyscraper downtown. A big red neon horse, a Pegasus, turned on its roof, and it became the symbol of the city.

H. L. Hunt, the Oklahoma oilman who bought out Dad Joiner’s leases, moved his company to Dallas in the 1930s. Other wildcatters followed. When a well came in, the money often passed through a Dallas bank. Dallas banks lent money for drilling, held the cash from oil sales, and sold shares in oil companies to investors. In the Depression years, when banks failed all across Texas, Dallas banks kept growing. Oil money gave them an unusual cushion.

Dallas used that money in clever ways. In 1930, two scientists started a company in Dallas called Geophysical Service. It used sound waves to find oil underground. Many years later, the same firm turned into Texas Instruments. Dallas also became known for fashion. Neiman Marcus, a store opened in 1907, drew wealthy shoppers from all over the country. During World War II, aircraft factories would come as well. The city was not tied to just one product.

📚 PART 3 · A CITY OF COMMERCE

A National City

In 1936, Texas celebrated the hundredth anniversary of its independence. Cities competed to host the main celebration, the Texas Centennial Exposition. Dallas won. Its leaders promised to put up large sums of money, and Dallas had more banks and businesses to raise it from than any other Texas city. Millions of people visited Fair Park, where new buildings, museums, and shows opened. Federal money from the New Deal helped pay for it. The fair showed off the city to the whole country.

This was a sign of where Dallas stood. By 1930, it had grown from about 42,000 people to over 250,000. Its great strength was that it did not depend on a single thing. Cotton, oil, banking, insurance, retail stores, and manufacturing all grew at once. When one part had a bad year, the others could carry the city. To diversify is to spread out like this. It is one reason Dallas survived the Depression better than cities that relied on one crop or one industry.

Dallas also looked to the sky. Love Field began as an Army flying school in 1917. In the 1920s it became a commercial airport, and Dallas was soon on the first airline routes across the country. A city that had won its start from railroads was now planning for the next kind of travel. Businesses that wanted to reach Texas customers often chose Dallas as their regional office, since a salesman could get there from almost anywhere. Each new office brought more workers, who needed houses, schools, and stores, and those in turn gave work to still more people.

Houston and Dallas grew in different ways. Houston was the city of oil, ships, and refineries. Dallas was the city of finance, selling, and deal making. People in Dallas were proud of their city, and often said they had built it with no natural advantages. Some historians point out that Dallas had a great one: its location on the main rail lines. Dallas leaders also sold their city hard, and did not wait for luck to come to them.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Dallas did not have a port or an oil field. It had railroads, banks, and people willing to work for the future. When you study a city, ask what it was given, what it built, and what happens when its leaders plan ahead.
