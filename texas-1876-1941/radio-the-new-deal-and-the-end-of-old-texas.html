💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
federalism (FED-er-ul-iz-um): A system in which power is divided between a national government and state governments.
mass culture (MAS KUL-cher): Songs, shows, and news that millions of people in many places hear or see at the same time.
New Deal (NOO DEEL): President Franklin Roosevelt's set of programs in the 1930s to fight the Depression and help the economy recover.
rural (RUR-ul): Having to do with the countryside, away from cities and towns.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
In 1930, a farm family in Texas might hear news from a neighbor or the weekly paper. By 1940, they heard a president, a band from Nashville, and a baseball game from New York in the same room.
Two forces changed Texas in the 1930s. One was the radio. The other was Washington. You will see how radio made a shared national culture, how the New Deal changed the way Texans were governed, and what that did to a state that had always seen itself as a place apart.

📚 PART 1 · THE RADIO AGE

The Radio Age

Radio came to Texas in the early 1920s. Stations in Fort Worth and Dallas, such as WBAP and WFAA, went on the air in 1922. At first, they played local music and read local news. Within a few years, national networks linked stations together. A show made in New York could be heard in a farmhouse near Abilene on the same night. By the 1930s, nearly every Texas town had radios, and families gathered around them at night.

This was a new kind of mass culture. For the first time, millions of people in many places heard the same songs, comedy shows, ball games, and news at the same moment. A Texan and a New Yorker laughed at the same joke. Radio also carried President Roosevelt’s “fireside chats,” in which he spoke to the nation in a calm voice about the troubles of the day. Many people felt that the president was talking directly to them.

Texans also sent their own culture out on the air. Bob Wills played with a band called the Light Crust Doughboys on Fort Worth radio in the early 1930s. He later led his own band, the Texas Playboys. Wills blended country, jazz, and Mexican music into a style called Western swing. The Doughboys took their name from a flour company. The company’s manager, W. Lee O’Daniel, hosted the show and discovered how well radio could win an audience. He used that skill to run for governor in 1938, with a hillbilly band and a promise to protect the Ten Commandments. He won, and later became a United States senator.

Radio also reached across the border. Some stations just inside Mexico, such as XERA at Ciudad Acuña, used far stronger transmitters than American law allowed. Their signals carried across much of North America at night. They played country and Western music by Texas and Southern bands and sold goods by mail. Radio also changed how candidates won votes. Instead of speaking in town squares, a candidate could speak directly to thousands of families at once, as O’Daniel showed.

📚 PART 2 · WASHINGTON ARRIVES

The New Deal Comes to Texas

The Great Depression pushed the federal government to do far more than before. Congress passed many new laws in the New Deal. Some programs helped farmers by paying them to grow less cotton, so that prices would rise. Others, like the Civilian Conservation Corps and the Works Progress Administration, gave people jobs. They built roads, schools, post offices, parks, and bridges all over Texas. The Social Security Act of 1935 created pensions for older Americans.

One program changed life on the farm. In the 1930s, most rural Texas homes had no electricity, because private power companies did not think it paid to string wires across thin country. In 1935, the Rural Electrification Administration began lending money to local cooperatives to build lines. A young Texan named Lyndon Johnson, who headed the National Youth Administration in the state, worked to help Texans. After he was elected to Congress in 1937, he helped bring electric lines to the Hill Country. Lights, radios, and refrigerators arrived on farms that had gone without.

These programs changed the balance of federalism. In 1876, Texans had written a constitution that kept their state government small and weak. Taxes and spending were tightly limited. The New Deal went around those limits. Washington could spend money where the state could not or would not. Texas officials like Governor James Allred took federal money and helped carry out the programs. Many Texans in Congress, including Vice President John Nance Garner of Uvalde, were powerful in Washington. But by 1940, Garner and other conservative Texans had broken with Roosevelt over his growing power.

📚 PART 3 · A SMALLER GAP

What Began to Close

Before the 1930s, Texans were proud that their state had once been a nation of its own. They were proud of their own history, their own laws, and their own ways. Many took pride in being hard to push around, and in solving their problems without help. Radio and the New Deal began to wear away at some of that. Texans now listened to the same programs and relied on the same federal programs as people in Georgia and Ohio.

This was not a sudden change, and not everyone welcomed it. Some Texans argued that the New Deal made people depend too much on Washington. Others saw the benefits and said Texas needed the help. Older ideas of independence did not vanish, and they would return in later politics. But the change was real. People who had once looked to the state capital for law and the local church for help now looked to Washington and the national networks.

Historians do not see a straight path here. The pull toward one national culture was strong, but Texans made choices along the way. Many of them were glad to take electric power and jobs. The younger generation, raised with radio, came to see themselves as both Texans and Americans. By 1940, Texas was still distinct, yet it was less apart from the nation than it had ever been. The next war would carry the change much further.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A new kind of tool, a new kind of government, and one crisis changed how Texans lived. When something new arrives, ask what it gives, what it takes, and which of the old ways people choose to keep.
