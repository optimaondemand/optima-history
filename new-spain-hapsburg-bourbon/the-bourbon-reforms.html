📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
A war over who would be king of Spain changed how Spain ruled half the world.
In 1700, the king of Spain died without a child to take his place. That sparked a war across Europe, and when it ended, a new royal family sat on the Spanish throne. The new family had new ideas about how to rule. They wanted a stronger government, more tax money, and tighter control of the colonies. Those changes are called the Bourbon Reforms, and they reshaped New Spain.

📚 PART 1 · A NEW ROYAL FAMILY

A war about a crown

For almost two hundred years, Spain had been ruled by one dynasty, the Hapsburgs. In 1700, the last Hapsburg king died with no child. Two families claimed the throne. One was the French Bourbon family, and the other was an Austrian branch of the Hapsburgs. Europe fought over the answer in the War of the Spanish Succession, which lasted from 1701 to 1714.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Bourbon (BOOR-bun). A French royal family. A Bourbon prince became king of Spain in 1700.

The war ended with a peace treaty signed at Utrecht in 1713. A Bourbon, Philip V, became king of Spain. In return, Spain gave up its lands in Italy and the Netherlands. It was left with Spain itself and a huge empire in the Americas. That empire was now the most valuable thing the new dynasty had.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Utrecht (YOO-trekt). A city in the Netherlands, where the peace treaty of 1713 was signed.

Notice how a fight about a family changed an empire. Nobody in the Americas had any say in who sat on the throne. Yet the winner would decide how they were taxed, who governed them, and what their children could hope to become. A quarrel in faraway Europe shaped what the people of San Antonio could expect from their government.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT THE REFORMS WERE

More control, more money

The Bourbon kings were influenced by the Enlightenment. They believed that a king should rule by reason and that a government should be well organized, efficient, and strong. They looked at the Spanish empire and saw a system that was slow, weak, and full of people who kept too much of the money. They set out to change it with a series of reforms.

The Enlightenment gave these kings a way to speak about what they wanted. If reason said the government should be efficient, then anything that slowed the government was a problem. Old customs, local privileges, and Church lands could all be called a waste. The kings did not see themselves as unkind. They believed they were making the empire work better. But a plan that looks reasonable in Madrid can feel harsh in a village far away.

The reforms had four main goals. The first was to centralize power in the hands of the king and his officials. The second was to collect more taxes. The third was to build a professional army and a better colonial government. The fourth was to limit the power of the Church. In 1767, the king expelled the Jesuits from the Spanish empire. The Jesuits had run many schools and missions in the Americas.

Each goal sounds sensible on its own, but together they changed who held power. Before, many decisions were made slowly, by local people who knew the place. After, more decisions were made by officials who answered to the king. A town could no longer count on friends in the local council to stand up for it. That was a big change for people who had been used to a good deal of independence.

The new officials were often sent from Spain. In New Spain, a new type of governor called an intendant was introduced in the 1780s to supervise a province and collect taxes. The changes worked, at least in money. Silver production and tax income rose sharply.

A professional army was also costly. The Crown had to pay soldiers, buy weapons, and build forts. It raised taxes to cover these costs, and the people who paid the taxes had little say in how the money was used. People notice when a government asks for more and gives them less of a voice in return.

📚 PART 3 · RESENTMENT

Left out of power

But the reforms made many people angry. The most important group was the person of Spanish descent born in the Americas (criollo). Before the reforms, criollos had held many local offices. Under the Bourbons, most of the highest posts went to officials born in Spain. A criollo who had grown up in the Americas, whose family had lived there for generations, could find himself passed over for a job by a newcomer.

Taxes were another source of anger. Merchants and landowners paid more than they ever had. The Church was angry about its lost privileges. Each group had a different complaint, and none of them was sure it could trust the Crown.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
person of Spanish descent born in the Americas (criollo) (kree-OH-yoh). A person of Spanish descent who was born in the Americas, not in Spain.

It helps to remember that these were not poor people. Many criollos owned land, ran businesses, or held positions in the Church. They were educated and proud. What hurt was not hunger but insult. They felt that a person born across the ocean was valued more than they were, though their families had built the colony. Being treated as lesser in your own home is hard to forgive.

📚 PART 4 · ON THE FRONTIER

A smaller, cheaper defense

On the northern frontier, the reforms meant doing more with less. The inspection by Rubí in the 1760s was part of them. In 1776, the king created a new government for the northern provinces, to make defense more efficient. Texas was part of it.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Rubí (roo-BEE). The Marqués de Rubí, a Spanish officer who inspected the northern frontier from 1766 to 1768.

For Texas, doing more with less meant fewer soldiers, fewer forts, and fewer priests. Officials in the 1700s wrote long reports asking how to save money. Their answer was usually to pull back and to defend only what mattered most. That made sense on a budget sheet. It also meant that the people left in the province had to protect themselves.

In 1793, the missions around San Antonio were closed as missions, and their lands were given to settlers and the Church. This was the end of the plan to turn mission Indians into Spanish farmers. By then, Spanish Texas was smaller and weaker than it had been in 1750. East Texas had been abandoned, and the Comanche raided the settlements.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Comanche (kuh-MAN-chee). A people who became the strongest power on the southern plains in the 1700s.

A mission that closed did not simply vanish. The buildings stayed, and many of the people who had lived in them stayed too. Their land was divided among families, and some mission residents became part of the growing Spanish-speaking town. In this way the old mission system faded into the ordinary life of San Antonio. The old walls of the missions are still there to see.

So the reforms strengthened the empire in one way and weakened it in another. The Crown took in more money, but it lost the loyalty of many of the people it ruled. That would matter very soon.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

The Bourbon Reforms were an attempt to make a huge empire work like a well-run machine. They raised money, but they also pushed away the people born in the Americas, who had the most to lose. When a crisis came, those people would not be on the Crown's side.
