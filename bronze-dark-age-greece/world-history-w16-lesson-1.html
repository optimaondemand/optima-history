📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What happens to a civilization that loses its own writing system?

This course left Greece in ruins back in Week 9. The great Mycenaean palace centers, Pylos, Mycenae, Tiryns, and the rest, had burned or emptied out during the Bronze Age Collapse. Their wanax-led palace economies vanished right along with them. What follows is one of this course's clearest examples of a civilization that did not bounce back quickly. In some ways, it did not fully bounce back for a very long time at all. This week's story matters for a reason beyond Greece itself. It is a direct test of this course's own way of reading history. Some civilizations recover fast. Others take centuries. A few never fully recover the way they once were. Greece belongs, for a long stretch, to that last group.

📚 A REAL DECLINE, NOT JUST A PAUSE

Historians call the roughly three and a half centuries that follow, from about 1150 to 800 BC, the Dark Agea long stretch of time in which population, trade, building, and writing all sharply decline.. The name is earned, not stretched. Population fell hard and stayed low. Fewer people meant fewer hands to farm, fewer soldiers to defend a village, and fewer people left to remember how things used to work. The great palace centers were not just damaged. They were left empty for good, never rebuilt on anything close to their old scale. Long-distance trade across the Mediterranean, which the Mycenaeans had once carried out all the time, slowed to almost nothing. Big building projects, of the kind this course studied at Mycenae's huge Cyclopean walls in Week 8, simply stopped happening. In other words, nearly every sign of a rich, organized society disappeared from Greece at almost the same time.

Most striking of all, Linear B, the palace record-keeping script this course met in Week 8, vanished completely. It had never been a widely taught skill to begin with. Only a few specially trained scribes working for the palace governments knew it. When those palaces disappeared, the scribes and their training disappeared right along with them. Greece became, in a very real sense, unable to read or write for roughly the next three centuries. This point is worth stating plainly, because it goes directly against any idea that history only ever moves forward. A society can lose a skill as basic as writing itself. It can then simply go without that skill again for generations. That is exactly what happened here. No new script rose to replace the old one, not for thin generations at a stretch. Writing simply stopped, and no one wrote again until it was slowly picked back up centuries later, in Week 16's own closing story.

📚 ONE THREAD THAT DID NOT BREAK

Not everything about this period was pure loss, though. As the bronze trade collapsed along with the wider Mediterranean network this course has followed since Phoenicia and Egypt's Bronze Age contacts, a different metal filled the gap. Ironworking, already given its full spotlight with the Hittites back in Week 8, spread widely across Greece during this same poor, hard period. Unlike tin, iron ore could be found close to home in many parts of Greece. It did not need the kind of long-distance trade network that had just fallen apart. A poorer, smaller, less literate Greece nonetheless became, quietly, a Greece that increasingly worked iron. Decline and new technology were happening at the very same time, in the very same struggling villages. This is a real complication worth holding onto, rather than squeezing it into one simple story. In other words, Greece was losing old skills and gaining a new one at the exact same time. It would be too simple to call this whole age nothing but a fall. It was a fall in most ways, and a quiet, slow gain in at least one other way, both at once.

📚 WHAT SURVIVES WHEN THE RECORD DOES NOT

Here is the real puzzle this Dark Age leaves behind. This course has grown used to studying civilizations through their own written words: Hammurabi's Code, Piye's stele, Ashurbanipal's library tablets. Dark Age Greece left behind almost none of that kind of record. Its people were mostly farmers and herders scattered in small villages. They had little wealth, no palace government, and no writing system in real use at all. Archaeologists can still dig up their pots, their graves, and their small houses. None of those objects, though, can speak in a person's own words the way a written record can.

And yet the memory of the vanished Mycenaean world did not vanish along with the palaces. It survived instead in a form this course has not studied closely yet: oral traditionOR-ul truh-DISH-unstories, family lines, and songs passed down by memory and speech, without being written down.. These were stories, family lines, and songs passed by memory from one generation of storytellers to the next. No written text anchored them down, for three centuries or more, before anyone thought to write them down at all. A memory passed by mouth for that many generations does not stay perfectly fixed. Some details get lost. Some get changed without anyone meaning to change them. Some get added later, because they made a good story better. What such a memory can keep safe, and what it inevitably changes, adds, or loses along the way, is exactly the problem Lesson 2 turns to directly. It does so through the two poems that eventually grew out of this very tradition.
