📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can a single carved cliff face unlock an entire lost language, centuries after the empire that carved it is gone?

Cambyses died in 522 BC, on his way back from Egypt, in ways no surviving source explains for sure. What followed was a real fight over the throne. The man who came out on top, Darius Iduh-RY-usthe Persian king who took the throne after a succession crisis in 522 BC and left behind the Behistun Inscription., left behind an account of his own rise carved directly into a cliff face, in a form this course can still read today.

📚 A CONTESTED THRONE

According to Darius's own inscription, a man claiming to be Cambyses's brother, Bardiya, seized the Persian throne while Cambyses was still in Egypt. Darius's account insists this man was actually a fake, an impostor named Gaumata. The real Bardiya, Darius claims, had secretly been killed by Cambyses's own order years earlier. Darius killed the pretender and took the throne for himself. He then spent much of his first year as king putting down a wave of revolts across the empire. An empire this large, stitched together so quickly under Cyrus and Cambyses, was never fully settled the moment a new king took the throne. Darius had to prove, by force, that it truly answered to him.

This course should be honest about a real limit here. Darius's version is the only full account of these events to survive. It was carved at his own command, to defend his own right to rule. No separate Persian or Egyptian record exists to check his claims against. This does not mean Darius made up the whole story. It does mean this course cannot simply take a king's own defense of seizing his own throne as plain, checked fact. It could not take the Cyrus Cylinder's words at face value either, back in Week 17, for the exact same reason. A king writing about his own rise to power is never a neutral witness to it.

📚 A ROCK THAT UNLOCKED AN ENTIRE FIELD

This week's second Primary Source is the inscription itself, known as the Behistun Inscription. It is carved high into a cliff face at Mount Behistun, in what is now western Iran. Beside the text sits a large relief image. It shows Darius standing over the defeated pretender and a line of other captured rebel leaders. What makes this inscription truly remarkable is not just its content, but its form. The same text appears three times, carved in three different languages and cuneiform scripts. This kind of carving is called a trilingual inscriptiontry-LING-gwula single carved text written out in three different languages side by side.. In other words, one single message, carved three separate times side by side, so that no one who could read any one of the three languages would be left out. Its three versions are written in Old Persian, Elamite, and Babylonian, the same script this course has read again and again, in the Epic of Gilgamesh, Hammurabi's Code, and Ashurbanipal's library tablets.

Because the same message appeared in three known and unknown scripts side by side, the Behistun Inscription became cuneiform's own key to deciphermentdee-SY-fur-mentthe work of figuring out how to read a lost or unknown system of writing., in much the way the Rosetta Stone had already done for Egyptian hieroglyphics. In the 1830s and 1840s, a British officer and scholar named Henry RawlinsonRAW-lin-sunthe British scholar who copied the Behistun Inscription at great personal risk and helped decipher Babylonian cuneiform in the 1830s and 1840s. again and again risked his own life climbing the steep, dangerous cliff face to copy the inscription by hand, section by section. Using the Old Persian part, already partly known, as a starting point, Rawlinson and other scholars slowly worked out how to read Babylonian cuneiform too. This is worth stating plainly, as a closing thought for this whole stretch of the course. Every cuneiform Primary Source this course has read since Sumer in Week 2 can be read today mostly because of the work this single inscription made possible.

📚 ONE EMPIRE, THREE RIVER VALLEYS

With Egypt's conquest in this week's first lesson and Darius's throne now secure, this course has reached a genuine turning point. For the first time in this course's story, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Persia's own homeland all answer to one empire. That is an achievement no earlier power this course has studied ever managed. Not Sargon, back in Week 3. Not Hammurabi, back in Week 4. Not even Assyria, at its height in Weeks 11 and 12. And this empire does not stop looking outward. This course already knows, from Weeks 16 and 17, that the Ionian Greek cities along Anatolia's coast have been Persian subjects since Cyrus's conquest of Lydia. Just across the Aegean Sea lie other Greek cities, the mainland city-states this course has not yet studied in any real depth at all. Those cities are about to matter a great deal.

📚 CLOSING

This is where this course's story ends, and on purpose. One vast empire, newly joined across three river valleys, now looks west toward a scattered handful of small, feuding Greek city-states. At this exact moment, those small cities would seem to have no real chance against an empire this size at all. What actually comes next for Greece, and for Persia's own plans in that direction, is a story for later in the year.
