📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can a single battle really mark the end of one civilization's independence and the start of something the ancient world had never quite seen?

Cyrus the Great died in 530 BC. His son Cambyses IIkam-BY-seezCyrus's son, who conquered Egypt for Persia in 525 BC. inherited an empire already stretching from Anatolia to the edge of Mesopotamia. That empire had grown from nothing to enormous in barely one man's lifetime. Now it passed whole into a second man's hands. Cambyses did not wait long to make it bigger still. He turned almost right away toward the one great river-valley civilization this course has studied that Persia did not yet control.

📚 EGYPT FALLS AT PELUSIUM

By 525 BC, Egypt was ruled by Psamtik IIISAM-tikthe last independent pharaoh of Egypt, defeated by Cambyses at Pelusium., the latest king of the Saite Dynasty this course studied reviving Old Kingdom art and custom back in Week 10. That revival had lasted barely a century before a new conqueror arrived from the east. At the border fortress city of Pelusiumpuh-LOO-see-uma fortress city guarding Egypt's eastern border, site of Cambyses's decisive victory over Psamtik III in 525 BC., on the eastern edge of the Nile Deltathe wide, fan-shaped area where the Nile River splits into many branches before reaching the Mediterranean Sea., Cambyses's Persian army met and beat the Egyptian forces outright. Egypt, the civilization this course has followed since the Nile's steady flooding and Narmer's joining of the land in Week 5, became a Persian satrapy, a province ruled under Persian oversight. Its own long, independent line of kings was now truly at an end. Egypt had ruled itself, in one form or another, for close to three thousand years, back to Narmer and further still. That whole long run now closed at Pelusium, in a single battle, on a single day.

This moment deserves to be named clearly and with care. For the first time in the history this course has studied, Mesopotamia, Egypt, and Persia's own homeland were bound together under a single ruler. Two of the ancient world's oldest and most storied civilizations, each with thousands of years of its own kings, gods, and written record behind it, now answered to the same distant court. This is not a small event to fold quietly into a bigger story. In a real sense, it is the arrival of something the ancient Near East had never quite seen before: one empire, spanning what had always been separate worlds. Think back across this whole course. Sumer, Akkad, Babylon, Assyria, and Egypt itself each rose and ruled their own separate patch of the map, sometimes trading, sometimes fighting, but never truly one. In other words, three of history's oldest river civilizations now shared a single king for the very first time.

📚 A PORTRAIT WORTH DOUBTING

This week's first Primary Source turns to Herodotus's account of Cambyses in his Histories, Book III. It is worth reading with real care, for reasons beyond the doubt this course has already applied to Herodotus's stories of Deioces in Week 14 and Cyrus in Week 17. Herodotus's Cambyses is not merely a conqueror. He is shown as more and more unstable, and lacking any respect for the gods. He is accused of killing Egypt's sacred Apis bull out of scorn for Egyptian faith. He is accused of harming the preserved body of the past pharaoh. He is accused of violent crimes against his own family back in Persia.

This course should ask a direct question of this account. What would a Persian source, describing this same king, most likely say instead? Honestly, this course cannot fully answer that question. No surviving Persian writing describing Cambyses's own reign and character, in his own words, has come down to us the way the Cyrus Cylinder or Darius's own inscriptions have. What this course can say is that Herodotus was writing generations later, as a Greek. He was likely drawing in part on Egyptian priestly sources. Those priests had every reason to resent the foreign conqueror who had just ended their native royal line. This was the same priesthood whose temple rights a new foreign ruler might well have cut back. A hostile source is not automatically a false one. Some of what Herodotus reports may well be true. But a source with this much clear reason to make a conqueror look bad deserves exactly the same doubt this course has already applied to sources with a reason to flatter a king. It does not earn a free pass just because its subject happens to be a foreign ruler rather than a loved one. This course has practiced this same kind of doubt week after week, on kings praising themselves and on scribes praising their own gods. It should not stop practicing that doubt now, just because the tone of a source has flipped from praise to blame.

📚 CLOSING

Egypt's fall at Pelusium is not, however, the end of this course's story. It closes one chapter, and Cambyses's own reign closes only a few years later, in ways this course cannot fully sort out either. What follows is a fight over the throne, and a king whose own account of what happened next has survived, still readable in stone, for this course to read directly. Lesson 2 turns to it now.
