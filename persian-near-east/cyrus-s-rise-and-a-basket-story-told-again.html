📚 ANOTHER INFANT, BARELY SAVED

According to Herodotus, King Astyagesas-TY-uh-jeezthe king of Media whose own grandson, Cyrus, overthrew him. of Media, Cyaxares's own son, dreamed that his daughter's newborn son would someday overthrow him. Terrified, Astyages ordered the infant killed. The herdsman given this grim task instead spared the child. He raised the boy in secret as his own. The boy grew up as Cyrus, a Persian vassal youth, and Astyages never learned the truth until it was already too late to matter.

🎯 TRY IT: WHO'S WHO IN THE DREAM
Match each description to the person it names in Herodotus's account.
The infant secretly spared by a herdsman and raised in obscurity, who grew up to lead a revolt against his own grandfather.
The Median king whose dream convinced him his own infant grandson would one day overthrow him.

However this story actually began, it should sound familiar right away. This course has now met an abandoned or endangered infant, secretly saved and raised in hiding before rising far beyond what anyone expected, three separate times. It happened in Sargon's basket back in Week 3. It happened in Romulus and Remus's river rescue in Week 15. Now it happens again in Cyrus. This course should say directly what it has only hinted at before. This is a known kind of story. It shows up across far-apart cultures with no contact at all. Its repeated appearance should make any student rightly careful about taking any single version as sure, word-for-word history. In other words, when the very same tale keeps showing up in unrelated places, that is itself a clue about how legends grow, not proof that the tale is true.

Whatever the truth of his childhood, the grown Cyrus led a Persian revolt that overthrew Astyages around 550 BC. This united Media and Persia under his own rule for the first time. Until this moment, the Medes had been the stronger of the two peoples, with Persia serving as a vassal state beneath them. Cyrus flipped that order permanently, and Persia never again played second to Media. This union marks the true start of the Achaemeniduh-KEE-muh-nidthe Persian royal line founded by Cyrus, and the name historians give to his empire. Persian Empire, named for Cyrus's royal line. Within this course's remaining weeks, that empire will grow larger than anything the ancient Near East had yet seen, larger even than Assyria at its own height in Weeks 11 and 12.

📚 CROESUS, SOLON, AND A WARNING IGNORED

Cyrus's next target lay to the west: Lydia, a wealthy Anatolian kingdom this course already met briefly in Week 14. Lydia was now ruled by King CroesusKREE-susthe wealthy king of Lydia, defeated by Cyrus in 547 BC., whose name became a byword for riches in the ancient world. Herodotus tells a good story about Croesus, set some years before his war with Cyrus. In this account, the Athenian lawgiver Solon, already introduced to this course through his reforms in Week 16, visited Croesus's court. The king proudly showed off his riches and asked Solon to name the happiest man alive, plainly expecting Solon to name him. Solon instead named two little-known private citizens who had each lived well and died with honor. When Croesus pressed him further, Solon offered a warning rather than praise. Count no man happy, Solon said, until his life has actually ended, since fortune can turn on anyone, however rich, at any time. In other words, do not call your own life a success until it is over and done.

Croesus, Herodotus tells us, brushed the warning aside as foolish talk. Sure of his own wealth and power, he went to war against the rising Persian empire and lost badly. His kingdom was conquered, and, in most versions of the story, his own life stood in real danger before Cyrus spared him. Whether or not this exact talk with Solon ever truly happened, and Herodotus himself was writing generations after the fact, it holds a piece of Greek moral thinking worth taking to heart on its own terms. Worldly success is not the same thing as a settled, safe happiness, and pride in present fortune has a long history of coming right before a fall. This course has already met this same lesson once, in a different shape, back in Week 3. There, the Curse of Akkad blamed Naram-Sin's own pride for his empire's fall. Here, a Greek lawgiver offers almost the same warning about one overconfident king.

📚 CLOSING

Lydia's conquest in 547 BC brought the wealthy Ionian Greek coastal cities, the same cities Homer's descendants and neighbors called home, under Persian rule for the first time, a detail this course flagged as coming back in Week 16. Cyrus's empire, barely a few years old, had already reached from the Zagros Mountains to the shores of the Aegean Sea. In less than a decade, Cyrus had gone from a hidden infant to the ruler of the largest kingdom the Near East had yet seen, and he was not finished yet.
