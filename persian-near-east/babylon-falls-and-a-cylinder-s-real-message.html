📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
What actually happened when Babylon fell, and what does the record it left behind really say?

In 539 BC, Cyrus's army took Babylon, the same grand city this course toured through Nebuchadnezzar's Ishtar Gate back in Week 13. Barely a decade earlier, Babylon had stood as one of the most powerful cities on earth. Now it answered to a king from a kingdom that had not even existed as a unified power until Cyrus himself built it. Old tradition says the city fell with little fight left in it. Its own king, Nabonidus, had angered Babylon's priests. He had let the worship of the chief god Marduk slip. Maybe Persian soldiers won the day. Maybe unrest inside the city itself did just as much. Either way, one fact stands out above the rest. Mesopotamia, the region this course has followed since Sumer and Uruk in Week 2, now belonged to a Persian king. This had never happened before in its whole long history.

📚 WHAT THE CYLINDER ACTUALLY SAYS

This week's first Primary Source is the Cyrus Cylinder, a clay cylinder marked in Akkadian cuneiform and buried in Babylon's foundations. Archaeologists found it in 1879. In recent years, this object has earned a striking new name for itself. Some call it the world's first human rights charter. It was even shown for a time at the United Nations under that label. This course needs to look hard at that claim, and question it directly.

Read on its own terms, in the kind of writing it actually belongs to, the Cyrus Cylinder is a fairly ordinary Mesopotamian royal inscription. It is close in style and purpose to Nebuchadnezzar's own building writings this course studied in Week 13. It presents Cyrus as chosen by the god Marduk himself to save Babylon from Nabonidus's bad rule. It tells of temples fixed up after years of neglect. It boasts of Cyrus's own duty and kindness. This is exactly the kind of self-praise nearly every Mesopotamian king before him wrote about his own reign. It is not a constitution. It does not describe rights belonging to all people simply for being human. That is a modern idea. It would have made little sense in this ancient world at all. Calling it a human rights charter tells this course far more about what modern people wish an ancient king had said than about what Cyrus actually meant to say.

📚 A REAL AND CONSEQUENTIAL DIFFERENCE

What truly deserves this course's attention is not the Cylinder's words, but the real policy behind them. The inscription tells of Cyrus sending displaced peoples back to their home cities. It tells of him giving back their captured statues of the gods to the right temples. Outside evidence backs this up. It really was a planned Persian habit, not just empty royal boasting. This stands in real contrast to Assyria's own habit of removals, which this course studied scattering the northern Kingdom of Israel for good in Week 11. It also contrasts with Babylon's own exile policy, which this course studied removing Judah's elite to Babylon in Week 13. This same policy is exactly what let the Judean exiles, already met in Week 13, return home to Jerusalem and begin rebuilding their Temple. Cyrus's own decreean official order or command issued by a ruler, carrying the full force of law., an official order carrying the full force of law, made that return happen. It was part of this same wider plan for ruling a newly won empire full of many different peoples. A decree like this cost Cyrus little. It won him the loyalty of a people who might otherwise have hated their new ruler on sight.

💡 A QUESTION WORTH ARGUING BOTH WAYS
This course has now studied two very different ways of holding a large, mixed empire together. Assyria, in Weeks 11 and 12, relied on planned terror. It fell apart with shocking speed the moment its strongest king was gone. Cyrus relies instead on going easy on conquered peoples. He rebuilds local temples. He lets exiles return home. As later weeks of the Achaemenid story will show, he mostly lets conquered peoples keep their own customs and their own local rule. In each province, a Persian-appointed governor called a satrapSAY-trapa governor appointed to rule one province, or satrapy, of the Persian Empire on the king's behalf. oversaw the region on the king's behalf. Even so, daily life and local law mostly stayed in local hands, not the satrap's own. This kind of layered rule, a central king above and local officials still handling most local business below, is a very different design from Assyria's own habit of tearing down local structures whenever it conquered a people. Does this truly bind an empire together better than terror does? Does it solve the first-order political problem of holding a vast, mixed land in lasting peace? Or is it simply too soon to tell, a policy that has not yet met the kind of real test that broke Assyria apart? A policy of tolerance has not yet had to survive a weak king, a lost war, or a hard famine. Assyria's terror looked strong too, right up until it did not. This course will not settle that question outright. Weigh the evidence from both empires with care, and be ready to argue it either way.
