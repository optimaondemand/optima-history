📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Spain ignored Texas for 140 years. Then a French ship ran aground in the wrong bay.
When Spain heard about Fort St. Louis, officials in Mexico City were alarmed. A French colony on the Gulf coast could threaten Spain's silver mines. So Spain did something it had not done in almost a century and a half. It began to settle Texas. This lesson follows that rushed effort, from the first missions to the founding of San Antonio.

📚 PART 1 · PANIC IN MEXICO CITY

Hunting for the French

Spanish officials heard about the French colony in 1685. They sent ships to search the coast, and then they sent soldiers overland. A governor named de León led several expeditions north from Mexico. In 1689, he found what was left of Fort St. Louis. The buildings were burned and the ground was littered with bones. The French were gone, and the rival colony was dead.

Spain's silver mines in northern Mexico were its richest source of income. A French fort within reach of those mines was a serious danger. Spanish officials also feared that the French would join Native peoples against Spain. For more than a century, Texas had seemed like a useless backwater. Now it looked like a doorway into the heart of New Spain.

A king has to think about what could happen, not only what has happened. The French had not attacked Mexico. But a fort so near to Spanish lands could become a base for raiders and for trade with Native peoples. Spain did not know how many ships France might send next. Spanish leaders chose to treat the danger as real, and they spent money to deal with it.

But Spain was not reassured. If the French could land in Texas once, they could do it again. The Spanish government decided that the safest way to hold Texas was to settle it. A claim on paper was not enough.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
de León (deh leh-OHN). Alonso de León, a Spanish officer who led expeditions into Texas to find the French colony.
📚 PART 2 · THE FIRST MISSIONS

East Texas, 1690

The Spanish plan was to build missions among the Caddo of East Texas. In 1690, a priest named Massanet helped found the first one. The Caddo greeted the Spaniards by saying a word that sounded like "Tejas," which means "friends." The Spaniards began using it as the name of the land, and it became Texas.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Massanet (mah-sah-NET). Damián Massanet, a Spanish priest who helped found the first East Texas mission.
Tejas (TAY-hahs). A Caddo word meaning "friends." The Spaniards used it, and it became the name Texas.
Caddo (KAD-oh). A group of peoples who lived in East Texas and farmed the land.

The mission did not last. Sickness spread through the Caddo villages, and many people died. Some blamed the priests. A drought hurt the crops. Soldiers were too few to help. By 1693, the Spaniards chose to abandon the missions, and they buried their bell and left. Texas was empty of Spaniards again.

Looking back, the first mission shows what the Spanish model needed. The priests had no way to force the Caddo to stay or to listen. The Caddo had farms, villages, and leaders, and the Spaniards depended on them for food. When the mission lost the trust of the Caddo, it had no power to hold on.

Notice that nobody planned this outcome. Spain did not set out to lose the missions, and the Caddo did not set out to push the priests away. Disease, drought, and distance did much of the work. A different year, with better rain and fewer deaths, might have made the missions last longer, and the story of Texas could have gone another way.

📚 PART 3 · THE FRENCH NEXT DOOR

Trade against religion

Spain was gone, but France was not. In 1714, a French trader named St. Denis set up a trading post at Natchitoches, on the Red River, just across the likely border. He traded cloth, tools, and guns to the Caddo. Then he traveled all the way to the Rio Grande, where Spanish officials arrested him. Later they used his knowledge to guide a new expedition back into East Texas.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Rio Grande (REE-oh GRAHN-day). The great river that flows between Texas and Mexico to the Gulf of Mexico.

The story of St. Denis shows how different the two models were. He was a trader, not a conqueror, and he made friends easily. He also fell in love with the granddaughter of a Spanish commander, and the two later married. A Spanish soldier at a mission could hardly match that kind of welcome.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Natchitoches (NAK-uh-tish). A French trading post on the Red River, founded in 1714. It is now a town in Louisiana.
St. Denis (san deh-NEE). Louis Juchereau de St. Denis, a French trader who worked in Louisiana and Texas.

St. Denis also shows how much one person's choices could matter. He crossed borders that were supposed to be closed, he learned the languages of the people he met, and he knew how to make a friend. A different trader might have been caught and forgotten. Because of him, Spanish officials learned how close the French were and how much they had to lose.

This was the French model working beside the Spanish model. The French offered trade. The Spaniards offered a new religion and a place to live under priests. Many Caddo people preferred the French goods, especially guns. In 1716, Spain founded new missions in East Texas, but they depended on supplies from far away, and the French trading post stayed busy.

This time Spain brought soldiers and more supplies, but the old problem remained. The East Texas missions were hundreds of miles from the nearest Spanish town of any size. Supplies came slowly over rough roads. Every cart that broke down or river that flooded meant hunger. Spain needed a closer, steadier base between the Rio Grande and the Caddo country.

📚 PART 4 · SAN ANTONIO, 1718

Building on the springs

Spain needed a base between the Rio Grande and East Texas. In 1718, it chose a place where a river and a series of springs came out of the ground. A mission was built there, and next to it a fort (presidio) to protect it. The settlement was called San Antonio de Béxar. It is the city of San Antonio today.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
presidio (preh-SEE-dee-oh). A Spanish fort where soldiers lived and guarded the frontier.
Béxar (BEH-har). The Spanish name for the settlement at San Antonio.

Why there? Water. West of that spot, a long escarpment rises across Texas. At its base, water flows out of the rock in springs. Springs mark a line across the country, and towns like San Antonio, San Marcos, and Austin sit along it. Spanish settlers needed water for crops, and the springs offered the best supply for hundreds of miles.

The springs gave more than water. A river valley with water, wood, and good soil could support crops and cattle. Open grassland nearby gave horses and cattle plenty to eat. The mission, the fort, and later the town all drew on the same water, and they shared it through ditches.

A base like this also needed people. Soldiers and their families came north from Mexico to make the place a real town. It was slow work. The settlers had to dig ditches, build houses, and plant fields before they could eat. Texas gave them water, but nothing else came easily.

San Antonio became the center of Spanish Texas. But notice what it was. It was a base built in a hurry, as a defense against a rival. Spain had not found anything in Texas worth the cost. It had come because of a French ship.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Spain came to Texas because it was afraid, not because it saw riches. That helps explain why its effort stayed so thin. Settling a land to defend it is very different from settling a land to profit from it.
