📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did a country that won its independence give it up?
For nine years, Texas was a nation of its own. Then, in 1845, it joined the United States. This lesson follows the failed treaty, the election that decided the question, the terms of annexation, and the one problem that was left unsolved: where the border of Texas lay.

📚 PART 1 · A TREATY THAT FAILED

Tyler and the Senate

By 1844, President John Tyler of the United States wanted to annex Texas. He believed that it would help his country, and it would help him win a second term. His secretary of state, John C. Calhoun, worked out an agreement with the Republic. On April 12, 1844, the two sides signed an annexation treaty.

A treaty with another country needs two thirds of the Senate to approve it. Tyler did not have the votes. Many senators from the North opposed adding a new slave state. Others feared a war with Mexico. And some thought that the whole matter was being rushed for political reasons.

Calhoun made things worse. In a letter to the British minister, he defended slavery, and the letter was published. Many Northerners were disgusted, and it turned a debate about land into a debate about slavery. It became harder for senators from the North to vote yes.

In June 1844, the Senate rejected the treaty by a wide margin. It looked as if annexation was dead. Texans, who had hoped to join the United States for years, were disappointed. Some began to think again about staying independent and making deals with Britain and France.

An election that settled it

But the matter was not over. The presidential election of 1844 was only a few months away, and annexation became one of its main issues. Henry Clay, the Whig candidate, tried to avoid the question. James K. Polk, the Democrat, said plainly that he wanted Texas to join the Union.

Polk won, and by a small margin. Many Americans, especially in the South and West, read his victory as a sign that the country wanted to grow. Tyler, who was about to leave office, took the result as a green light. He decided to try again, using a different method.

Think about how elections shape history. A small number of votes in a few states decided the next president. And the next president's views decided whether Texas would join the nation. No treaty, no battle, and no speech had been as powerful as that single election.

📚 PART 2 · THE JOINT RESOLUTION

A second way in

Tyler's new plan did not need a treaty. Instead, Congress would pass a joint resolution, which is a kind of law that needs only a majority in each house. Supporters could count on a majority more easily than on two thirds. In early 1845, both houses voted yes.

President Tyler signed the resolution on March 1, 1845, just days before leaving office. It offered Texas a place in the Union. But the offer was only an offer. Texas had to accept it. The people of the Republic would have to decide whether to give up their independence.

That decision was not simple. The government of Texas had also received an offer from Mexico, backed by Britain and France. Mexico would recognize Texas as independent, if Texas agreed not to join the United States. Some Texas leaders weighed the offer carefully. But most voters wanted to join the Union.

Texas says yes

In the summer of 1845, a convention met in Austin. On July 4, it voted to accept the American offer. In October, Texas voters approved the new constitution for a state. In December, President Polk signed the bill that made Texas the twenty-eighth state of the Union.

Notice how many hands it took. The United States Congress made an offer. The Republic's president, Anson Jones, called a convention, and the Texas Congress also had to vote. Then the voters had to approve the new constitution. A change that large needed many people to agree, and each of them had a chance to say no.

The terms were unusual. Texas kept its public lands, which it could use to pay its debts. It also kept its public debt, so the United States did not have to pay for it. And Texas kept the right to divide itself, later on, into as many as five states.

Slavery was part of the deal, too. The resolution said that in any states carved out of Texas north of a certain line, slavery would be banned. South of the line, slavery would be allowed. It was a compromise that reflected the deep split in the country over slavery.

Look at what Texas gained and what it gave up. It gave up its flag, its president, and its place as a separate nation. It gained safety, a market for its cotton, and the strength of a larger country. For many Texans, it was a good trade. For Mexico, it was a threat.

📚 PART 3 · A BORDER WITHOUT A LINE

Nueces or Rio Grande?

The resolution left one question open. Where did Texas end? Texas claimed that its southern border was the Rio Grande, as the Republic's Congress had declared in 1836. Mexico said that the border was the Nueces River, a good distance to the north.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Rio Grande (REE-oh GRAHN-day). The great river that flows between Texas and Mexico to the Gulf of Mexico.
Nueces (noo-AY-ses). A river in South Texas that Mexico said was the southern border of Texas.

Each side had a case. Mexico pointed out that, under Spanish and Mexican rule, the Nueces had marked the edge of the province of Texas. Texas pointed to its own law and to the treaty signed by Santa Anna at Velasco. Neither argument settled anything, since each side had already decided what it believed.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Santa Anna (SAN-tah AH-nah). Antonio López de Santa Anna, a Mexican army officer who became one of the most powerful men in Mexico.
Velasco (veh-LAHS-koh). A Texas port at the mouth of the Brazos River where treaties with Santa Anna were signed in 1836.

The land between the two rivers was a broad stretch of South Texas. It was thinly settled, and most of it was dry. But it was large, and it mattered to both sides. If the border was the Nueces, then American soldiers who crossed it were entering Mexico. If the border was the Rio Grande, they were at home.

The question was not settled by the annexation. The United States simply accepted the Texan claim, without asking Mexico. Mexico, which had warned that annexation meant war, broke off relations with Washington. The stage was set for a new conflict, and the border was the cause.

The answer to a long question

Step back and look at the whole story. Spain had claimed Texas for three hundred years but could not fill it. Mexico had invited settlers in and then lost them. Texas had won its independence but could not support itself. In the end, the land came under the flag of the nation whose settlers had come to dominate it.

The reasons were not simple, and none of them was certain. A different treaty, a different election, or a different choice by Texas leaders might have led to a very different result. But after 1845, the question of who would own this place had an answer, and the answer had a border still to be argued over.

💪 REMEMBER THIS QUESTION

Keep one question in mind: why did a nation that had won its independence choose to join another country? The answer is made of debts, dangers, elections, and hopes.
