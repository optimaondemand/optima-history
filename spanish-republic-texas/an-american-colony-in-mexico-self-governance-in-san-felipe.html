📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How do you govern a colony when the law that applies to you does not fit your way of life?
Austin's colony was part of Mexico, but nobody in the colony had grown up under Mexican law. Austin had to keep order, settle quarrels, and protect the settlers with very little help from the government far away. This lesson follows how he did it, and how a Mexican court system ended up with American habits inside it.

📚 PART 1 · ONE MAN, MANY JOBS

Power in one pair of hands

In the early years, Austin held more power than any single official would hold today. He was the empresario who brought in the families. He was the judge who settled disputes. He was also the commander of the colony's militia, a force of ordinary settlers who trained to defend their homes.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
colony agent (empresario) (em-preh-SAH-ree-oh). A person who got a contract from the government to bring settlers to Texas.

That much power in one man would worry anyone. But it fit the circumstances. The colony was far from the state capital, and the Mexican government had few officials in Texas. Someone had to settle quarrels over land and cattle, catch thieves, and deal with raids. Austin was there, and he had the trust of both the settlers and the government.

Austin used the power with care. He wrote letters explaining his decisions. He listened to people who disagreed with him, and he tried to keep the settlers' goodwill. He knew that a colony run by force would not last, and he knew that the government in Mexico could take away his contract if he caused a scandal.

Austin also had to think about money. The colony had no regular taxes in its first years, and he often paid expenses out of his own pocket. Roads, courts, and a militia that was ready to march all cost something. Settlers gave labor and small fees. It was a thin and frugal government, but it kept order.

The code of 1824

In 1824, Austin wrote a set of rules for the colony. Today we call them his civil and criminal code. They covered crimes such as theft and murder, and they explained how courts should work, how land should be recorded, and what punishments should follow. The rules drew on Spanish law, on the laws of the United States, and on common sense.

A written code did several things. It told settlers what to expect. It reminded everyone that the colony was ruled by law and not just by Austin's wishes. And it showed Mexican officials that the colony was well run. When the officials accepted the code, it gave the colony a standing under Mexican law.

📚 PART 2 · MEXICAN FORMS, AMERICAN HABITS

Alcaldes and councils

Mexican law gave towns two main kinds of leaders. A mayor and judge (alcalde) heard cases and kept order. A town council (ayuntamiento) ran local business. Austin's colony set up alcaldes in each district. The settlers accepted this system, because it gave local people real authority over their own affairs.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
town council (ayuntamiento) (ah-yoon-tah-mee-EN-toh). The governing council of a Spanish or Mexican town.
mayor and judge (alcalde) (ahl-KAHL-day). A local official in Spanish and Mexican towns who acted as both mayor and judge.

San Felipe de Austin, on the Brazos River, became the center of the colony. It had stores, an inn, and a printing press where a newspaper was published. Settlers came here to record their land, to attend court, and to hear the news. It was the first real town of the American colony.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
San Felipe (sahn feh-LEE-peh). San Felipe de Austin, the main town of Austin's colony, on the Brazos River.
Brazos (BRAH-zus). A major river in Texas that flows to the Gulf of Mexico.

The problem of the jury

Here Mexican law and American habit did not fit. Americans expected a jury of ordinary citizens to decide guilt in a trial. Mexican law of the time did not provide for juries. A judge decided the case alone. To settlers from the United States, this felt like the loss of a basic right.

The settlers found a workaround. The alcaldes would call together a group of local men to hear the evidence and give their opinion. The group acted like a jury, although the law did not call it one. Then the official court, which was the body that made the legal decision, accepted the group's verdict and made it official.

Think about what a jury meant to these settlers. In the United States, jury trial was a protection honored for generations. A farmer who might be accused of a crime or sued over his land wanted his neighbors to decide, not one official. The jury was less a rule than a feeling of safety.

This solution kept both sides content for a time. The colony could say that its courts followed Mexican law. The settlers could say that they had their juries. It worked because Austin and the local officials cooperated and did not push the law to its limits. But it depended on goodwill, and goodwill could change.

A complaint that would not go away

When Mexico's government grew stricter, juries became a problem. The informal system had no firm place in the law. If an official chose to enforce the rules as they were written, trials by jury could stop. Settlers began to see this as a threat to their rights.

The complaint grew louder over time. Settlers listed trial by jury in their petitions to the government in 1832 and 1833. In 1836, when Texans wrote their Declaration of Independence, they listed the lack of jury trials among their grievances against Mexico. A small fix in a courtroom in the 1820s became a major cause in the 1830s.

Notice how long the complaint lived. A habit that started in 1824 was still on the list in 1836. The settlers did not forget what they had been used to, and they remembered when it was taken away. The small rights that people use every day are often the ones they defend most fiercely.

📚 PART 3 · A PATTERN FOR THE YEARS AHEAD

Keep the form, change the practice

The colony's courts show a pattern that appears again and again. The settlers did not rebel against Mexican law. They kept its names and forms. Inside those forms, they placed American habits. They used Mexican words, Mexican offices, and Mexican procedures, and they filled them with the practices that they knew.

This approach was clever, and it carried a risk. As long as the Mexican government left the colony alone, it could work smoothly. But sooner or later the government would look closely at what was actually happening. When it did, the gap between the form and the practice could look like disobedience.

Think about how each side saw the same courtroom. A settler saw a fair trial with a jury. A Mexican official might see a law bent out of shape. Both could be sincere. The disagreement was about what the law meant, and that kind of disagreement is hard to settle without trust.

Austin understood this. He worked to keep communication open and to show that the colony respected Mexican authority. Yet even the most careful leader cannot stop a government from changing, or a colony from drifting apart from the government that rules it.

💪 REMEMBER THIS QUESTION

Keep one question in mind: what happens when two peoples use the same words for different things? In San Felipe, a court was a court, but a jury meant something very different to each side.
