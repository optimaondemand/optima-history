📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Three hundred years. Three towns. How did that happen?
By the early 1800s, Spain had claimed Texas for almost three hundred years. How many Spanish towns existed in the whole province? Three. This lesson names them, counts the people in them, and asks what Spain's long effort actually left behind. It ends with a question to argue about.

📚 PART 1 · THE COMPLETE LIST

San Antonio, La Bahía, and Nacogdoches

There were three towns in Spanish Texas. The first was San Antonio de Béxar, founded in 1718 beside its springs. It was the largest and the capital. The second was La Bahía, a fort and a mission that had moved several times and finally settled at the place now called Goliad. The third was Nacogdoches in East Texas. It began as a mission in 1716 and became a town in 1779, when settlers who had been moved out of East Texas moved back.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
La Bahía (lah bah-EE-ah). A Spanish fort and mission in South Texas. Today it is in the town of Goliad.
Nacogdoches (nak-uh-DOH-chiz). A town in East Texas, named for a Native people who lived nearby.

Each town had its own reason for being there. San Antonio guarded the road and held the springs. La Bahía watched the coast, where French or other ships might land. Nacogdoches stood near the French trading post at Natchitoches, so it watched the eastern border. None of them began because settlers wanted to farm or trade there. All three began as answers to a threat.

Laredo, on the Rio Grande, was founded in 1755, but at the time it belonged to a different Spanish province. Within Texas itself, the list ends at three.

Notice how far apart the three towns were. San Antonio and La Bahía were about ninety miles apart, and Nacogdoches lay hundreds of miles to the northeast. The roads were rough, and the trip took many days. Each town stood almost alone on a very large map, and settlers in one could not easily help another.

📚 PART 2 · THE ARITHMETIC

How many people?

In the early 1800s, Spain took a census of the province. It showed only a few thousand Spanish-speaking people living in all of Texas. Most of them lived in San Antonio. Look at how many years that took. Spain had claimed the land since the 1500s. It had built missions and forts since 1690. Even so, the whole province held about as many people as a single large town in the United States.

To see how small this was, picture a school. A large middle school today might hold a thousand students. Spanish Texas, after three hundred years, would fill only a few of those schools. Compare that to Mexico City, which had well over a hundred thousand people. The difference between the capital and the northern frontier was enormous.

The numbers hide another problem. Many of the people counted were soldiers and their families, who could be sent elsewhere. Few had come to Texas to stay for generations. The population grew slowly, partly because the Comanche raided ranches and killed or captured settlers, and partly because the government could offer very few reasons to move there.

The census also leaves out the Native peoples who lived in and around Texas. The Comanche, the Apache, the Caddo, and many others made up most of the people who lived on this land. Spain counted only the people it ruled. That tells us something. For three hundred years, most of the land that Spain claimed was in fact held by other peoples.

Meanwhile, the English colonies on the Atlantic coast had begun as a few small villages. By 1800, the United States had millions of people. The English model produced a very different result in the same amount of time.

📚 PART 3 · WHAT DID TAKE ROOT

Cattle, land, water, and names

Spain did leave real marks on Texas. The first was ranching. Spanish settlers brought cattle, horses, and sheep. The cowboy (vaquero), a horseman who worked with cattle, was born on these ranches. The skills, the tools, and many of the words of the American cowboy trace back to him.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
cowboy (vaquero) (vah-KEH-roh). A Spanish and Mexican horseman who worked with cattle. The word comes from vaca, which means cow.

The second was the land system. Spain gave out land in large grants, measured in leagues and in smaller parcels. That system, and the records that came with it, would shape land ownership in Texas for a long time. The third was water law. Spanish rules about sharing water from rivers and ditches also lasted. The fourth was names. Many Texas places, such as San Antonio, the Brazos, and the Trinity, have Spanish names, or names the Spaniards gave.

Spanish ranching also gave Texas its first lasting business. By the late 1700s, large herds of cattle and horses grazed on ranches near San Antonio. Some animals escaped. Spanish horses that ran wild became the mustangs of the plains. It is worth noticing that these lasting marks came from ordinary ranchers and farmers, not from soldiers or priests. Their skills passed from family to family, and later ranchers, Mexican and American alike, adopted them. The things that lasted were practical, not grand.

Notice a pattern in what lasted. Priests, soldiers, and officials came and went, and their orders changed with every new king. Ranchers stayed in one place and passed down their land and their skills. A lasting thing is often one that ordinary people find useful in their own lives, even when no government is watching.

📚 PART 4 · AN ARGUMENT

Colony or garrison?

Historians still argue about what Spanish Texas really was. One side says it was a colony. It had towns, missions, ranches, and families who lived and died there for generations. They built lasting things, such as churches, ditches, and ranches.

The other side says it was a garrison. The province existed mostly to guard Mexico from rivals. Soldiers were the largest group, supplies came from far away, and few people lived there by choice. If the soldiers had left, the province might have vanished.

Evidence for the garrison side is easy to find. The Spanish government paid for most of what stood in Texas. Without money from Mexico City, the presidios would have run out of food and gunpowder. Evidence for the colony side is also real. Families married, raised children, and held land that their grandchildren inherited. Both lists are true, and that is what makes the question hard.

Which side do you find more convincing? Write a short argument in your notebook. Use at least two pieces of evidence from this lesson. You may find that the best answer says it was a little of both.

As you decide, think about what each reading explains. If Spanish Texas was a colony, why were there so few settlers? If it was a garrison, why did families build homes and ranches that lasted for generations? You may find that each side explains something the other cannot.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Three towns, a few thousand people, and a handful of lasting traditions are what three centuries of Spanish effort left in Texas. The Spanish model had been built for a different kind of place. The question for the rest of this story is who or what would fill the empty space.
