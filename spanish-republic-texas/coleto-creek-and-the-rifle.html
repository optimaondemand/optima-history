Mexican horsemen owned open ground; Texian riflemen owned broken ground. Everything from here to San Jacinto follows from that single fact.

📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Same soldiers, different ground, opposite result.
On March 19, 1836, Colonel James Fannin's force was caught in open prairie, surrounded by cavalry with no water and no cover, and forced to surrender. Set this directly against Concepción from last week: largely the same kind of soldiers, but this time caught in the open instead of fighting from cover. The result could not have been more different.

📚 PART 1 · CAUGHT IN THE OPEN

Fannin's force was caught crossing open prairie on March 19. Surrounded by cavalry, without water and without any cover to fight from, they were forced to surrender. Days later, on March 27, most of the surrendered prisoners were executed at Goliad on Santa Anna's orders.

⚠️ WATCH OUT
Do not read Coleto Creek as proof that Texian soldiers were somehow worse fighters than the men at Concepción. Set the two battles side by side: largely the same kind of soldiers, but different ground produced an opposite result.
📚 PART 2 · WHY GROUND MATTERED SO MUCH

The weapons themselves explain why. The Kentucky rifle was accurate well past 200 yards, but took a full minute to reload and carried no bayonet. The Mexican musket was faster to reload and carried a fixed bayonet, though its useful range was much shorter. The lance, in the hands of skilled horsemen, needed no reload at all.
