📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Anglos joining Mexico, or the American South moving west?
Austin's Colony was not simply Anglo-Americans joining Mexico. It was an English-model settlement, built around private families and private land, operating inside Spanish and Mexican legal forms. The first three hundred families to receive land under Austin's grant, remembered as the Old Three Hundred, came almost entirely from one part of the United States, and the land itself explains a lot about what kind of place they built.

💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
Two land-measurement words are worth reviewing before you read.
labor (LAY-bor) — a smaller unit of land, about 177 acres, granted for farming.
league (leeg) — a larger unit of land, about 4,428 acres, granted for grazing livestock.
📚 PART 1 · WHO CAME, AND ON WHAT TERMS
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If you were offered nearly free land in a new country, in exchange for an oath of loyalty and a religious requirement, would you take the deal? (No wrong answer — just check in with yourself.)

The first three hundred families to receive land under Austin's grant, remembered as the Old Three Hundred, came overwhelmingly from Tennessee, Alabama, Mississippi, and Louisiana, the part of the United States sometimes called the Old Southwest. In exchange for a league of grazing land and a labor of farmland, nearly free, colonists swore an oath of loyalty and agreed to practice Catholicism, though enforcement of the religious requirement was often loose in practice.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Austin was not the only empresario working this system. Erasmo Seguín, a Tejano ally who had helped negotiate the original grant, and fellow empresarios Martín De León and Green DeWitt were bringing in colonists of their own around the same years. Austin's colony was the largest and best documented, not the only one.
📚 PART 2 · THE GEOGRAPHY THAT SHAPED THE COLONY

The colony sat between two rivers, the Brazos and the Colorado. Its northern edge, near present-day Bryan, gets about 41 inches of rain a year, enough to support cotton. El Camino Real, the old Spanish royal road, crosses the colony roughly where the rivers can actually be forded, not by coincidence. West of the Colorado, the country dries out fast: ranching country, not cotton country. The conclusion is straightforward. Economically and culturally, this colony is essentially the western edge of the American South, transplanted onto Mexican soil.
