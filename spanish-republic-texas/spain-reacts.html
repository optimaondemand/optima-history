📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Occupy the province, or forfeit the claim.
Word of a French fort on the Texas coast reached Mexico City, and Spain panicked. After 140 years of near-total disinterest in Texas, Spain suddenly had to decide: occupy this land in fact, not just on paper, or risk losing the claim to France entirely.

📚 PART 1 · MISSIONS FOUNDED, ABANDONED, RE-FOUNDED

Spain's first response was to found missions in East Texas in 1690, close to where Fort St. Louis had failed. Those missions were abandoned in 1693 and not re-founded until 1716. Founded, abandoned, re-founded, twenty-six years apart, is not the pattern of a settled, sustained colonization effort. It is the pattern of a thin, unstable claim, propped up only when a French scare made it politically necessary.

One thread in this story deserves a name: the missionary Francisco Hidalgo, whose own overture to French traders at Natchitoches is part of what draws Louis Juchereau de St. Denis into Spanish Texas in the first place. A missionary reaching across an imperial boundary for help ends up reshaping that boundary.

📚 PART 2 · SAN ANTONIO AND THE SPRING LINE

In 1718, Spain founded a presidio and mission at San Antonio, sited directly on the springs where the escarpment meets the plain. This was not a random choice. Reliable water and a defensible position made this exact spot viable in a way Fort St. Louis's exposed coastline never was. The French model, still trading next door

Meanwhile, at Natchitoches, St. Denis kept trading rather than evangelizing, the same French pattern from Week 1: few settlers, trade, alliance. Spain's mission model and France's trading post model now sit right next to each other on the same frontier, on completely different terms.
