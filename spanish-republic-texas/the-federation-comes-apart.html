💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
One word is worth reviewing before you read. It is the direct opposite of last week's "federalism."
centralism (SEN-truh-liz-um) — a system of government in which power is concentrated in a single national authority, with states or provinces holding little independent power of their own.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Texas was not the only province with a grievance. It was one of several.
While Austin sat in a Mexico City prison, Santa Anna turned centralist and voided the Constitution of 1824 altogether. Texas was far from the only province to resist. Zoom out, and a much bigger pattern comes into view.

📚 PART 1 · THE LEGAL GROUND SHIFTS AGAIN

Santa Anna abandoned the federalist cause he had once claimed to champion and moved Mexico toward centralism instead. The Constitution of 1824, the same federalist framework Texas had relied on to claim real self-governing power back in Week 12, was voided. Everything colonists had built their governance around suddenly had no legal foundation left under it.

Zacatecas, a Mexican state far south of Texas, rebelled against this shift in May 1835. Santa Anna crushed the rebellion decisively, and the same army that crushed it then marched north, toward Texas.

📚 PART 2 · MAP THE REVOLTS

Zoom out across the whole decade, and Texas is one entry on a much longer list of Mexican provinces that resisted centralist rule: Zacatecas in 1835, Texas in 1835, California in 1836, New Mexico in 1837, the short-lived Republic of the Rio Grande in 1840, and Yucatán from 1839 to 1841. Texas did not act in isolation, and at this point in the story, it does not yet think of itself as unique.

⚡ TRY IT · SORT EACH REVOLT
For each province, did the revolt end in lasting separation from Mexico, or did the province stay part of Mexico?
1. Zacatecas, 1835
2. Texas, 1835
3. California, 1836
4. New Mexico, 1837
5. Republic of the Rio Grande, 1840
6. Yucatán, 1839–41

Two provinces on this list actually broke away. Only one stays gone. Texas's lasting independence was not the predictable outcome of some general pattern of Mexican provinces splintering; most similar revolts did not end in permanent separation at all.
