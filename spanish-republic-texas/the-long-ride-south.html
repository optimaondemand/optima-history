📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Before he settles a single family, Austin spends a year watching a constitution fail.
Stephen F. Austin inherited his father's grant in 1821, only to learn that Mexican independence had voided it. To keep the project alive at all, he had to travel to Mexico City and wait, sometimes for months at a time, while an entirely new government decided whether his colony would exist. He came home a year later knowing more about Mexican politics than any other Anglo colonizer alive.
🎯 BY THE END OF THIS LESSON YOU WILL BE ABLE TO…
1
I will be able to explain why Mexican independence in 1821 put Austin's original Spanish grant in doubt.
2
I will be able to describe what Austin found when he arrived in Mexico City in April 1822, and what a year of waiting there taught him.
3
I will be able to identify the Imperial Colonization Law of January 1823 as the document that finally confirmed his grant.
📚 PART 1 · A GRANT WITH NO GOVERNMENT LEFT TO HONOR IT

Put these three events in the order they actually happened.
1. Which came first?
2. Which came last?

Moses Austin's original grant had come from the Spanish government. When Mexico won its independence in 1821, that government no longer existed, and with it went the legal footing for the colony his son Stephen had inherited only months before. A grant from a government that no longer existed was not automatically honored by the government that replaced it. Stephen Austin had no real choice: he would have to travel to Mexico City himself and ask a brand-new government to recognize a right his family believed it already held.

📚 PART 2 · ARRIVING INTO A CORONATION

Austin arrived in Mexico City in April 1822, walking straight into the middle of a spectacle: the general Agustín de Iturbide was in the process of crowning himself emperor of Mexico. Austin had left home hoping for a quick confirmation. Instead he found a government still inventing itself, with no settled answer yet about who actually held power or how colonization law would work under it.

⚠️ WATCH OUT
Do not picture Austin walking into a tidy office and filling out paperwork. He walked into a capital still deciding what kind of government it even was. That instability, not any error on Austin's part, is why his errand took a full year instead of a few weeks.

Austin spent nearly a year in Mexico City. He used it to learn Spanish more fluently, to learn who the real decision-makers were, and simply to wait while Iturbide's new government worked out its own colonization policy. It was patient, unglamorous work, and it paid off: in January 1823, the Imperial Colonization Law finally confirmed his grant in writing.

💪 CARRY THIS FORWARD

Austin's first lesson in Mexican politics was patience: a year of waiting secured what a piece of paper alone could not. Hold onto that patience.
