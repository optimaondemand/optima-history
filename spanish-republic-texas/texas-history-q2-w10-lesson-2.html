One word is worth reviewing before you read.
abdicate (AB-dih-kate) — to formally give up a position of power, such as a throne or a title of rule.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The government that just confirmed Austin's grant does not survive the year.
In March 1823, only two months after the Imperial Colonization Law confirmed Austin's grant, Iturbide's empire collapsed and he abdicated. Austin's paperwork had barely dried, and the government that issued it was already gone.

📚 PART 1 · CONFIRMED TWICE IN ONE YEAR
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN
If you were Austin, and the government that had just approved your colony collapsed two months later, would you trust the next government's approval any more than the last one's? (No wrong answer — just check in with yourself.)

When Iturbide's empire fell apart, whatever legal footing it had granted fell into question too. The successor government that took Iturbide's place had to re-confirm Austin's grant all over again, which it did the following month. In less than a year, Austin's right to bring settlers into Texas had been approved twice, by two different governments.

📚 PART 2 · WHAT AUSTIN LEARNED, AND WHY IT MATTERS LATER

Two confirmations in one year taught Austin something no single document could: Mexican governments in this period were provisional, meaning they could rise and fall quickly, and legal footing alone was not enough to guarantee his colony's survival. Personal relationships with the people actually holding power, whoever they happened to be, mattered as much as any paper he carried.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW
Watch for this pattern to recur for the rest of the quarter. Every time a government in Mexico City changes, Texas's legal footing changes with it, whether Texans wanted it to or not.

This lesson in patient, relationship-based diplomacy served Austin well for years. But hold onto it carefully: he will make this same journey to Mexico City a second time, and it will go far worse.
