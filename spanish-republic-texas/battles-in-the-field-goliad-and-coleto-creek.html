💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth a look before you read. They show up in bold, and you can hover over any of them for a reminder.
bayonet (BAY-uh-net). A blade fastened to the end of a gun for fighting up close.
cannon (KAN-un). A large gun on wheels that fires heavy metal balls.
cavalry (KAV-ul-ree). Soldiers who fight on horseback.
cover (KUV-er). A place where a soldier can hide from enemy fire, such as a wall, a ditch, or a river bank.
lance (lans). A long spear used by soldiers on horseback.
mission (MISH-un). A religious settlement where priests taught Native people the Christian faith.
musket (MUS-kit). A long gun, loaded from the front of the barrel, that was used by most armies in the early 1800s.
retreat (rih-TREET). To pull back from a battle or a danger.
rifle (RY-ful). A gun with grooves inside the barrel that make it much more accurate at long range.
scout (skowt). A person sent ahead to learn where the enemy is and what the land is like.
surrender (suh-REN-der). To give up to an enemy.
Texian (TEK-see-un). A word used in the 1830s for a settler in Texas who came from the United States.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Why did the same kind of soldiers win one battle and lose the next?
In October 1835, a small group of Texians won a fight at Concepción by shooting from a riverbank. In March 1836, a much larger group of Texians, including some of the same men, was destroyed on an open prairie near Goliad. This lesson compares the two fights and shows how the ground, the weapons, and one hard decision made the difference.

📚 PART 1 · A COMMANDER WHO WAITED

Fannin at Goliad

After the fall of the Alamo, about four hundred Texians sat in the old fort at La Bahía, near Goliad. Their commander was Colonel James Fannin. He was brave and well educated, and he had fought at Concepción. But he was uncertain about what to do next, and that uncertainty would cost him dearly.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Concepción (kon-sep-see-OHN). A Spanish mission on the San Antonio River, south of the town of San Antonio.
Goliad (GOH-lee-ad). A town on the San Antonio River with a Spanish fort and mission.
Coleto (koh-LEH-toh). A creek near Goliad where Fannin and his men were caught in open country.
La Bahía (lah bah-EE-ah). A Spanish fort and mission in South Texas. Today it is in the town of Goliad.
Alamo (AL-uh-moh). A former mission in San Antonio that was used as a fort in 1835 and 1836.

On March 11, Sam Houston ordered Fannin to leave Goliad and fall back to Victoria. The Mexican army was close, and Houston wanted to gather every soldier he had. Fannin agreed, but he did not go. He waited for scouts, for wagons, and for another detachment. Each day made the retreat more dangerous.

Fannin had reasons to hesitate. Goliad had a strong fort with thick walls, and a commander who leaves a strong place for open country takes a risk. He also had sick soldiers, heavy wagons, and supplies to protect. A leader who is asked to give up a strong position needs great confidence, and Fannin had doubts about Houston's orders.

Think about the problem of a commander who cannot decide. Fannin did not ignore Houston's order. He doubted it, he delayed it, and he looked for reasons to wait. By the time he finally left on March 19, the Mexican general Urrea had moved fast and was only a few miles away.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Urrea (oo-RAY-ah). José de Urrea, the Mexican general who defeated the Texian forces in southern Texas in 1836.

The retreat began badly. The Texians took along cannon and heavy wagons. A cart broke, and the oxen pulling it gave out. They had marched only a few miles before they stopped to rest in open prairie, near a creek called Coleto. They had no cover, and they had little water.

Surrounded on the prairie

Urrea's cavalry appeared on the horizon before the Texians could resume their march. Mounted soldiers with lances rode in a ring around the column. Mexican infantry followed behind. Fannin formed his men into a hollow square, with cannon at the corners and riflemen facing outward on every side.

The Texians fought well. They drove back several attacks, and their rifles killed many Mexican soldiers. But they had nowhere to go. Fannin was wounded. The men could see that the army around them was growing as reinforcements arrived. By night, the Texians had lost nine men and had dozens of wounded.

Think about the night that followed. The wounded cried out for water. The Texians piled up what they could, including carts and dead horses, to make a rough barrier. They hoped to hold out until help arrived. But nobody was coming, and every man in the square knew it by the time the sun rose.

The next morning, March 20, Urrea brought up cannon. The Texians were out of water, and their wounded were suffering. Fannin decided to surrender. He believed that his men would be treated as prisoners of war. They were marched back to the fort at Goliad and locked inside.

A week later, on March 27, a Palm Sunday, the prisoners were marched out of the fort in small groups and shot. Santa Anna had ordered the execution. More than three hundred men died. A few escaped, and a few were spared. The news spread across Texas, and "Remember Goliad" joined "Remember the Alamo" as a battle cry.

🌐 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Santa Anna (SAN-tah AH-nah). Antonio López de Santa Anna, a Mexican army officer who became one of the most powerful men in Mexico.
📚 PART 2 · THE SAME SOLDIERS, DIFFERENT GROUND

Concepción and Coleto side by side

Look at the two fights side by side. At Concepción, ninety Texians won against a much larger force. At Coleto Creek, about three hundred Texians lost to an army of similar skill. Fannin himself had been at both. The soldiers were much the same. The difference was the ground.

At Concepción, the Texians fought from a deep riverbank. They could load their rifles in safety and fire at soldiers crossing open ground. At Coleto, the Texians stood on a flat prairie with no ditch and no trees. Mexican cavalry could circle them, and Mexican cannon could bombard them without any shelter in return.

That is the lesson of these two fights. Soldiers who fight from cover have a great advantage. Soldiers who fight in the open lose it. It is not enough for an army to be brave. It must also choose its ground, and Fannin's army had let the ground choose for it.

Rifle, musket, and lance

The weapons help explain why. The Texian soldiers carried a long rifle, sometimes called a Kentucky rifle. A skilled shooter could hit a target past two hundred yards. But the rifle took a long time to load, about a minute for each shot, and it had no blade on the end for close fighting.

The Mexican infantry carried a musket. It was less accurate and could not shoot as far, but it could be loaded much faster. It also carried a bayonet, which made the soldier dangerous in close combat. A line of Mexican infantry that reached the Texians could do terrible harm.

Mexican cavalry carried a lance, and its riders were famous for skill on horseback. A man on a horse with a long spear could ride down a soldier who was stopping to reload. In open country, the combination of cavalry and infantry was hard to beat.

Put these facts together. A rifleman behind a wall or a riverbank could shoot many times from a safe place and drive off an enemy. A rifleman in the open was slow, easy to surround, and easy to reach with a lance or bayonet. The weapon was only as good as the place where it was used.

A rule to remember

The Texians won when they fought from cover, and they lost when they did not. That rule held at Concepción, at San Antonio, and at Coleto Creek. It was not a law of nature. But it was close to one, and the smartest Texian leaders understood it.

This rule also helps explain the Alamo. The walls of the old mission kept a far larger army at bay for almost two weeks. Fighting from cover was not a guarantee of victory, but it gave a small band of men a real chance. Fighting in the open gave them almost none.

Notice that this rule is about choices. Fannin chose to stay too long, and he chose to rest on open ground. Each of those choices made his army easier to defeat. A different decision on March 11 might have brought his four hundred men safely to Houston's army, and the story of the war could have gone differently.

💪 REMEMBER THIS QUESTION

Keep one question in mind: what does it cost a commander to wait? At Goliad, the answer was measured in lives.
