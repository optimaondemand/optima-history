📚 PART 1 · THE ROAD TO MEXICO CITY

The War in the North

In May 1846, General Taylor won the first battles of the war near the Río Grande. Then he marched his army deep into northern Mexico, toward the strong city of Monterrey.

At Monterrey, in September 1846, the Americans attacked from two sides. One force took the hills west of the city. Another fought its way in from the east, house by house. Soldiers cut holes through the walls of buildings so that they could move without crossing the open streets, where they were easy targets. Texas Rangers fought in the battle too. After four days, the Mexican commander asked for terms.

Taylor let the Mexican army leave the city with its weapons, and he agreed to stop fighting for about eight weeks. Polk was angry. He thought Taylor had been too generous to an enemy who was still unbeaten.

Buena Vista

In February 1847, Taylor faced his hardest test at Buena Vista, near the city of Saltillo. By then, many of his best soldiers had been pulled away to join the army that would land at Veracruz. Taylor was left with about 5,000 men, most of them volunteers. Facing him was Antonio López de Santa Anna, who had marched an army of roughly 15,000 across a hard desert to reach him.

The battle lasted two days in a narrow mountain pass. Several times the Mexican army nearly broke the American line. American cannon and a famous stand by Mississippi volunteers, led by Jefferson Davis, helped hold it together. On the night of February 23, Santa Anna pulled his army back. Both sides claimed a victory, but Taylor held the field. He became a national hero, and in 1848 Americans elected him president.

These were real victories. Yet Mexico’s leaders still would not agree to give up land.

A Second Road

Polk decided to try a different road. He would strike at the heart of Mexico. The army would land on the coast, then march inland to the capital, Mexico City. He chose General Winfield Scott to lead it. The capture of the capital would turn out to be the pivot of the whole war.

The Landing at Veracruz

In March 1847, Scott landed his army from the sea near the port of True Cross (Veracruz). The American navy kept up a blockade so that no help could reach the city. Scott surrounded it and laid siege to it. After about three weeks, Veracruz surrendered.

Cerro Gordo (SEH-roh GOR-doh): Spanish for "Fat Hill." A mountain pass on the road to Mexico City.
Chapultepec (chah-POOL-teh-pek): A castle on a hill above Mexico City. It held a military college.
Guadalupe Hidalgo (gwah-dah-LOO-pay ee-DAHL-go): A town near Mexico City where the treaty was signed.
Veracruz (vair-ah-KROOZ): Spanish for "True Cross." The main port of Mexico on the Gulf of Mexico.

Now the army had a harbor. It also had a long road ahead, over mountains, to the capital.

An Old Enemy Returns

Mexico’s army was led by Santa Anna, the same general Taylor had faced at Buena Vista. He was also the general who had fought at the Alamo and had been captured at San Jacinto. In 1846 he was living in exile. The American navy let him pass through its blockade, because Americans hoped he would make peace. Instead, Santa Anna took command of the Mexican army and fought.

This shows how much depended on choices and chance. Polk’s choice to let one man return helped create the army Scott had to fight.

A short timeline of the road to Mexico City.

At Cerro Gordo, in April 1847, Santa Anna placed his army in a strong spot on the road. A young captain of engineers named Robert E. Lee saved the day. He scouted the rough hills and found a path around the Mexican position. Scott sent soldiers along it. The Mexican army was hit from the side and broke.

Lee did the same kind of work again in August. Near Mexico City, a field of sharp volcanic rock blocked the way. Lee crossed it at night to carry a message to other American units. His skill at reading the land was one reason the army kept moving.

Many officers in this war would lead armies in the Civil War, years later. Lee was one. Ulysses S. Grant was another.

The Fall of the Capital

On September 13, 1847, the Americans attacked the castle at Chapultepec, high on a hill over Mexico City. A military college stood there. Young Mexican cadets helped defend it, and Mexico remembers them with honor. U.S. Marines took part in the attack. The Marines’ Hymn still sings of the “halls of Montezuma,” a name for the Mexican capital. The next day, Scott’s army marched into Mexico City.

📚 PART 2 · PEACE AND ITS PRICE

Making Peace

The war ended with a treaty. It was signed on February 2, 1848, in the town of Guadalupe Hidalgo. An American diplomat named Nicholas Trist wrote it with Mexican leaders. Polk had ordered Trist home, but Trist stayed and finished the work. Polk sent the treaty to the Senate anyway, and it was approved.

The border
Mexico gave up its claim to Texas. The Río Grande became the border. Texas had won the argument.
The land
Mexico also gave up California and a huge stretch of land between California and Texas. This is called the Mexican Cession. Mexico lost about half of its land.
The money
The United States paid Mexico $15 million. Mexicans who lived in the new lands could stay and become American citizens.

Texans had gotten what they wanted along the Río Grande. For Mexico the peace was a bitter loss. Remember that a treaty can end a war and still leave anger behind.

📚 PART 3 · SLAVERY AND THE TEXAS BOUNDARY

The Question of Slavery

The new lands raised a hard question. Would slavery be allowed there? Texas was a slave state, and so were most Southern states. Many Southerners wanted slavery to spread into the new lands. Many Northerners did not. In 1846 a congressman named David Wilmot asked Congress to ban slavery in any land won from Mexico. The House passed the idea, but the Senate did not. The fight went on for years.

Texas Gives Up Land and Gets Paid

Texas had a second border problem, this time on its west. Texas claimed land all the way to the upper Río Grande, including the city of Santa Fe. The United States said that land belonged to New Mexico. Some Texas leaders talked of using force.

In 1850, Congress passed a group of laws called the Compromise of 1850 to cool the whole slavery quarrel. One of them settled the Texas border. Texas gave up its claim to the land that is now part of New Mexico and other states. In return, the United States paid Texas $10 million, which helped it pay off the debts left from its days as a republic.

So Texas had kept the Río Grande, lost its western claims, and cleared its debts. Each of these things happened because Texas was now part of the United States.
