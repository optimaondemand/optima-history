📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Can a pharaoh win greatness through trade and peace instead of war?

Egypt's Old Kingdom eventually gave way to a Middle Kingdom, a period of foreign Hyksos rule, and finally the New Kingdom, an age of empire and bold pharaohs. This lesson meets one of its most unusual rulers: Hatshepsut, a woman who took the full throne and held it for two decades. Rather than building her name through conquest, she is remembered for a great trading expedition to the land of Punt. This lesson asks whether that kind of greatness counts for as much as a warrior-king's.

4
I will be able to weigh whether trade and building count as greatness the way conquest does.

Egypt's Old Kingdom, the pyramid-building age Week 5 introduced, did not last forever. Around 2181 BC, central power grew weak. Local governors grew stronger than the pharaoh in Memphis. Egypt broke apart into a time historians call the First Intermediate Period. Egypt came back together under the Middle Kingdom (about 2055 to 1650 BC). This was a time of new order, growing trade, and some of Egypt's finest writing. That order broke down again in the Second Intermediate Period. A Semitic-speaking people called the Hyksos took control of Lower Egypt from their capital at Avaris. They ruled as outsiders, though partly in the Egyptian style, from roughly 1650 to 1550 BC. The Hyksos are remembered for one key import: the horse-drawn chariot and the composite bow. Egypt would soon turn these into the backbone of its own New Kingdom armies. Egyptian princes later drove out the Hyksos. They joined the country back together under the New Kingdom (about 1550 to 1150 BC), the age this week and next week's lessons explore. It was an age of empire and bold pharaohs. In this lesson, it is also the age of a queen who ruled as king.

📚 AN UNUSUAL PATH TO THE THRONE

Hatshepsuthat-SHEP-soota female pharaoh of Egypt's New Kingdom, ruling in her own right from about 1479 to 1458 BC. came to power around 1479 BC. She first ruled as regent for her young stepson and nephew Thutmose III. Within a few years, she ruled as pharaoh in her own right. She took on the full royal titles, regaliarih-GAY-lee-uhthe special clothes, crowns, and objects that mark someone as a king or queen., and even the old false beard of Egyptian kingship. Still, carvings and statues sometimes show her with female features alongside these male royal symbols. This was, by any measure, a rare path. Egyptian kingship had never shut women out by rule. But almost no woman before Hatshepsut had claimed the full throne and held it for roughly two decades, as she did.

Her advisors worked to explain her rule through careful writings. They called her chosen by the god Amun himself. This divine-selection story is not unlike the "divine kingship" claim Week 5 introduced, now stretched to cover a truly new case. Carvings at Deir el-Baharidayr el-ba-HAH-reethe site near Thebes where Hatshepsut built her mortuary temple, cut into the cliffs. go even further, showing Amun himself as Hatshepsut's true father, visiting her mother in the form of the reigning pharaoh. This was a bold claim for any pharaoh to make about their own birth. It was even bolder for a female ruler seeking to hold power in her own name. Only one earlier woman, Sobekneferu near the end of the Middle Kingdom, had ever ruled Egypt as pharaoh in her own right, and her reign lasted only about four years. No queen before Hatshepsut had ever ruled anywhere near as long as her two full decades on the throne.

📚 TRADE INSTEAD OF CONQUEST

Rather than building her name mainly through war, Hatshepsut is best remembered for a different kind of feat: a major trading expeditionek-spuh-DISH-una journey made by a group of people for a clear purpose, such as trade or exploration. to the land of Punta land likely along the Red Sea coast of modern Eritrea, Sudan, or Somalia, a source of incense, gold, and other prized goods.. She sent this expedition out around the ninth year of her reign. Carvings that record the trip show a fleet of about five ships, each roughly seventy feet long, sailing down the Red Sea coast. This week's Primary Source lets you read the writings Hatshepsut had carved at her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari, near Thebes. They record the voyage in vivid detail: ships loaded for the return trip with incense trees dug up with their root balls whole, ebony, ivory, gold, myrrh resin, leopard skins, and even live baboons.

Egypt's own land could not make many of these luxury and ritual goods at home. Incense above all was burned nonstop in temple rituals across Egypt. This made a steady supply from Punt a real need, not just a show of royal wealth. This is a useful case study in scarcitySKAIR-sih-teenot having enough of something people want or need.: Egypt lacked certain resources it needed. Rather than simply doing without them or seizing them by force, Hatshepsut's government organized a large, carefully planned trading trip to get them through fair exchange.

⚙️ TECHNOLOGY SPOTLIGHT · TRADE AND SCARCITY
Photo: Olaf Tausch, CC BY-SA 4.0, via Wikimedia Commons.

Every civilization this course has studied faces the same basic problem: scarcity, not having enough of something people want or need. Egypt had plentiful grain and stone, but it lacked incense, ebony, and other goods its temples and elites demanded. Hatshepsut's Punt expedition shows one common answer to scarcity: organized trade. Rather than seizing goods by force or simply doing without, her government sent ships, sailors, and trade goods on a long journey to exchange for what Egypt needed. This same basic pattern, a society identifying what it lacks and organizing to trade for it, will reappear across the rest of this course, from Phoenician traders to Persian roads.

Hatshepsut also spent resources on building projects at home. At the great temple of KarnakKAR-naka huge temple complex at Thebes, dedicated mainly to the god Amun., she raised a pair of tall granite obelisksOB-uh-liska tall, four-sided stone pillar that narrows to a point at the top, often set up in pairs at a temple entrance., four-sided pillars carved from single blocks of stone, built for the god Amun. One still stands today, nearly 97 feet tall, among the tallest ever raised in Egypt. Cutting and moving a single block of stone that size took hundreds of workers. It likely also took barges built strong enough to carry the stone down the Nile without breaking apart. 📚 A DIFFERENT KIND OF GREATNESS

Compare Hatshepsut's approach to what you might expect from a bold New Kingdom pharaoh eager to prove himself through war. Hatshepsut's Punt expedition, and the peaceful building projects that filled much of her reign, stand for a real, clear choice: greatness reached through careful rule, careful peace-making, and strong trade, rather than through the battlefield. Whether this made her a lesser pharaoh than a conquering warrior-king is a real question worth thinking about, not one with a clear answer already settled. Next lesson introduces her stepson, Thutmose III, who chose a very different path to greatness once the throne became fully his own. This week's assessments will ask you to judge the two approaches side by side.
