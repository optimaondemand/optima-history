📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Would you gamble your whole army on a single narrow mountain pass?

When Hatshepsut died, her stepson Thutmose III became sole ruler and chose a very different path to greatness: the battlefield. His first campaign, against a rebel coalition at Megiddo, produced the earliest battle in world history with a detailed surviving account, and it turned on one risky decision his own officers argued against.

📚 A REBELLION IN CANAAN

A coalitionkoh-uh-LISH-una group of separate rulers or states that join forces for a shared goal, such as a rebellion or a war. of Canaanite and Syrian rulers had gathered at the fortified city of Megiddo, in modern northern Israel. They were backed by the strong kingdom of Mitanni to the northeast. Together, they rose up in open revolt against Egyptian rule. Egyptian records claim the coalition included the chiefs of over 330 towns and cities across Canaan and Syria, a sign of just how big this revolt was. Thutmose III marched his army north to face them. His route took about ten days, moving up the coast road Egyptians called the Ways of Horus. This week's Primary Source, drawn from the official Annals of Thutmose III recorded on temple walls at Karnak, tells what happened next in rare detail for an ancient war record.

📚 A RISKY CHOICE OF ROADS

Three roads led to Megiddo through the surrounding hills. Two were wide, safer routes coming in from the sides. One was a narrow, direct mountain pass called the Aruna Pass. It was so tight that soldiers had to march single file, horse following horse. They would be fully exposed if the enemy chose to attack while the army was strung out along it. According to the Annals, Thutmose III's own war council argued strongly against the narrow pass. They urged him to take one of the safer roads instead. Thutmose III overruled them. He reasoned that the enemy would expect an Egyptian attack from the safer routes. He guessed the enemy would gather their own forces there, leaving the direct pass open. He himself led his army through the narrow pass at its head, sharing the same risk he asked of his soldiers.

The gamble paid off. The Egyptian army came out of the pass with no enemy in sight, just as Thutmose III had guessed. His forces formed up in the open plain before Megiddo while the coalition's forces were still spread out elsewhere. In the battle that followed, the coalition army broke and ran back into the city. The open field was now Thutmose III's. To keep help from reaching the city, Egyptian soldiers built a wooden wall and ditch all the way around Megiddo, cutting it off from the outside. Still, Megiddo itself held out under siege for seven more months before it finally gave up. Egyptian scribes later listed a huge haul of goods taken from the fallen city. The list included hundreds of horses, chariots trimmed with gold, and enough grain to fill Egypt's royal storehouses for years.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW · CONTINGENCY AT THE MOUNTAIN PASS
This is worth pausing on as a real historical contingency, in the spirit of Sargon's basket and Hammurabi's late-life wars from earlier weeks. Thutmose III's own officers thought the narrow pass was reckless. By ordinary war logic, they had a point. Suppose the coalition army had simply posted a small guard at the pass's far end. The exposed, single-file Egyptian column could have been badly hurt before ever reaching open ground. Nothing about this outcome was set in stone ahead of time. A different set of scouts, a slightly different coalition plan, or a pharaoh less willing to overrule his own generals, and this course's account of Egyptian history might record a very different first campaign for Thutmose III.

He led as many as seventeen military campaigns into the Levant, earning a lasting reputation as one of Egypt's greatest warrior-kings.

📚 A LASTING GRIP ON THE LEVANT

Megiddo was only the beginning. Over the next two decades, Thutmose III returned to the Levant again and again. Local rulers who had once rebelled were now forced to send tribute to Egypt each year. Some were even made to hand over their own sons to be raised at the Egyptian royal court. Those sons, once grown, sometimes went home as rulers loyal to Egypt. This was a quiet but strong way of binding a conquered region to the empire without constant war. By the end of his reign, Egypt controlled more land than at any earlier point in its history, stretching from Nubia in the south to the Euphrates River in the north. Thutmose III kept careful written lists of the tribute he collected each year, recording exact counts of gold rings, cattle, timber, and captives from every subject city, a habit of precise record-keeping that echoes the Mesopotamian scribal traditions this course met back in Weeks 2 through 4.

⚔️ BATTLE · MEGIDDO

Fought around 1457 BC, the Battle of Megiddo is the earliest battle in world history with a detailed surviving account. Thutmose III's risky choice to march his army through a narrow mountain pass, rather than a safer road, caught the rebel coalition by surprise and helped decide the outcome before the fighting even began.

📚 TWO PHARAOHS, TWO VIRTUES

Set beside Hatshepsut's Punt trip from Monday's lesson, Thutmose III's Megiddo campaign offers a clear contrast in what ancient greatness could look like. Hatshepsut sought careful rule, patience, and strong peace-making. Thutmose III sought a bold gamble he led in person, growing Egyptian power by force. Neither path was clearly the right one. Both rulers are remembered, in their own ways, as strong pharaohs. This week's assessments will ask you to weigh both approaches directly against each other.
