📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Could one basket on a river really stand at the root of the first empire?

Every civilization this course has studied so far, Jericho, Çatalhöyük, and Uruk, has been a single city or a small region. This week, a man named Sargon builds something the world has never seen: an empire ruling many cities and peoples at once. The story his own scribes told about his birth is strange, humble, and possibly half legend, and it is the perfect place to ask how much of history really turns on small, uncertain events instead of one fixed path.

📚 AN IMPROBABLE BEGINNING

According to the Sargon Birth Legend, a text you will read directly this week, Sargon was born to a priestess who could not openly raise him. She placed her infant son in a basket sealed with tar and set him adrift on the Euphrates River. The basket floated downstream until a man named Akki, a drawer of water, found it. Akki rescued the child and raised him as his own son, later training him as a gardener. From this humble, obscure beginning, the story says, the goddess Ishtar came to favor young Sargon. He rose to become cupbearer, a position of real trust and access, to the king of the city of Kish. From cupbearer, Sargon eventually seized power for himself. He then began the conquests that built the Akkadian Empire and founded his own royal dynastyDY-nuh-steea line of rulers from the same family, passing power down from parent to child..

Whether every detail of this story happened exactly as told is hard to check for sure. No other written record from Sargon's own lifetime lives on to check the story against. Here is what matters for how you think about history: the entire empire that follows rests on a chain of events that did not have to happen. What if the basket had sunk? What if Akki had not been drawing water at that exact bend of the river that day? What if Sargon had never caught the king's eye? If any one of these small things had gone differently, none of the rest of this lesson would exist to tell. This is what historians mean by contingencykun-TIN-jen-seethe idea that history could easily have turned out differently, because it depended on small, uncertain events, not one fixed path.: the real past was not marching toward Sargon's empire like some fixed endpoint it had to reach. A single unlikely childhood, real or made up, stands at the root of the first empire in recorded history.

📚 FROM CUPBEARER TO CONQUEROR

Once in power, Sargon moved with real speed. He conquered the Sumerian city-states one by one, including Uruk, the city you studied last week. He defeated Uruk's king, Lugalzagesi, and brought the whole of southern Mesopotamia under one rule for the first time. He then pushed his conquests northward and westward, as far as the Mediterranean coast, and into parts of Anatolia and Elam. Sargon founded a new capital city, Akkad. Its exact spot has never been found again by archaeologists, a small but telling reminder: even a capital city ruling an empire can vanish from the map while still living on in written memory.

📚 WHY "EMPIRE" IS A NEW KIND OF WORD

It is worth pausing on why historians save the word empire for what Sargon built, rather than just calling it a large kingdom. A kingdom mostly rules one people, in one place, under one set of customs. An empire rules many different peoples, cities, and languages at once. These peoples did not always choose to be ruled together, and often kept their own local customs, gods, and leaders, even while bowing to one distant ruler. Sargon had no telephones, no fast roads, and no trained government passed down from anyone before him, since no one had ever built one this large. Lesson 2 turns to how Sargon and those who ruled after him tried to solve this problem, and to what later went wrong.

Sargon rose from a humble, disputed birth to found the world's first known empire, ruling Mesopotamia and beyond from his capital at Akkad around 2334 to 2279 BC. His conquests united dozens of independent Sumerian city-states under a single ruler for the first time in recorded history, setting a pattern that later empires across the ancient world would imitate.

💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

Sargon's empire did not grow out of a plan anyone could have predicted from a basket on a river. It grew because a string of small, uncertain events broke his way, and because he then moved fast once in power. Ruling that many cities and peoples at once, though, is a very different problem from winning them. Lesson 2 turns to what Sargon's empire actually had to solve to hold together at all.
