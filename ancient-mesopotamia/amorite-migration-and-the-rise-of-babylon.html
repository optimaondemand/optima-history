📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
How does a minor town on the Euphrates become the capital of an age?

Last week's lesson ended with the Akkadian Empire in ruins, and this week opens on the power vacuum it left behind. Migrating Amorite herders slowly took over Mesopotamia's cities, one of them a small, unremarkable town called Babylon. Nothing about Babylon marked it for greatness until a king named Hammurabi finished a long, cautious reign with a fast burst of conquest, and then left behind something that would outlast his empire itself: a written code of law.

The Akkadian Empire's collapse, which closed last week's lesson, left a power vacuum across Mesopotamia. No single city held the kind of power Sargon or Naram-Sin once held. Into this shaken land, over the next centuries, came waves of a people the Mesopotamians called the Amorites (AM-uh-ryts). The Amorites were herders who moved from place to place and spoke a Semitic language. They came in from the western desert and the Levant, starting around 2000 to 1900 BC. Amorite chiefs and their followers slowly settled among the Mesopotamian cities already there, sometimes simply taking the cities over, but keeping the old language, writing system, and faith already in place.

Some Amorite groups still lived in tents even after their own kings sat on Mesopotamian thrones. Letters found in the city of Mari describe this mixed way of life in real detail: part herder, part city ruler, all at once. Within just a few generations, Amorite kings wrote in cuneiform, prayed to Mesopotamian gods, and ruled from Mesopotamian palaces like anyone born there.

📚 A MINOR CITY RISES

Among the many small city-states an Amorite family came to rule was Babylon, a fairly small town on the Euphrates. At the start of the second millennium BC, Babylon had no special claim to greatness. That changed with the reign of Hammurabi, who took Babylon's throne around 1792 BC. For much of his reign, Hammurabi ruled a modest kingdom, careful and mostly on guard, forming and breaking deals with bigger neighbors as needed. Only late in his reign did this pattern change. In a fast series of wars during his final decade of rule, Hammurabi finally joined southern and much of central Mesopotamia under Babylon's rule, beating rival cities one after another, including Larsa, Mari, and Eshnunna, each of which had once been at least as strong as Babylon itself.

This timing matters. It would be easy to picture Hammurabi as a conqueror from the very start of his reign. The actual pattern was not that simple. Contingencythe idea that history could easily have turned out differently, because it depended on small, uncertain events, not one fixed path. shows up here too, just as it did with Sargon in Week 3. If Hammurabi had died at fifty instead of ruling into his final years of war, Babylon might have stayed a middling kingdom, known today, if at all, as just one Amorite city-state among many.

📚 MORE THAN A CONQUEROR

Hammurabi's military wins are not what makes his reign matter most to history. He is best known today for something else instead: a written code of lawa single, written collection of laws meant to apply the same way across an entire kingdom, rather than being decided case by case.. This code was carved onto a huge stone steleSTEE-leea tall, upright stone slab, often carved with writing or images, set up in a public place. and set up in public. Hammurabi's Code survives largely whole today, nearly four thousand years later, covering nearly every part of daily life: family, land, work, farming, and trade. It also clearly frames Hammurabi's rule around one central promise: justice for his subjects. Lesson 2 looks closely at that promise, and at whether it held up.

💡 HELPFUL TO KNOW · A NOTE ON CHRONOLOGY
Historians place Hammurabi's reign at roughly 1792 to 1750 BC using the "Middle Chronology," one of several rival dating systems scholars use for the ancient Near East. A Babylonian record of the planet Venus, kept by ancient sky-watchers, is one key piece of evidence scholars compare against modern star charts to test rival systems. This course uses the Middle Chronology throughout, but other textbooks may use a different one.

💪 KEEP THIS IN MIND

Babylon's rise, like Sargon's before it, did not have to happen. A late-life burst of conquest turned one Amorite king's cautious kingdom into the namesake of an entire age. But Hammurabi's real claim on history is the written code that followed the wars, and whether it delivered on its own promise of justice is exactly what Lesson 2 puts to the test.
