📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
When the Comanche were gone from the Plains, the grass was still there. So were the questions of who would use it, and how.
After 1875, the Texas frontier changed quickly. Land that no Texan had dared to settle was suddenly open. You will see what changed for the Rangers, for Native Texans, and for the land itself, and what problem stood in the way of settling the dry plains.

📚 PART 1 · A TURNING POINT

A Turning Point

For over a century, the Comanche had held the high plains of Texas, and every settler’s plans had to take them into account. After 1875, they no longer did. It was one of the sharpest turns in Texas history. Land that had been held by force suddenly was not.

The change was quick for the Texas Rangers, too. The Frontier Battalion had been created to guard against raids. With the raiders gone, it found new work. Its companies hunted outlaws and helped keep order in rough new towns. In 1877, the Rangers helped bring in John Wesley Hardin, a famous gunman. In 1878, they tracked down the outlaw Sam Bass, who was killed at Round Rock. The Rangers became lawmen as much as soldiers. That shift would shape their reputation for the next hundred years.

The army’s forts moved with the frontier. Fort Richardson at Jacksboro closed in 1878, and Fort Griffin closed in 1881. Fort Elliott had opened in the Panhandle in 1875, to watch the new country beyond the old line of settlement. Where soldiers had once been needed to hold the edge of Texas, they were now needed farther out, and then hardly at all.

Not every Native Texan left. A few small peoples remained within the state. The Alabama and Coushatta lived on a reservation in East Texas, land the state had set aside for them in the 1850s. The Tigua people had lived near El Paso since the 1680s and kept their community there. They are a reminder that Native peoples did not simply disappear from Texas. Even so, the main story of these years is one of loss. A huge region, the home of the Comanche and their neighbors for generations, was now open to others.

📚 PART 2 · THE STAKED PLAIN

The Land Beyond

What was this land like? Spanish explorers called the high plateau of West Texas the Llano Estacado. The name means the “Staked Plain.” Nobody is sure why. One idea is that the explorers marked their way with stakes, because the plain was so flat and empty that a traveler could get lost. A flat sea of grass stretched for hundreds of miles with hardly a tree.

💡 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Llano Estacado (YAH-noh es-tah-KAH-doh): Spanish for the Staked Plain, the huge, flat, treeless plateau of the Texas Panhandle and South Plains.

The grass was good. Bison had grazed it for centuries, and cattle could too. But the land had one big problem: water. There were few rivers. The rain was light. Water collected in shallow lakes called playas, which dried up in drought, and in springs along the edges of canyons. A rancher could have good grass and still lose his herd for lack of water. The great bison herds were gone from Texas by the early 1880s. For a time, people gathered the bleached bones from the plains and sold them to be ground up for fertilizer.

The solution came from a machine. The windmill pumped water up from underground and filled tanks for cattle. Windmills spread across the plains in the 1880s. A rancher no longer had to stay near a river. Later, farmers drilled deep wells into the Ogallala aquifer, a huge layer of water beneath the High Plains. That water let farmers grow crops in a place that had always been too dry. But the aquifer fills very slowly, and in many places farmers pump water out faster than the rain puts it back. It is a gift that can be used up.

📚 PART 3 · RANCHERS AND RAILS

Cattle, Rails, and the Next Step

The first ranchers moved onto the plains almost at once. In 1876, a Texas rancher named Charles Goodnight drove a herd of cattle into Palo Duro Canyon, the same canyon where the army had struck the Comanche camp only two years before. The canyon had water, grass, and walls to shelter the cattle from winter storms. The next year, Goodnight joined with an Irish investor, John Adair, to start the JA Ranch. It became one of the great ranches of the Panhandle.

Goodnight was not alone. Others followed him up onto the plains with cattle, and cattle companies bought huge tracts of land. Some of the money came from as far away as Britain. They saw a chance for profit on grass that cost almost nothing.

The railroads were just behind them. The Texas and Pacific Railway reached Fort Worth in 1876 and pushed westward in the following years. Another line, the Fort Worth and Denver City, crossed the Panhandle in the 1880s, and new towns grew up beside the tracks. Amarillo was founded in 1887. A rancher on the plains could now ship cattle by rail. The old trail drives, across hundreds of miles of open country, were about to give way to the train.

Farmers came too, slowly. The first settlers on the dry plains were mostly ranchers, but the land was also good for crops if the rain came. Over the next generation, more families arrived and began the cultivation of wheat and cotton, plowing ground where grass had grown for thousands of years. The same plain that had been Comanche hunting ground now became a different kind of country.

Notice that none of this was planned from the start. The opening of the plains came from a chain of events: soldiers, bison hunters, windmills, rails, and investors. Each link depended on the others. That opened land was about to become the home of a cattle kingdom.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

When a frontier closes, something ends and something begins. Ask what each side gained and lost, and what the land itself would require of the people who came to use it.
