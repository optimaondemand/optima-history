📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Out on the frontier, the Rangers were not an army. They were neighbors who had learned to ride and shoot like the people they fought.
Texans did not trust distant forts to protect them. So they built their own defenders, men who rode hard and carried little. You will see where the Texas Rangers came from, how they fought, and what their early fights taught them. You will also see the limits of that record.

📚 PART 1 · ORIGINS

Origins

The first Texas settlers did not have an army to guard them, so in 1823, Stephen F. Austin hired ten men to “range” the countryside, that is, to ride over it and watch for danger. Austin later called for more. In November 1835, as the Texas Revolution began, the leaders meeting at the Consultation voted to create a corps of rangers. From then on, the Texas Rangers were an official part of Texas government.

They were not like a regular army. A Ranger company was a group of volunteers, led by a captain. Men signed up for a short time. Often they brought their own horse and gun. They wore no uniform. Their pay was low, and pay was often late. What they had instead was skill in riding, tracking, and shooting, and they knew the land.

When Texas became a state in 1845, the United States took charge of frontier defense. Yet Texans kept calling for Rangers. Settlers did not think the army forts could protect them, and the state government agreed. This was a choice with a price tag. The state could not always afford Ranger companies, and it asked Washington to pay for them. Sometimes Washington did, and sometimes it refused.

📚 PART 2 · TACTICS

How the Rangers Fought

The Rangers learned a lot from their enemies. The Comanche were excellent horsemen, and a rider on foot could not catch them. So Rangers fought from horseback, too. They traveled light, living off the land, and covering long distances. They learned the water holes and trails. Many Rangers also learned from Native scouts who worked with them, such as Tonkawa and Lipan Apache men, whose own peoples were enemies of the Comanche. A good scout could read signs of a camp from a day’s ride away.

Then came a new weapon. In 1844, a Ranger captain named John Coffee “Jack” Hays and about fifteen men were attacked by a much larger Comanche party near Walker’s Creek in the Hill Country. They carried a new kind of gun, the Colt five-shot revolver. Before then, a man with a single-shot rifle had to stop to reload, which was dangerous against a mounted warrior with a bow, who could shoot many arrows in that time. With revolvers, the Rangers could fire again and again from horseback. They drove the Comanche back. The fight made Hays famous, and the revolver became part of the Ranger’s kit. Samuel Walker, a Ranger who fought beside Hays, later helped Samuel Colt improve the design. One model is still called the Walker Colt.

Think about how much a small invention can matter. The Rangers did not win because they were braver than their enemies. The Comanche were extremely brave. They won partly because of a tool that changed the odds, and partly because of tactics, the use of scouts, and the discipline of working together.

📚 PART 3 · EARLY FIGHTS

Early Fights

Several fights set the pattern. In 1840, at Plum Creek near present-day Lockhart, a force of Rangers and militia caught a large Comanche party that had raided as far as the Gulf coast. They fought the Comanche as the warriors were heading home with stolen horses and goods. It was a big victory for the Texans.

In 1858, Captain John S. “Rip” Ford led Rangers and Native allies north across the Red River into the Comanche homeland, to a place called Antelope Hills. They attacked a Comanche camp. The Texans claimed a victory, and said they had killed many. But the Comanche were not defeated. Raids went on.

In December 1860, Captain Sul Ross led Rangers and soldiers against a Comanche camp at the Pease River, in North Texas. They captured a woman who turned out to be Cynthia Ann Parker. She had been taken as a child from Parker’s Fort in 1836, and she had lived with the Comanche for almost twenty-five years. She had married a Comanche leader, and she was the mother of Quanah Parker, who would become the last great Comanche war leader. She was brought back to her white relatives, but she never settled among them, and she died about ten years later. The Pease River fight is remembered by Texans as a Ranger victory, but it shows how hard it was to tell a camp of warriors from a camp of families.

What the Record Shows

Taken together, the Rangers did what Texans needed. The state had few soldiers, and the Rangers gave it a way to protect its people. A government that cannot defend its people has failed at its first job. In that sense, they met a first-order need.

But the record has two sides. The Rangers had no formal training in rules for war. The frontier was far from courts and newspapers, and it is often hard to know what happened in any one fight. Some Ranger actions were fierce and some Native people, including women and children, were killed. Native peoples remembered the Rangers as feared enemies. Both the Texans’ fear and the Comanche’s anger were real, and an honest account has to hold them together.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

The Rangers began as a few men watching the roads, and grew into a legend. As you read about them, try to separate what they actually did from the stories told later, and ask who told the stories.
