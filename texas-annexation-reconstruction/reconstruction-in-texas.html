💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
Black Codes (BLAK KOHDZ): Laws passed in Southern states in 1865 and 1866 that limited the rights of freed people.
Freedmen's Bureau (FREED-menz BYOOR-oh): A federal agency set up to help formerly enslaved people after the war.
freedpeople (FREED-pee-pul): People who had been enslaved and were now free.
Reconstruction (ree-kun-STRUK-shun): The years after the Civil War when the nation worked out how to bring the South back into the Union.
Redemption (ree-DEMP-shun): The name Democrats gave to their return to power in the South at the end of Reconstruction.
sharecropping (SHAIR-krop-ing): A farming system where a family works land owned by someone else and pays with part of the crop.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Slavery ended in 1865. The next fight was over what freedom would mean.
When the war ended, Texas had to answer a hard question. Who would hold power now, and what rights would the newly freed people have? You will see how Texas moved through Reconstruction, and why it ended the way it did.

📚 PART 1 · FREEDOM ARRIVES

Juneteenth

On June 19, 1865, Union General Gordon Granger arrived at Galveston. He announced that all enslaved people in Texas were free. Texans still celebrate that day as Juneteenth. Black Texans began marking the anniversary the very next year, with picnics, prayers, and readings, and the custom spread across the country. Freedom had been law elsewhere for some time, but the Union army could not enforce it in Texas until the war was over.

What did freedom bring? Many freedpeople left the farms where they had been enslaved. Some went to search for family members. Some went to the towns. Many wanted land and schools. They built churches and schools, and some started new communities of their own.

Many freedpeople hoped the government would give them land of their own. It did not. Most had no money to buy any, so they had to work for the people who owned the land. Black communities often built and paid for their own churches and schools, and teachers came to help from the North and from among freedpeople themselves.

The Freedmen’s Bureau and the Black Codes

The federal government set up the Freedmen's Bureau to help. In Texas, its agents opened schools, helped freedpeople make fair work agreements, and tried to protect them from violence. But there were few agents and Texas was huge. Reports from agents describe beatings and killings of freedpeople that went unpunished.

In 1866, the Texas legislature passed laws about work and about crime. One said that a person with no job could be arrested for “vagrancy,” and in some cases forced to work for a landowner. Freedpeople called such rules a trap.

Under the first plan for Reconstruction, set by President Andrew Johnson, Texas wrote a new state government in 1866. The legislature then passed Black Codes. These laws limited where freedpeople could work and what rights they had. Congress and many Northerners were angry, because they saw the Black Codes as slavery under another name.

📚 PART 2 · TWO PLANS FOR RECONSTRUCTION

Congress Takes Over

1865-1866
Presidential plan
President Johnson lets Southern whites rebuild their state governments quickly. Texas passes Black Codes.
1867-1870
Congress takes over
The army governs Texas. Black men vote. A new constitution is written. Texas is let back into the Union.
1874
Redemption
Democrats return to power. Governor Davis leaves office.
The three stages of Reconstruction in Texas.

In 1867, Congress put a stricter plan in place. The army would govern Texas until the state met new rules. General Philip Sheridan removed Governor James Throckmorton from office. Black men were allowed to vote for the first time, and many white Texans who had led the Confederacy were not.

To return to the Union, Texas had to approve the 13th, 14th, and 15th Amendments. They ended slavery, made Black Americans citizens, and protected the right of Black men to vote.

Texas wrote a new constitution in 1869. It gave Black men the vote and created a public school system. Black Texans were elected to office. George Ruby of Galveston and Matthew Gaines of Washington County served in the state senate. Edmund J. Davis, a Republican who had fought for the Union, became governor by a very narrow margin. In 1870, Texas was allowed back into the Union.

Davis tried to build a strong state government. He created a state police and a larger school system. His opponents hated both, and many said his government cost too much and held too much power.

📚 PART 3 · THE END OF RECONSTRUCTION

Redemption

White Texans who opposed Reconstruction began to win elections. Violence by groups like the Ku Klux Klan frightened many Republican voters and many Black voters. In 1872, Democrats won control of the legislature. In 1873, Richard Coke ran for governor against Davis and won by a wide margin.

Davis claimed the election was not legal. He asked President Ulysses S. Grant to send soldiers. Grant refused. In January 1874, Davis left office and Coke took his place. Democrats called their return to power Redemption. That word shows their view of it. Many freedpeople saw it as the loss of the rights they had just gained.

What If Grant Had Sent Troops?

Historians ask what might have happened if Grant had sent soldiers to support Davis in 1874. Soldiers might have kept Davis in office for a while, and that might have protected Black voters for longer. But Davis had lost the election by a wide margin, and many white Texans would have fought the army. Holding a government in place by force would have been very hard to keep up. Historians cannot be sure what would have happened.

What Changed

The new Democratic government cut state spending, lowered taxes, and ended Davis’s state police. It also weakened the public school system Davis had built. Texans soon wrote a new constitution that limited the power of government. Black Texans lost much of their political power over the following years. Many farmed as sharecropping families on land owned by others. A landowner with little cash would let a family work a plot. The family got a place to live and supplies on credit, and paid back with a large share of the cotton crop. A bad year left them in debt, and the debt could last for years. At the same time, freedpeople kept the churches, schools, and communities they had built.

Reconstruction had not followed a straight path. It could have gone another way at several points, and the choices people made decided where it ended.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Freedom did not arrive all at once. It came in steps, and some of those steps were later taken back. As you read about Reconstruction, keep asking who had power at each stage, and what they chose to do with it.
