📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
One state sent its sons to fight more than a thousand miles apart: in the woods of Virginia, and on the bayous and borders of the Trans-Mississippi.
When the war began, thousands of Texans joined the Confederate army. Some marched east to fight in Lee’s army. Others stayed to fight in the West, or to guard Texas itself. You will follow Texans on both fronts and see how the war came home.

📚 PART 1 · THE WAR IN THE EAST

The Texas Brigade

In 1861, Texans rushed to join the Confederate army. Three Texas regiments went to Virginia. There they became part of the Army of Northern Virginia, led by General Robert E. Lee. They were soon known as Hood’s Texas brigade, after John Bell Hood, an officer who had served in Texas before the war.

Lee used the Texans for hard jobs. At Gaines’s Mill, in June 1862, they led a charge that broke the Union line. At Second Manassas that summer, they fought again. At Antietam in Maryland, in September 1862, the 1st Texas lost more than 80 of every 100 men in a single day. At Gettysburg in July 1863, the Texans attacked on the second day of the battle. Hood himself was badly wounded there.

In May 1864, in a tangle of woods called the Wilderness, Lee rode forward to lead the Texans into a charge himself. They shouted, “Lee to the rear!” They refused to attack until he went back. It shows how much the soldiers trusted him.

Hood did not stay with the brigade. He was wounded again at Chickamauga in Georgia in September 1863 and lost a leg. In 1864 he took command of an entire army, the Army of Tennessee, and lost most of it in failed attacks at Atlanta, Franklin, and Nashville. The same daring that made him a fine brigade leader was a danger in command of a whole army.

These men fought bravely. They also fought for a Confederacy whose leaders had said that protecting slavery was a reason for leaving the Union. A fair account holds both facts.

📚 PART 2 · THE WAR IN THE WEST

Two Fronts

The land west of the Mississippi River was called the Trans-Mississippi theater. It was far from Richmond and Washington, and it often had to fight on its own. Texas troops did much of that fighting.

The East
Virginia, Maryland, Pennsylvania. Hood’s Texas Brigade in Lee’s army.
The West of the Mississippi
Louisiana, Arkansas, and Texas itself. The Trans-Mississippi theater.

Texans also marched far to the west. In 1862, General Henry Sibley led about 2,500 Texans into New Mexico. He hoped to win the Southwest and a road to the gold of California. The Texans won a battle at Valverde, but they were turned back at Glorieta Pass and went home in defeat.

A Texas division led by General John G. Walker became famous for long, fast marches in Louisiana and Arkansas. Soldiers called them “Walker’s Greyhounds.” Texas cavalry fought there as well.

In the spring of 1864, a Union army moved up the Red River in Louisiana. It aimed to take Shreveport, the Confederate headquarters west of the river, and to threaten Texas. At Mansfield, Louisiana, on April 8, Confederate forces, with many Texans among them, struck first and drove the Union army back.

📚 PART 3 · THE WAR COMES HOME

The War Reaches Texas

Union ships blockaded the Gulf coast. In October 1862, they took Galveston. On New Year’s Day 1863, Confederate General John Magruder struck back. His men fought from two steamboats lined with cotton bales and from the shore. They won Galveston back.

In September 1863, a Union fleet tried to land an army at Sabine Pass, on the Louisiana border. Lieutenant Dick Dowling and about 40 men held a small fort with six cannons. They drove off the fleet and captured two gunboats. It was one of the most famous Texas victories of the war.

The Cotton Road

The blockade made it hard to sell cotton overseas. So Texans found another way. Cotton was hauled in wagons to Brownsville, then across the Río Grande to the Mexican port of Matamoros. Mexico was a neutral country. Ships from other nations could dock there, take the cotton, and bring back guns and supplies.

💡 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Matamoros (mat-uh-MOR-ohs): A Mexican city on the south bank of the Río Grande.
Tejano (teh-HAH-noh): A Texan of Mexican descent.

A Tejano named Santos Benavides, from Laredo, rose to the rank of colonel. His 33rd Texas cavalry guarded this route. In 1864, his men defended Laredo against a Union raid.

The Border Again

The cotton trade made the Río Grande important once more, the same river that had caused the war with Mexico fifteen years earlier. Union ships could stop vessels at Confederate ports, but they could not easily stop neutral ships at a Mexican port. That is why the Texas border mattered so much to the Confederacy.

Enslaved Texans did much of the work that kept the war effort going. They farmed, hauled cotton, and built defenses. Some risked everything to escape across the Río Grande into Mexico, where slavery was against the law.

Back home, women ran farms and ranches while the men were away. Prices climbed, and goods like cloth and salt were hard to find. The war reached almost every Texas family.

Not Every Texan Agreed

Not all Texans supported the Confederacy. Some, especially among German settlers in the Hill Country, were Unionists. In 1862, Confederate forces attacked a group of them near the Nueces River. In the same year, in Gainesville, dozens of men accused of disloyalty were hanged. Texas was divided, and some people paid a heavy price for their beliefs.

The Union tried to close the Cotton Road. In November 1863, Union soldiers landed near the mouth of the Río Grande and took Brownsville. They held it for months, until Texas troops under Colonel John Ford retook the area in the summer of 1864.

The Last Battle

Robert E. Lee surrendered in Virginia in April 1865. More than a month later, in May, Texans fought a small battle at Palmito Ranch near Brownsville. The Confederates won. It was the last land battle of the war.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

Texans fought on two fronts, in Virginia and at home, and they paid for it in lives and in loss. A good account of the war keeps three things in view at once: the courage of the soldiers, the cause their government served, and the Texans who disagreed with it.
