💡 GLOSSARY OF SUBJECT VOCABULARY
A few words in this lesson are worth reviewing before you read. They are shown in bold with a dotted line. Hover over one to see what it means.
Confederacy (kun-FED-er-uh-see): The group of Southern states that left the Union in 1860 and 1861 and formed their own government.
convention (kun-VEN-shun): A meeting of chosen delegates who make a big decision.
delegate (DEL-uh-git): A person chosen to speak and vote for others.
oath (OHTH): A solemn promise, often to be loyal to a government.
ordinance (OR-dih-nunz): An official order or law. Here it means the convention's decision to leave the Union.
referendum (ref-uh-REN-dum): A vote by the people on one question.
secession (sih-SESH-un): The act of formally leaving a country or union.
states' rights (STAYTS RITES): The idea that the states hold powers the national government cannot take away.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
The hero of San Jacinto told Texans what a war would cost. They voted him out of office for saying so.
In early 1861, Texas leaders met in Austin to decide whether Texas should leave the Union. You will see how they decided, and why the most famous man in Texas refused to go along.

📚 PART 1 · BEFORE THE CONVENTION
⚡ QUICK CHECK-IN

The Man Texans Knew

By 1861, Sam Houston was the best-known Texan alive. He had won the Battle of San Jacinto in 1836. He had been president of the Republic of Texas, a senator for the new state, and, since 1859, its governor. When the national quarrel over slavery turned into a crisis, everyone wanted to know what Houston would do.

Houston did not want Texas to leave the Union. He was not against slavery. He held enslaved people himself, and he defended the system. His argument was about whether leaving would work. He said it would bring a war, and that Texas would lose.

A Convention Without the Governor

Lincoln was elected in November 1860, and South Carolina left the Union in December. Many Texans wanted to follow. Houston, as governor, refused to call the legislature into a special session to discuss it. Secession leaders did not wait. They held an election for delegates and called a convention. It met in Austin on January 28, 1861. The legislature later gave its approval.

📚 PART 2 · THE CONVENTION DECIDES

The Vote

On February 1, the convention voted 166 to 8 for an ordinance of secession. Ordinary voters had to agree, so a referendum was held on February 23. About three of every four voters said yes.

Against: 8 (the small gold bar)

About 3 in 4 voters said yes

What Reason Did They Give?

The convention also wrote a paper explaining why. It is called the Declaration of Causes. It said that Texas had joined the Union as a state that allowed slavery. It accused Northern states of attacking slavery and breaking their duty to Southern states.

Many leaders also spoke of states' rights. This was the legal argument: that each state had the right to make this choice. But when you read what Texas leaders wrote, the cause they named again and again was slavery. The expansion of slavery had been the national quarrel for years, and Texas answered it by leaving.

By March, Texas had joined the Confederacy, the new government formed by the Southern states.

📚 PART 3 · HOUSTON STANDS APART

Houston Warns Texas

Houston spoke out in plain words. He said that war was coming, and that it would not be short or easy. He argued that the North had more than twice as many people. It had most of the factories and most of the railroads. He warned that Northerners were slow to anger but would not quit once they began. In his view, anyone who thought the North would not fight was fooling himself.

Many secession leaders told Texans something else. They said the North was weak and would give up quickly. Houston judged that this was a dangerous way to talk. He believed a leader should look at the real strength of the other side, even when the crowd wants to cheer.

The Oath

In March, the convention told state officials to take an oath of loyalty to the Confederacy. Houston refused. On March 16, the convention called his name three times. He did not answer. The delegates declared his office empty, and Edward Clark became governor.

Houston is also said to have turned down an offer of Union troops that could have kept him in office. He did not want to bring civil war to Texas. He left quietly. He died in July 1863, while the war was still going on.

What Happened Next

The war lasted four years. It cost hundreds of thousands of lives. In the end, the South lost, and slavery ended. Houston had not been sure of every detail. But he had looked at the facts and told Texans what to expect, and in the main he was right.

⚡ TRY IT · WHOSE VIEW?
Read each statement. Does it match Houston, the secession leaders, or both?
1. Slavery was worth defending.
2. The North is strong, and it will not give up easily.

4. A war would be long and costly.
▶  Write your answer in your notebook, then click to compare. Houston lost his office by refusing the oath. Give one reason it was the right choice, and one reason it was not.
Many answers can be good. A reason for: Houston believed secession would bring a disaster, and he refused to promise loyalty to a cause he thought would hurt Texas. A reason against: if he had taken the oath, he might have kept his office and used it to slow the war. The important thing is to give a reason that fits what you now know about him. A strong answer also names the cost of the choice he made.
💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A leader can be popular for saying what a crowd wants to hear, or respected for saying what is true. Houston chose the second, and it cost him his office. When you read about big choices in history, ask not only what people decided, but how clearly they saw the facts.
