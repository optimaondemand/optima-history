disputed (dis-PYOO-tid): Argued over. Each side says it is right.
expansionist (ek-SPAN-shun-ist): A person who wants their country to grow larger by gaining more land.

scout (SKOWT): A soldier who rides ahead of an army to look over the land and find the enemy.
volunteer (vol-un-TEER): A person who joins an army by choice, usually for a short time, instead of being a full-time soldier.
📖 TODAY'S FOCUS
Two rivers. One strip of land between them. Two countries that each said it was theirs.
In 1845, Texas joined the United States, and a problem came along with it. No one agreed on where Texas ended in the southwest. You will see how that argument turned into a war, and how Texans took part in it.

📚 PART 1 · TWO RIVERS, ONE BORDER

One Country Becomes One State

For nine years, Texas was its own country, the Republic of Texas. In 1845 it joined the United States. This is called annexation. Texas became the twenty-eighth state.

Yet one question came along with it. Where did Texas end in the southwest? Texas said its boundary was the Big River (Río Grande). In 1836 the Republic’s congress had passed a law naming that river as its border. Mexico gave a different answer. Mexico had never accepted that Texas was a separate country. Mexico said the old border of its Texas province was the Nut River (Río Nueces), farther to the north.

💡 FOREIGN LANGUAGE VOCABULARY
Matamoros (mat-uh-MOR-ohs): A Mexican city on the south bank of the Río Grande.
Nueces (NWAY-ses): Spanish for "nuts." The name of the Nut River.
Río Grande (REE-oh GRAN-day): Spanish for "Big River."

Between the two rivers lay a wide strip of dry brush country. People call it the Nueces Strip. Both countries said it was theirs, so it was disputed land.

Who Really Held the Land?

Not Texas. Texans had never really governed the strip. A few Americans lived near Corpus Christi, on the coast. But Mexican ranchers and towns stood along the Río Grande, such as the city of Matamoros.

When the United States Congress voted to annex Texas, it did not settle the question. It left the exact border to be worked out with Mexico later. Mexico cut off its ties with the United States over the annexation. The border had become a problem for the whole country.

That changed Texas’s position. As a republic, Texas had faced Mexico alone. As a state, it could count on the United States Army. But it gave up something too. The choice between talking and fighting now belonged to the president and Congress in Washington, not to Texans.

📚 PART 2 · POLK SENDS THE ARMY

A President Who Wanted More

The new president was James K. Polk of Tennessee. He took office in March 1845. Polk was an expansionist. He wanted the United States to grow, and he wanted it to happen while he was in office. Many Americans felt the same way. That year a writer named John O’Sullivan gave the feeling a name, Manifest Destiny.

Polk wanted more than the Río Grande. He also wanted California. He sent a diplomat, John Slidell, to Mexico City to offer money for California and to settle the border. Mexico’s leaders refused to receive him.

Mexican leaders had their own reasons. To many Mexicans, Texas was a province that had rebelled, and its loss still hurt national pride. Accepting the Río Grande as a border would mean giving up even more land. Any Mexican president who seemed ready to deal with Slidell could be attacked as a traitor. In December 1845, President Herrera was overthrown, partly because people thought he was willing to talk. His successors were less willing.

The Army Moves South

Polk also sent soldiers. In the summer of 1845, General Zachary Taylor led an army to Texas. His camp was at Corpus Christi, on the northern edge of the disputed land. The army’s job was to protect Texas.

In March 1846, Polk gave a new order. Taylor was to march all the way to the Río Grande. He built a fort across the river from Matamoros. To Americans, this was defending Texas. To Mexico, it was an army marching into Mexico. Mexican officers told Taylor to leave. He stayed.

On April 25, 1846, Mexican soldiers attacked an American patrol north of the river. Several Americans were killed and the rest were captured. On May 11, Polk sent a message to Congress. He said Mexico “has invaded our territory and shed American blood upon the American soil.” Two days later, Congress declared war.

But whose soil was it? That was the whole question. Some Americans doubted Polk’s claim. A new congressman from Illinois, Abraham Lincoln, later asked Polk to name the exact spot where the blood had been shed.

What Might Have Been

Historians like to ask what would have happened if Taylor had stayed at the Nueces River in 1846. The clash of April 25 happened because two armies were standing face to face along the Río Grande. If Taylor had stayed farther north, that clash would not have happened in the same place at the same time. But Mexico still refused to accept Texas, and Polk still wanted California. War might have come anyway, later or from a different spark. Historians cannot be sure, but the question shows how much one order could matter.

📚 PART 3 · THE RANGERS GO TO WAR

Texans in the Army

Texans did not wait on the sidelines. Many Texas volunteers joined Taylor’s army. Among them were Texas Rangers, men who had spent years fighting on the frontier. Two of the best known were Jack Hays and Samuel Walker. Hays led a regiment of Texas horsemen. Walker had been a prisoner in Mexico before the war, and he knew the country.

The Rangers were used for what they did best. They rode fast and shot well from horseback. They worked as scouts, carried messages, and struck quickly. In September 1846, Rangers fought in the streets of Monterrey, a city in northern Mexico. Walker also worked with the inventor Samuel Colt to design a larger, stronger revolver, which was later called the Walker Colt.

Two Facts to Hold Together

Both Hays and Walker had shown courage in many fights before 1846, not in just one. That steady kind of courage is why officers trusted them with dangerous work.

But the Rangers were feared in Mexico for another reason too. Many Mexican civilians remembered them for cruelty. An honest account has to hold both facts.

Texans had joined the United States to be safer. Now the first thing their new country did was go to war over the border Texas claimed. That was one of the costs of belonging.

💪 WHAT TO CARRY FORWARD

A border is only a line until someone decides to defend it. Texas claimed the Río Grande. Mexico claimed the Nueces. Polk chose to send an army into the space between. As you read about the war, ask who chose what, and what each person might have done differently.
